## Summary {#sec-network-architectures-summary-e642}

*Architecture is infrastructure.* Choosing among MLPs, CNNs, RNNs, transformers, and recommendation models (DLRM) shapes the physical terms in the serialized form of the iron law of ML systems ($T = \DataVolume/\Bandwidth + \Operations / (\PeakRate \cdot \EfficiencyHw) + \Latency$). Examining each architecture through the four-part lens (pattern processing needs, algorithmic structure, computational mapping, and system implications) connects mathematical inductive bias to work, state, and dependencies. Execution conditions and the target machine then determine which term binds hardware performance.

The architectural families and five lighthouse models established at the chapter opening illustrate these physical bounds. ResNet-50's spatial weight reuse raises arithmetic intensity ($\ArithmeticIntensity = \Operations/\DataVolume$), favoring the compute-bound regime ($\Operations/(\PeakRate \cdot \EfficiencyHw)$) when reuse exceeds the machine's balance. Small-batch autoregressive GPT-2 generation with streamed weights has low arithmetic intensity, making memory bandwidth ($\DataVolume/\Bandwidth$) a likely limit. RNN temporal recurrences impose $\SequenceLength$ ordered state updates; per-step utilization $\EfficiencyHw$ and launch overhead $\Latency$ depend on batching and fusion. Oversized DLRM embeddings hit a capacity wall first, and placement decides which bandwidth the data term $\DataVolume/\Bandwidth$ must pay: HBM when the tables fit, a host or peer link when they do not. MobileNetV2 and KWS demonstrate how low FLOP counts do not guarantee throughput if operational shapes fail to saturate hardware functional units.

::: {.callout-takeaways title="Architecture is infrastructure"}

* **Inductive bias is the unifying concept**: Every architecture encodes structural assumptions, such as locality for CNNs, sequence for RNNs, and global context for transformers. These biases trade generality for sample efficiency and determine which problems an architecture can solve efficiently.
* **Arithmetic intensity helps identify the bottleneck**: Comparing a workload's arithmetic intensity with the target hardware's roofline balance helps determine whether compute or memory bandwidth is likely to bind.
* **Quadratic costs are permanent constraints**: Dense transformer attention computes $\mathcal{O}(S^2)$ score interactions. Naive implementations also use quadratic score storage; tiled exact attention removes that storage cost but not the dense computation.
* **Lighthouse models isolate distinct bottlenecks**: ResNet-50 (compute), GPT-2 (bandwidth), DLRM (capacity), MobileNetV2 (latency and power), KWS (power envelope). These archetypes diagnose which physical constraint dominates a given system.
* **Depth benefits from architectural support**: Skip connections and normalization improve optimization in very deep networks rather than acting as universal prerequisites beyond a fixed depth. These building blocks, illustrated here through CNNs, transfer to many deep architectures, including transformers.
* **FLOPs do not equal speed**: MobileNetV2 uses `{python} CNNLighthouseProfile.mobilenet_flops_ratio_mult_str` fewer FLOPs than ResNet-50 but may run slower on some data center GPUs when its operation shapes and arithmetic intensity use the available compute units poorly. Architecture-hardware alignment, not operation count alone, determines throughput.
* **Architecture selection is deployment selection**: Choosing a transformer over a CNN determines memory requirements, latency floors, hardware utilization, and infrastructure costs. The architecture is the system constraint.

:::

Inductive bias and systems cost are two views of the same choice. The assumptions that help a model generalize from limited samples, such as locality, recurrence, or unrestricted token interactions, also determine the shape, reuse, and lifetime of intermediate data. An algorithmic choice therefore propagates into memory capacity, bandwidth, and scheduling requirements before a framework chooses kernels.

Architecture selection signs the silicon contract (principle \ref{pri-silicon-contract}) with hardware. A CNN commits to spatial locality and weight reuse; dense attention commits to $\mathcal{O}(\SequenceLength^2)$ score computation; and an RNN commits to serial time-step dependencies. These topological choices constrain operations $\Operations$, opportunities for reducing transferred data $\DataVolume$, and dependency order. Realized memory footprint, bandwidth demand, and parallel efficiency also depend on the implementation. Tiled exact attention removes intermediate score storage without changing the quadratic arithmetic floor, while pipelining consecutive stages across execution units overlaps work without removing the serial time-step path. Systems engineers can therefore anticipate likely bottleneck shifts before implementation by matching model inductive bias and data geometry to silicon physics.
