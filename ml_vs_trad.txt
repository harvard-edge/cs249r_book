
## ML vs. Traditional Software {#sec-introduction-ml-vs-traditional-software-e19a}

Under the D·A·M taxonomy, machine learning systems integrate data that defines behavior, algorithms that extract statistical patterns, and machines that execute training and inference.[^fn-inference-serving-deployment] Understanding how engineering these systems diverges from traditional software begins with how they fail in production.

[^fn-inference-serving-deployment]: **Inference**\index{Inference!deployment}: From Latin *inferre* ("to bring in" or "to conclude"). Applying a trained model occurs during evaluation as well as deployment, and may process an offline collection or answer an individual request. Interactive serving often imposes a response-time limit, whereas offline inference may prioritize total processing rate. The workload determines the relevant balance of latency, throughput, and resource cost (see @sec-model-serving).

Crashes, unhandled exceptions, and failed health checks make some software defects directly observable. Conventional software can also produce silent numerical errors or logic bugs. Machine learning adds another route to **silent degradation**\index{Silent degradation}: learned behavior can lose predictive quality as the operating population changes. The runtime executes instructions without error, memory allocations succeed, and inference servers return predictions well within latency service level agreements (SLAs), yet the statistical validity of the output steadily deteriorates.

In an automotive driver-assistance system, for example, conventional control software exposes hardware or communication faults through diagnostic trouble codes and hardware watchdog timers. An ML-based perception pipeline introduces a failure mode invisible to these watchdogs. Its pedestrian detection recall might decline from 95 percent to 85 percent over several months as seasonal shifts introduce low-angle winter sunlight, heavy precipitation, or bulky clothing underrepresented in the training distribution. The embedded accelerator continues dispatching vision kernels at full framerate without raising a single hardware fault, yet the vehicle operates with an elevated safety risk that eludes standard operating-system telemetry.

The magnitude of this degradation is severe in safety-critical contexts. A perception model operating at 10 Hz evaluates 36,000 frames per hour. If pedestrian recall falls from 95 to 85 percent, the miss rate triples, from 1 in 20 pedestrian frames to 3 in 20. Temporal filtering and sensor fusion absorb some misses, but the planner now receives three times as many gaps in exactly the cases where detection was already marginal.

This failure mode engages all three D·A·M axes simultaneously. On the data axis, input distributions drift as environmental conditions, user behaviors, or physical sensor calibrations shift away from the training baseline [@gama2014; @quinonero2009dataset]. On the algorithm axis, the model applies fixed parameter weights to inputs that violate original distribution assumptions, generating degraded probabilities without triggering numerical exceptions like NaNs or infinities. On the machine axis, accelerators execute kernel dispatches and stream tensors through memory hierarchies at maximum throughput, scaling degraded outputs across millions of inferences without a single hardware interrupt.

Outcome measurements establish whether predictive quality has fallen; distribution-divergence signals can prompt investigation before those outcomes arrive. When both are observed, model degradation can be approximated locally as a function of distribution shift. Let $\InitialAccuracy$ denote the baseline accuracy at deployment, $\StatisticalDivergence$ represent the statistical divergence between current production distribution $\CurrentDistribution$ and training distribution $\TrainingDistribution$, and $\FittedSensitivity$ denote the locally fitted sensitivity to that shift measure, so that $\AccuracyAtTime$ is the accuracy after the model has been deployed for time $t$. This relationship, formalized in @eq-degradation and illustrated in the margin, is the **degradation equation**\index{Degradation equation!definition}:
$$ \AccuracyAtTime \approx \InitialAccuracy - \FittedSensitivity \cdot \StatisticalDivergence $$ {#eq-degradation}

This first-order linearization captures local trends when labeled ground truth validates the empirical relationship; Part IV adopts the same equation as the statistical drift diagnostic (principle \ref{pri-statistical-drift}). **Data drift**\index{Data drift!definition} occurs when $\CurrentDistribution$ differs from $\TrainingDistribution$. Such a shift can reduce inference quality despite byte-identical model binaries and execution environments, but the shift alone does not establish a quality loss. The approximation breaks down under severe distribution collapse, and the divergence measure $\mathcal{\DatasetSize}(\cdot \lVert \cdot)$ is left deliberately general here; @sec-ml-operations-feature-distribution-monitoring-b8a0 develops concrete choices.

The equation separates baseline quality from sensitivity to shift. Holding $\FittedSensitivity$ fixed, raising $\InitialAccuracy$ shifts the intercept upward. Better data, objectives, and architectures can change both quantities, so an improved baseline does not by itself establish robustness to drift. For the driving example, training on representative winter images may reduce sensitivity to seasonal conditions; evaluating pedestrian recall under those conditions establishes whether it does. Monitoring $\mathcal{\DatasetSize}(\CurrentDistribution \lVert \TrainingDistribution)$ provides an early warning that prompts engineers to seek outcome evidence. Because ground-truth labels rarely arrive immediately, engineers watch observable proxies such as input feature distributions, prediction frequencies, and confidence scores, alert when divergence crosses an operational threshold $\ResponseThreshold$, and assess whether retraining\index{Retraining} addresses the loss confirmed by delayed labels. *Knowing when to reevaluate is as important as knowing how to train.*

Silent degradation does not originate solely from external environmental shifts. It frequently arises from internal architectural defects known as **training-serving skew**\index{Training-serving skew!definition}. In production ML systems, feature engineering is typically implemented across two distinct software stacks: an offline batch pipeline (such as Spark or SQL) that generates training datasets from historical data lakes, and an online microservice (written in C++, Go, or Rust) that computes features under millisecond latency constraints during live inference. If online feature extraction diverges even subtly from the offline implementation, for example by resizing images with a different interpolation method or applying different missing-value defaults, the model receives feature representations that violate its training assumptions. The model binary executes cleanly, memory buffers remain valid, and serving latency remains nominal, but prediction accuracy collapses at deployment. Training-serving skew is fundamentally a software pipeline parity defect that manifests as an algorithmic failure.

These failure modes reshape the entire engineering lifecycle. Host-health signals such as CPU utilization, memory residency, network I/O, and HTTP status codes remain necessary, but do not measure whether predictions are useful. Machine learning operations must track these infrastructure metrics alongside data quality, feature distributions, and prediction accuracy. Because an ML system can fail silently while infrastructure metrics appear nominal, system design must integrate continuous evaluation from data ingestion through inference serving.

Silent degradation addresses the first half of the ML systems mandate: ensuring that learned behavior remains statistically reliable in a non-stationary world. The second half of the mandate governs physical execution: ensuring that the machine can evaluate and update that behavior within strict latency, memory, energy, and monetary budgets. As computational scale continues to dictate ML capability, systems engineering requires quantitative reasoning about the memory bandwidth, arithmetic throughput, and communication overheads that physically govern that scale.
