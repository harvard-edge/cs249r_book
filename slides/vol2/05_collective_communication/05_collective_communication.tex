\begin{frame}{Each way of splitting a model creates its own traffic}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{traffic_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\footnotesize\ttfamily\begin{tabular}{@{}l@{}}
  \textcolor{midgray}{\# torch.distributed}\enspace all\_reduce\enspace all\_gather\_into\_tensor\\
  reduce\_scatter\_tensor\enspace send\enspace recv\enspace all\_to\_all\_single
  \end{tabular}}
  \mlsyscredit{Collective Communication, table 1 (\S5.3), abridged. Function names: PyTorch v2.4.0, torch/distributed/distributed\_c10d.py.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Previewing the parallelism strategies of Lecture 6, five ways of splitting a model map onto the \texttt{torch.distributed} primitives listed below. Data parallelism replicates weights and synchronizes full gradients with AllReduce, making it bandwidth-limited. Sharded data parallelism, such as PyTorch's Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP) and Stage 3 of the Zero Redundancy Optimizer (ZeRO-3), shards weights, gradients, and optimizer state across ranks to save memory, splitting synchronization into many per-layer AllGather and ReduceScatter calls. Tensor parallelism splits weight matrices inside a layer and must sum partial outputs with AllReduce before the next layer can run, placing that collective on the critical path and typically keeping it on fast intra-node links such as NVLink. Pipeline parallelism hands small activations between layer stages with point-to-point Send and Recv, where latency dominates. Expert or embedding parallelism routes Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) tokens or recommender lookups with AllToAll, limited by routing skew and fabric bisection bandwidth, the capacity across the narrowest cut dividing the network in half (Lecture 3).

\begin{frame}{A collective names what each rank holds afterward}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{contracts_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{Collective Communication, \S5.4 and figure 2. Ranks R0 to R3; color marks the source rank; numbers in AllToAll cells are chunk indices.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Once a split selects a primitive, that collective specifies a mathematical outcome across participating ranks R0 to R3, independent of the wire schedule used to achieve it. In AllReduce, every rank contributes a full tensor and receives the identical global sum. In AllGather, each rank starts with one shard and finishes holding all shards. In ReduceScatter, every rank contributes a full tensor and rank $i$ retains only the $i$-th summed shard; notice that running ReduceScatter followed by AllGather equals AllReduce. Finally, AllToAll performs a distributed transpose in which rank $i$ sends chunk $j$ to rank $j$. Broadcast, where one rank sends to all, and Reduce, where all ranks aggregate data to one root, round out the core six primitives.

    \notecue{Watch for}{A collective defines a data-dependency contract rather than a strict barrier. Ranks can enter asynchronously, and a late rank stalls only the ranks waiting on its chunks.}

    \notecue{Next}{Next, an optional slide shows why sharded data parallelism decomposes AllReduce into separate AllGather and ReduceScatter calls.}
  }
\end{frame}

\section{Pricing}

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Because AllReduce decomposes into ReduceScatter followed by AllGather, a logical ring splits an $M$-byte tensor into $N$ chunks of size $M/N$ and runs $N-1$ ReduceScatter rounds clockwise to finish one chunk per rank, then $N-1$ AllGather rounds to circulate those sums, giving $2(N-1)$ rounds in all (traced concretely in Example 5.1 ahead). Each round pays startup latency $\alpha$ plus one chunk transfer at rate $\beta$, giving Lecture 1's $T_{\text{comm}}$ for one gradient synchronization. The latency term $2(N-1)\alpha$ grows linearly with $N$, whereas the bandwidth term $2\frac{N-1}{N}\frac{M}{\beta}$ approaches $2M/\beta$ regardless of cluster size. Every optimization ahead targets round count, chunk size, or the link rate $\beta$ used per round.

  \notecue{Next}{When does a ring round spend more time starting the transfer than sending its chunk?}
}
\mlsysfocus{Ring AllReduce time}{%
$T_{\text{ring}} \;=\; \underbrace{2(N-1)\,\alpha}_{\text{one startup per round}} \;+\; \underbrace{2\,\frac{N-1}{N}\,\frac{M}{\beta}}_{\text{one chunk per round}}$\\[0.4cm]
{\normalsize $N$ ranks\quad $M$ bytes per rank\quad $\alpha$ startup\quad $\beta$ bandwidth}}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    To see which term of the ring equation dominates, compare each round's chunk against the link's critical chunk size $n^* = \alpha\beta$, where startup and transfer cost the same (Table 4 midpoints across five interconnects). Below $n^*$, startup latency dominates; above $n^*$, bandwidth dominates. InfiniBand NDR crosses at $1.5\text{ \textmu s} \times 50\text{ GB/s} = 75\text{ KB}$, whereas high-bandwidth NVLink stays latency-bound up to about 0.7 MB. Applying Napkin Math 5.1 to a 25 MB gradient bucket shows how group size shifts the regime. In a 64-GPU ring, each chunk is $25\text{ MB} / 64 = 390.6\text{ KB}$, which sits above 75 KB and is bandwidth-bound. In a 1,024-GPU ring, each chunk shrinks to 24.4 KB, falling below 75 KB into the latency-bound regime where faster links barely help.

    On large, bandwidth-bound payloads, topology sets the byte ceiling, explaining why Uber's parameter server stalled. On the 64-worker, 1 GB scoreboard over 50 GB/s ports, a single parameter server must receive 64 GB and return 64 GB through one port. That takes $64\text{ GB} / 50\text{ GB/s} = 1{,}280\text{ ms}$ each way (derived), setting a 1,280 ms one-direction bottleneck even if receive and send overlap on a full-duplex link. That is $32.5\times$ slower (near $N/2 = 32$) than the ring's $2\frac{N-1}{N}M/\beta = 39.4\text{ ms}$ byte term, alongside a simple tree at 240 ms (derived in Section 3). Combining $N$ tensors and distributing the result requires moving at least $2(N-1)M$ bytes across all ranks, so at least one rank must send $2\frac{N-1}{N}M$ bytes. The ring meets this full-duplex lower bound, whereas the star topology funnels all traffic through one port.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Sharding parameter servers reduces port concentration only by adding extra server ports, whereas a peer-to-peer ring uses every worker's port automatically.}

\begin{frame}{Compression pays when it removes more time than it adds}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=3.2cm,keepaspectratio]{payback.pdf}\\[0.3cm]
  {\normalsize Pays only if $t_{\text{codec}} < T_{\text{comm}}\,(1 - 1/r)$, with compression ratio $r$.}\\[0.15cm]
  {\small INT8: up to 4$\times$ vs.\ FP32.\quad Top-1\% values + indices: up to 50$\times$.\quad 1-bit: up to 32$\times$.}\\[0.15cm]
  {\footnotesize\ttfamily ddp\_model.register\_comm\_hook(process\_group, fp16\_compress\_hook)}
  \mlsyscredit{Collective Communication, Napkin Math 5.7 (assumed 8$\times$ effective, 2 ms codec) and table 12. Code: PyTorch v2.4.0, ddp\_comm\_hooks/default\_hooks.py docstring.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Because compression shrinks only the bandwidth term, it helps large payloads well above $\alpha\beta$ and never 4 KB control packets. It pays off only when compression and decompression overhead, called codec time, satisfies $t_{\text{codec}} < T_{\text{comm}}(1 - 1/r)$. In Napkin Math 5.7, compressing the 40 ms flat ring by an effective $8\times$ with a 2 ms codec cuts time to $5 + 2 = 7$ ms, which is $5.7\times$ faster. On a 1 ms NVLink collective, that same 2 ms codec makes the step slower. Headline ratios of $4\times$ for INT8 integer quantization, $50\times$ for keeping Top-1\% values plus indices, and $32\times$ for 1-bit sign encoding are measured against single-precision FP32 and halve against BF16. PyTorch's Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) \texttt{fp16\_compress\_hook} provides a $2\times$ FP32-to-FP16 half-precision cast.

    \notecue{Next}{Even when compression passes that per-step codec test, can slower convergence still turn a speedup into a slowdown?}
  }
\end{frame}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    To keep aggressive compression from adding extra steps or stalling learning, error feedback carries dropped values forward instead of discarding them. Example 5.2 tests an integer-rounding compressor in which any gradient below 0.5 rounds to zero. Across five steps whose values ($0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.4, 0.3$) sum to 1.6, naive rounding sends 0 every step and prevents learning. Error feedback retains the dropped residual $e = +0.4$ locally at step 1, so adding $e$ pushes $g + e$ past 0.5 at steps 2 and 5, firing transmitted green pulses of 1. For a constant 0.3 gradient, roughly three 1s fire every ten steps. At every step, the transmitted total plus the stored residual conserves the true sum, giving $2 + (-0.4) = 1.6$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Error feedback is not automatic inside generic quantization hooks. The runtime must allocate memory to store and accumulate residuals per parameter.}

\begin{frame}{Overlap hides each reduction under the next layer's backward}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.35cm,keepaspectratio]{overlap_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Faster buckets: 9 buckets/layer $\times$ 0.85 ms $=$ 7.6 ms hides under 15 ms backward; exposed time is what remains.}
  \mlsyscredit{Collective Communication, Napkin Math 5.9 and \S5.10.4 (interactive Explore figure: Communication-Compute Overlap). 487.6 ms derived from 9 buckets/layer $\times$ 0.85 ms.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    Once payload size is set, overlap hides transfers behind ongoing backward compute, which flows from output to input so ready layers can communicate while earlier layers compute. Napkin Math 5.9 models a 7B model on 64 GPUs with 32 layers of 15 ms backward compute each; 9 buckets of 100 MB at 3 ms per bucket take 27 ms per layer. Sequential execution adds 480 ms compute to $32 \times 27\text{ ms}$ communication for 1,344 ms. Launching each reduction immediately leaves a 12 ms backlog per layer ($27 > 15$ ms) plus the final reduction, totaling $480 + 27 + 31 \times 12 = 879$ ms (34.6\% faster). A hierarchical schedule speeds each 100 MB bucket from 3 ms to 0.85 ms, or 7.6 ms per layer under 15 ms compute. Only the final 7.6 ms stays exposed, giving 487.6 ms total (derived) and maximizing $T_{\text{overlap}}$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Even with ideal overlap, at least the final bucket and its fixed launch overhead remain exposed after backward finishes.}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.15cm}
    \item Choose the narrowest contract the next computation consumes.
    \item Each round pays $\alpha$ on a chunk of $M/N$; the ring sends the minimum bytes in $2(N-1)$ rounds.
    \item Trees cut rounds; hierarchy shrinks the slow tier's payload to $M/G$ when each GPU has its own port.
    \item An AllToAll finishes when its hottest receiver or its narrowest cut finishes.
    \item Compression and overlap act on what remains; judge them by step time and time to quality.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    Five takeaways answer the driving question of how long $N$ accelerators take to agree on a gradient and which algorithm to choose. Pick the narrowest collective contract, price $2(N-1)$ ring rounds on $M/N$ chunks, cut rounds with trees and each port's slow-tier payload to $M/G$ with hierarchy (where $G$ is the number of GPUs per node, assuming one port per GPU), bound AllToAll by its hottest receiver or narrowest cut, and judge compression and overlap by wall-clock time to matched quality.
