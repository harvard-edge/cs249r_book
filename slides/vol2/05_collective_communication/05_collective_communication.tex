\begin{frame}{A collective names what each rank holds afterward}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{contracts_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{Collective Communication, \S5.4 and figure 2. Ranks R0 to R3; color marks the source rank; numbers in AllToAll cells are chunk indices.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Every collective specifies a mathematical outcome across participating ranks (R0 to R3) independently of the wire schedule used to achieve it. First, step 1 shows AllReduce: every rank contributes a full tensor and receives the identical global sum. Second, step 2 shows AllGather: each rank starts with one shard and finishes holding all shards. Third, step 3 shows ReduceScatter: every rank contributes a full tensor and rank $i$ retains only the $i$-th summed shard; notice that running ReduceScatter followed by AllGather equals AllReduce. Fourth, step 4 shows AllToAll: a distributed transpose where rank $i$ sends chunk $j$ to rank $j$ (Broadcast and Reduce round out the core six).

    \notecue{Watch for}{A collective is a data-dependency contract, not a strict barrier: ranks can enter asynchronously, and a late rank stalls only those ranks waiting on its chunks.}

\section{Pricing}

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Read the ring AllReduce cost equation term by term, representing Lecture 1's $T_{\text{comm}}$ for one gradient synchronization. Across $N$ ranks reducing an $M$-byte tensor, the ring executes $2(N-1)$ rounds, paying startup $\alpha$ per round and transferring one $M/N$-byte chunk at rate $\beta$. The latency term $2(N-1)\alpha$ scales linearly with $N$, whereas the bandwidth term $2\frac{N-1}{N}\frac{M}{\beta}$ saturates near $2M/\beta$ regardless of cluster size. Every optimization in today's lecture targets round count, chunk size, or the link rate $\beta$ used per round.

  \notecue{Next}{The next slide identifies the critical chunk size $n^* = \alpha\beta$ across five physical interconnects.}
}
\mlsysfocus{Ring AllReduce time}{%
$T_{\text{ring}} \;=\; \underbrace{2(N-1)\,\alpha}_{\text{one startup per round}} \;+\; \underbrace{2\,\frac{N-1}{N}\,\frac{M}{\beta}}_{\text{one chunk per round}}$\\[0.4cm]
{\normalsize $N$ ranks\quad $M$ bytes per rank\quad $\alpha$ startup\quad $\beta$ bandwidth}}

\begin{frame}{Ring AllReduce takes six steps, one chunk per GPU each}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{ring_allreduce_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{Collective Communication, Example 5.1 and table 5 (interactive Explore figure: Ring AllReduce). Chunks a to d are the four elements.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3.5 min}{Core}%
    Trace Example 5.1 across four GPUs reducing four chunks (a--d) whose true global sum is $[10, 26, 18, 34]$. First, step 1 initializes each GPU's local vector. Second, step 2 executes ReduceScatter rounds 1 and 2: every GPU sends one chunk clockwise and adds incoming values (follow amber chunk a growing $1 \to 3 \to 6$). Third, step 3 completes ReduceScatter round 3 ($N-1 = 3$ rounds, $0.75M$ sent per GPU): each GPU now owns one finished green sum (GPU 0 holds $b=26$, GPU 1 holds $c=18$, GPU 2 holds $d=34$, GPU 3 holds $a=10$). Fourth, step 4 runs $N-1 = 3$ AllGather rounds circulating completed chunks with no arithmetic until every GPU holds $[10, 26, 18, 34]$. In $2(N-1) = 6$ steps, every GPU stays busy sending and receiving, sending $1.5M$ bytes total, though any single half-speed GPU paces the entire ring.

\begin{frame}{Overlap hides each reduction under the next layer's backward}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.35cm,keepaspectratio]{overlap_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Faster buckets: 9 buckets/layer $\times$ 0.85 ms $=$ 7.6 ms hides under 15 ms backward; exposed time is what remains.}
  \mlsyscredit{Collective Communication, Napkin Math 5.9 and \S5.10.4 (interactive Explore figure: Communication-Compute Overlap). 487.6 ms derived from 9 buckets/layer $\times$ 0.85 ms.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    Because backpropagation flows from output layer to input layer, each layer's gradients become ready while earlier layers are still computing (Napkin Math 5.9, 32 layers at 15 ms backward each). First, step 1 runs sequentially: 480 ms compute plus $32 \times 27\text{ ms}$ communication equals 1,344 ms. Second, step 2 launches each 27 ms reduction immediately: because $27 > 15$ ms, a 12 ms backlog accumulates per layer and the last reduction has no compute left to hide behind, totaling $480 + 27 + 31 \times 12 = 879$ ms (34.6\% faster). Third, step 3 switches to the hierarchical schedule: 9 buckets of 100 MB at 0.85 ms (7.6 ms per layer) fit comfortably under 15 ms backward, leaving only the final layer's 7.6 ms exposed for 487.6 ms total (derived) and maximizing the Fleet Law's $T_{\text{overlap}}$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Even with ideal overlap, at least the final bucket's communication remains exposed after backward finishes.}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.15cm}
    \item Choose the narrowest contract the next computation consumes.
    \item Each round pays $\alpha$ on a chunk of $M/N$; the ring sends the minimum bytes in $2(N-1)$ rounds.
    \item Trees cut rounds; hierarchy shrinks the slow tier's payload to $M/G$ when each GPU has its own port.
    \item An AllToAll finishes when its hottest receiver or its narrowest cut finishes.
    \item Compression and overlap act on what remains; judge them by step time and time to quality.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    These five takeaways span today's entire arc: picking the narrowest collective contract, pricing $2(N-1)$ ring rounds on $M/N$ chunks, cutting rounds with trees and slow-tier bytes with hierarchy, bounding AllToAll by its hottest receiver or narrowest cut, and judging compression and overlap by wall-clock time to matched quality.
