\begin{frame}{Synchronous training releases its flows together}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.9\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{inversion_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\footnotesize\uncover<3->{ML traffic inversion. \textbf{BSP}: compute, exchange, wait at a barrier. \textbf{p99}: the time 99\% of transfers beat.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Network Fabrics, \S3.2 and table 1 (Valiant 1990). Flow timings illustrative.}
  \note{[2.5 min] [CORE] Step 1: a web service. Many short flows ("mice"), each on its own schedule; their peaks rarely coincide, so a network can share links among them, which is statistical multiplexing. Step 2: synchronous training. A few long flows ("elephants"), released at the same moment when the backward pass finishes, then again next step. Step 3: the barrier. In bulk synchronous parallel execution every worker computes, exchanges, and waits until the last required transfer arrives, so the slowest flow (red) sets the step. The tail of the completion-time distribution, often reported as its 99th percentile, p99, decides when the step ends; at a barrier the tail is not an outlier. Three more inversions follow from this: the measure becomes collective completion time; a lost packet delays everyone; and oversubscription, provisioning less uplink than downlink, fails when all workers send at once. Fleet Law terms: transfer time is $T_{\text{comm}}$, residual waiting at the barrier is $T_{\text{sync}}$. LINK: Lecture 1's synchronous coupling, now on the wire. WARN: the barrier waits for the last participant; the p99 only summarizes the distribution.}
\end{frame}

  \note{[2.5 min] [CORE] Step 1: eight large gradient flows across four equal-cost paths. An even split puts two on each path; each path drains in 2 units, where 1 unit is one flow alone on a path. WARN: this is not a capacity shortage; the four paths together have room. Fleet Law term: $T_{\text{comm}}$ doubles. \begin{frame}{RDMA takes the kernel and host copies off the data path}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.1cm,keepaspectratio]{rdma_path_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Network Fabrics, \S3.4.1, figures 5 and 6, redrawn. Latencies: Definition 3.3.}
  \note{[2 min] [CORE] Step 1: the ordinary path. The training process calls the socket interface; the kernel copies the payload into its buffers and runs TCP in software on the CPU, and the driver copies again toward the NIC. Every byte crosses host memory twice, costing CPU cycles and memory bandwidth the data loader also needs. A kernel TCP message costs tens of microseconds end to end. Step 2: remote direct memory access (RDMA). The collective library, NCCL here, posts work through the verbs interface from user space; the kernel is off the data path, and the NIC runs the reliable transport in hardware. With GPUDirect RDMA the NIC reads GPU memory directly over PCIe, removing about $2n$ bytes of host-memory traffic for an $n$-byte payload. Tuned RDMA reaches low single-digit microseconds. Fleet Law term: RDMA shrinks the fixed startup cost $\alpha$ inside $T_{\text{comm}}$, paid by every message. WARN: RDMA is an access and offload model; it does not by itself say how lost packets are recovered. That is the next slide.}
\end{frame}

  \note{[2.5 min] [CORE] Both RDMA stacks must keep switch buffers from overflowing under synchronized bursts. Left: InfiniBand uses credits. The resent window is line rate times detection delay: 50 GB/s times a 5 microsecond round trip is 250 KB, and the barrier waits for it. ENGAGE: is losslessness a property of RDMA? \begin{frame}{A full queue pauses its sender, and the pause spreads}

  \mlsyssteps[width=0.9\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{pfc_spread_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.2cm}
    \item Synchronous training waits for its slowest transfer, so completion time and its tail measure a fabric.
    \item $T(n) = \alpha + n/\beta$: below $n^* = \alpha\beta$ (75 KB on NDR) startup sets the time; above it, bandwidth.
    \item Bisection bandwidth caps cut-crossing traffic; wiring such as rails should follow the parallelism plan.
    \item Lossless flow control can spread pauses and deadlock; congestion control and routing act first.
    \item Fabric faults usually slow a job silently; compare with a baseline and diagnose top down.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{[1 min] [CORE] One line per segment. Ask students to star the one they would have gotten wrong before today.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
