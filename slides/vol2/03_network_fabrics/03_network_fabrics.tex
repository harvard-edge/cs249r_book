\begin{frame}{Synchronous training releases its flows together}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.9\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{inversion_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\footnotesize\uncover<3->{ML traffic inversion. \textbf{BSP}: compute, exchange, wait at a barrier. \textbf{p99}: the time 99\% of transfers beat.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Network Fabrics, \S3.2 and table 1 (Valiant 1990). Flow timings illustrative.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Why does cross-node training behave so differently from ordinary cloud traffic? Synchronous training inverts traditional data-center assumptions (\S3.2). Conventional web fabrics carry many short, independent flows whose peaks average out through statistical multiplexing (sharing links across unsynchronized senders). By contrast, synchronous training workers finish backward computation together and release large, long-lived elephant flows at once. This lockstep follows the bulk synchronous parallel (BSP) execution model proposed by Valiant (1990) as a bridging model between parallel hardware and software, echoing Lecture 1's synchronous tight coupling. Because every worker waits at the step barrier for the slowest flow, tail completion time (p99 and worst case) sets step time, and oversubscription (provisioning less uplink capacity at a switch tier than the downlink capacity it serves) breaks down.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Although engineers monitor p99 flow latency closely, a synchronous barrier waits for the very last rank rather than the 99th percentile.}

    \notecue{Next}{Predict what equal-cost multipath (ECMP) hash routing does to eight simultaneous elephant flows across four paths.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RDMA takes the kernel and host copies off the data path}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.1cm,keepaspectratio]{rdma_path_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Network Fabrics, \S3.4.1, figures 5 and 6, redrawn (interactive Explore figure: GPUDirect RDMA Data Path). Latencies: Definition 3.3.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    At Level 2 (transport), even a fast wire stalls if host software copies every byte. Three data paths show how RDMA removes that charge. Traditional Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) sockets bounce every payload through host DRAM twice (two host copies, moving $2n$ bytes for an $n$-byte message, user to kernel and kernel to NIC) while host CPUs run the transport stack. Using RDMA verbs (the user-space programming interface for posting transfers directly to the NIC) bypasses the operating system (OS) kernel and offloads reliable transport to NIC hardware. Recall from Lecture 2's node diagram that GPUDirect RDMA completes the shortcut, letting the NIC read and write GPU HBM directly across PCIe (zero host copies, or $0n$ bytes). That direct path cuts startup latency to single-digit microseconds and shrinks the fixed startup cost $\alpha$ inside $T_{\text{comm}}$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{RDMA defines direct memory access and NIC offload; how the network handles dropped packets is a separate transport decision shown next.}

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide contrasts how InfiniBand and RoCE enforce lossless delivery beneath RDMA.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{InfiniBand prevents drops with credits; RoCE adds pauses}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{ib_roce_step}{2}\\[0.15cm]
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.3\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=1.9cm,keepaspectratio]{infiniband-switch-cables.jpg}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.66\textwidth}
      \small The reliable-delivery contract: completion with a bounded recovery tail.\\[0.1cm]
      \uncover<2->{\footnotesize A drop on a Go-Back-N NIC at 50 GB/s resends about 250 KB.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: InfiniBand switch and cables, older generation (ChrisDag, CC BY 2.0, Wikimedia Commons). Network Fabrics, \S3.4.2, \S3.4.3, table 4.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Once RDMA moves transport onto the NIC, packet drops become costly. Traditional RDMA NICs use Go-Back-N recovery, where the receiver discards out-of-order arrivals and the sender resends from the lost packet onward. At 50 GB/s across a 5 \textmu s round-trip, one dropped packet forces roughly 250 KB of retransmission and stretches the collective tail. The two dominant fabrics suppress drops differently. InfiniBand builds credit-based link flow control into the link layer, where receivers advertise free buffer credits so senders transmit only when space exists. Because Ethernet was built to drop packets on overflow, RoCE adds Priority Flow Control (PFC) PAUSE frames and Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN) marks (queue-buildup signals telling senders to slow down) to keep queues lossless. Losslessness is a deployment choice for bounded tail recovery rather than a law of RDMA; newer PFC-free designs instead use selective endpoint retransmission, resending only the missing packets at the endpoints.

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide introduces Hockney's $\alpha$-$\beta$ model to price any transfer by message size.}
  }
\end{frame}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    To separate startup cost from wire speed, Hockney's 1994 $\alpha$-$\beta$ model prices an $n$-byte transfer as $T(n) = \alpha + n/\beta$, adding startup latency $\alpha$ to serialization time $n/\beta$ (Napkin Math 3.1). Compare modeled NDR InfiniBand ($\alpha = 1.5$ \textmu s, $\beta = 50$ GB/s) against 100 Gb/s RoCE ($\alpha = 5$ \textmu s, $\beta = 12.5$ GB/s). For a 4 KB control packet, NDR takes 1.58 \textmu s vs.\ 5.32 \textmu s ($3.4\times$), because $\alpha$ dominates. For one worker's 350 MB gradient shard (roughly 350 GB divided across 1,024 GPUs in the running case), NDR takes 7.0 ms vs.\ 28.0 ms ($4.0\times$), because $\beta$ dominates. Raising RoCE to 400G still leaves 4 KB at 5.08 \textmu s while matching NDR at 7.0 ms on 350 MB. Setting startup equal to serialization ($\alpha = n/\beta$) gives the NDR crossover $n^* = \alpha\beta = 75$ KB, so a 10 KB pipeline control message depends on $\alpha$.

\begin{frame}{Rails give each GPU rank its own switch network}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.9\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{rails_step}{3}

    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%

\begin{frame}{A full queue pauses its sender, and the pause spreads}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.9\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{pfc_spread_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small PFC (priority flow control): a per-class PAUSE frame to the upstream port.}
  \mlsyscredit{Network Fabrics, Definition 3.8 and \S3.6.1 (Guo et al. 2016; Gangidi et al. 2024).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Follow that filled incast buffer into Priority Flow Control (PFC) on a lossless RoCE fabric. First, one receiver port fills in red and avoids dropping packets by sending per-class PAUSE frames upstream. Next, upstream switches halt and fill their own buffers, so pauses cascade backward tier by tier. Then the unrelated green flow heading to a healthy receiver stalls because it shares a paused switch, a phenomenon called congestion spreading that creates victim flows. One slow receiver or degrading optic can trigger this chain. Across 24,576 optical transceivers (cable-end converter modules) on 4,096 GPUs at a 0.001\% daily fault rate each, the daily chance of at least one degradation is 21.8\%. Stalling even one victim flow halts the synchronous barrier, increasing both $T_{\text{comm}}$ and $T_{\text{sync}}$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{PFC does not eliminate congestion; it trades packet drops for upstream pause spreading.}

    When a degrading transceiver cuts link throughput by 10\%, exposed communication stretches each step by only 2 to 3\% (at an assumed 30\% communication share, 3.3\% without overlap, derived), hiding inside routine checkpoint and data-loading jitter. Over 30 days on 1,024 GPUs (737,280 GPU-hours), that 2 to 3\% drag wastes 14,746 to 22,118 GPU-hours. At \$2 per GPU-hour, the loss reaches \$29,491 to \$44,237 (rounded to \$30,000 to \$44,000 in the chapter) with zero errors logged. Links that negotiate down to HDR instead of NDR during link training (hardware speed negotiation), or lose two of four lanes, remain ``up'' at half rate. Across about 3,000 links at an assumed 0.1\% degradation rate each, about 3 links are degraded at once (a 95\% chance of at least one). One slow link paces a whole ring AllReduce, an instance of partial, silent gray failure (Lecture 7).

    \notecue{Watch for}{Connectivity probes such as \texttt{ping} and basic Simple Network Management Protocol (SNMP) counters miss throughput loss. Both the 0.1\% rate and \$2 per GPU-hour are scenario assumptions.}

    \notecue{Next}{Without error alarms, how do operators tell healthy throughput from a silently degraded link?}
  }
\end{frame}

  \safeimg[width=0.82\textwidth,height=3.4cm,keepaspectratio]{sriov_slices.pdf}\\[0.15cm]
  {\small Virtual lanes and traffic classes keep separate buffers on one link; they share its bandwidth.}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
    On a shared cluster, slow steps can also come from competing jobs rather than a bad cable. Multi-tenant clusters isolate traffic in two ways. Single Root I/O Virtualization (SR-IOV) partitions one 400 Gb/s NIC into 8 hardware-isolated 50 Gb/s virtual functions. That stops competing tenants (noisy neighbors) from stealing bandwidth at the cost of a hard ceiling, because even a solo job on an idle node cannot burst past its 50 Gb/s slice. InfiniBand virtual lanes (up to 16) and Ethernet traffic classes instead separate switch buffers so 100 GB checkpoint writes (Lecture 4) do not cause head-of-line blocking (stalling gradient collectives behind queued checkpoint packets in a shared buffer), while still sharing full wire capacity.

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Together, the chapter's five-level fabric model answers today's driving question of what decides collective time and why a healthy-looking fabric can still stall a job. At Level 1 (wire), high-speed links pair four-level signaling (PAM4) with forward error correction (FEC), while copper covers 1 to 3 m and optics carry longer runs. At Level 2 (transport), $\alpha = 1.5$ \textmu s and $\beta = 50$ GB/s cross at 75 KB. At Level 3 (topology), non-blocking bisection reaches 25.6 TB/s versus 0.53 throughput under a worst-case 4:1 spine. Level 4 (load behavior) governs incast, PFC pauses, congestion control, and adaptive routing. Level 5 (cluster) combines four 256-GPU SuperPOD units into 1,024 GPUs. In Microsoft's 2016 outage, every wire and port was healthy, yet Level 4 flow control froze the fabric, confirming that tail collective completion defines performance. Keep three numbers in mind. They are the $9\times$ node cliff, the 75 KB crossover, and the $4\times$ cut penalty of a 4:1 spine.

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.14cm}
    \item Synchronous training waits for its slowest transfer, so completion time and its tail measure a fabric.
    \item $T(n) = \alpha + n/\beta$: below $n^* = \alpha\beta$ (75 KB on NDR) startup sets the time; above it, bandwidth.
    \item Bisection bandwidth caps cut-crossing traffic; wiring such as rails should follow the parallelism plan.
    \item Lossless flow control can spread pauses and deadlock; congestion control and routing act first.
    \item Fabric faults usually slow a job silently; compare with a baseline and diagnose top down.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    These five points mirror the five sections of the lecture. They cover judging fabrics by synchronous collective tail time, applying $T(n) = \alpha + n/\beta$ around the 75 KB NDR knee, matching bisection and rail topology to parallelism, acting before lossless queues deadlock, and diagnosing silent link slowdowns top down against baselines.
