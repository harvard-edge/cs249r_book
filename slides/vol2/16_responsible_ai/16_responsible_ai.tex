\begin{frame}{Predict: how many grades did the method lower?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{2 min}
  \centering
  {\small England, August 2020: exams canceled. Teachers submitted a grade per student; a statistical method recalculated them to match past years' grade distributions.}\\[0.2cm]

  {\normalsize\choice{A}{about 5\%}\qquad\choice{B}{about 15\%}\qquad\answerat{2}{C}{about 40\%}\qquad\choice{D}{about 70\%}}
  \mlsyscredit{Ofqual, Grading gaps in summer 2020 (July 2021), Table 1 and text (457,420 A level entries). Chapter War Story 16.2.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3.5 min}{Core}%
  In August 2020, after COVID-19 canceled England's A level exams (War Story 16.2), teachers submitted a center-assessed grade (CAG) and rank per student. To control grade inflation, Ofqual, England's qualifications regulator, ran a statistical algorithm matching each school's historical grade distribution. Predict what share of entries fell below teacher grades among A (5\%), B (15\%), C (40\%), and D (70\%).

  \notecue{Check}{The answer is C, about 40\%. Across 457,420 entries, 39\% fell below teacher grades (39.1\% in the interim report), while 59\% stayed equal and just over 2\% rose (Ofqual 2021, Table 1). A tempting answer is A, because matching national distributions seems likely to leave individual grades mostly intact, yet aggregate matching overturned nearly four in ten grades.}

  \notecue{Watch for}{The algorithm met its historical-distribution objective. The failure lay in the deployment evidence and remedy it lacked.}

  \notecue{Teach}{Take a one-minute finger vote on A, B, C, and D before revealing C.}

\section{Assurance}

\begin{frame}{A registry records what shipped but cannot justify it}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{assure_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{After chapter figure 1 (\S16.2), redrawn for the loan-screening case.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Lecture 12's model registry records which artifact shipped, yet it cannot justify why deployment was safe. The diagram builds a four-part assurance case for the loan-screening service. First comes a bounded claim for one intended use, supported by audits, logs, and disaggregated evidence broken down by subgroup, all linked through an explicit argument. Beneath that sits the set of operating assumptions about population, setting, suppliers, and workflow. Next, the case names an accountable owner authorized to launch, restrict, or block release, a role that was missing per student in Ofqual. Finally, an invalidation rule names the material changes that expire approval. Launch approval sits on the coordination axis ($C_3$), deciding when compute may run.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A fairness dashboard or a privacy accountant, which tracks cumulative privacy loss, supplies evidence only for a narrow claim. Neither is blanket proof that a system is responsible.}

\begin{frame}{Approval expires on material change; duties outlast shutdown}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{lifecycle_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Frameworks map onto it: NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, the EU AI Act.}
  \mlsyscredit{Chapter table 1 and table 2 (\S16.2); Tabassi 2023 (NIST AI 100-1); EU Regulation 2024/1689.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  An assurance case is not a one-time sign-off because models, data, and contexts keep changing, so governance spans eight lifecycle stages (table 1). At Launch, a signed record identifies who authorized release and who may halt it. Two durability rules govern what follows. First, Material change reopens risk review when a team swaps a large pretrained base model, a retrieval corpus (the document collection a model searches when answering), a population, a region, or a workflow, even with zero source-code change. Second, Retire confirms that stopping inference does not end appeals for past decisions or data retention and deletion duties. Major frameworks map onto this loop, including the 2023 U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology AI Risk Management Framework (NIST AI RMF, organized around Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage), the international ISO/IEC 42001 organizational AI management-system standard, and the 2024 EU AI Act, whose risk tiers attach to uses and affected people rather than server location, echoing Lecture 1's governance control plane.

  \notecue{Next}{Next, test whether swapping a retrieval corpus without changing code leaves prior approval valid.}
}
\end{frame}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  The loan model failed every criterion, yet real deployments face a mathematical limit. In 2016, ProPublica (Angwin et al. 2016) audited COMPAS, a proprietary criminal recidivism risk score used in U.S. courts, finding Black defendants falsely flagged nearly twice as often as white defendants while its vendor defended score precision. Recomputing ProPublica's data with its published filters and right-hand outcome counts (true and false positives and negatives, TP, FP, FN, and TN) shows unequal base rates, the actual reoffending shares, at 52.3\% for Black defendants versus 39.1\% for white defendants. Flagged PPV stays close at 65.0\% versus 59.5\%, yet false positive rates diverge at 42.3\% versus 22.0\% (45\% versus 23\% in ProPublica's report). By Chouldechova (2017) and Kleinberg et al. (2017) in principle 26, no imperfect score with unequal base rates equalizes predictive value and error rates together, just as equal TPR breaks demographic parity when qualified shares differ. Both sides measured true properties, making criterion selection a governance choice rather than an optimization bug.

  \notecue{Next}{Next, see how postprocessing with separate group thresholds equalizes TPR while splitting identical scores.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Group thresholds equalize TPR but split equal scores}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.88\textwidth,height=4.7cm,keepaspectratio]{dual_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small A parity policy on another credit model cost 4 accuracy points (85 to 81\%).}
  \mlsyscredit{Illustrative scores after chapter figure 5 (\S16.5.3); rates. Napkin Math 16.4 (scenario).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Optional}%
  Once an owner chooses one criterion such as equal opportunity, the most direct lever on a trained model is postprocessing its decision thresholds. Under one shared cutoff of 0.75 across both loan groups (pluses mark repayers and circles mark defaulters), 75\% of qualified Group A applicants pass versus 25\% of Group B, a threefold opportunity gap. Lowering Group B's threshold to 0.45 equalizes TPR at 75\%, yet raises Group B's FPR from 0\% to 25\% and gives two applicants with identical 0.60 scores opposite decisions. Runtime threshold lookup requires authorized access to group attributes, which some jurisdictions restrict or prohibit, along with policy logging, while leaving score calibration (Lecture 14) untouched. In Napkin Math 16.4, raising Group B's approval rate from 60\% to 80\% to match Group A cost 4 accuracy points, dropping accuracy from 85\% to 81\%, a 4.7\% relative reduction.

  \notecue{Teach}{In a 50-minute class, skip this slide and note that COMPAS already proved the criterion conflict, while group thresholds trade FPR and individual consistency.}

\begin{frame}{Will more data remove this bias?}
  \mlsysactivity{Concept question}{2 min}
  \centering
  {\small A hospital risk score predicts next year's health \emph{cost} to choose patients for extra care.}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<2->{\safeimg[width=0.80\textwidth,height=3.1cm,keepaspectratio]{obermeyer.pdf}\\[0.05cm]}
  {\normalsize\choice{A}{Yes, more data}\qquad\answerat{2}{B}{No: the label is wrong}\qquad\choice{C}{Yes, a bigger model}}
  \mlsyscredit{Obermeyer et al., Science 2019 (chapter \S16.5.1 and footnote 7): simulated correction at the automatic-enrollment threshold.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Optional}%
  Thresholds adjust rates for a fixed label, yet bias can live in the target itself. A widely used U.S. hospital score (Obermeyer et al., Science 2019) predicts next year's health cost to pick patients for extra care. Decide whether more data (A), fixing the label (B), or a bigger model (C) removes the bias.

  \notecue{Check}{The answer is B. Unequal access meant equally sick Black patients incurred lower costs, creating proxy-label bias. Replacing cost with illness burden lifted Black patients' share at the automatic-enrollment threshold 2.6$\times$, from 17.7\% to 46.5\%. Options A and C are tempting, yet more data reduces sampling noise and more capacity only predicts spending better, leaving target-label misspecification intact. Unlike COMPAS's mathematical conflict, a bad proxy is a design defect, showing that criterion choice is governance and thresholds cannot repair a wrong label.}

  \notecue{Teach}{Take a vote and brief discussion on A, B, and C before revealing B. In a 50-minute class, skip this slide and close the fairness section on COMPAS.}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item An assurance case justifies one use and expires on material change.
    \item Unequal base rates make criteria conflict; choosing one is governance.
    \item A gap is evidence only with its denominator, interval, and subgroups.
    \item Each safeguard is capacity on a named path; plan deletion before training.
    \item Evidence protects people only when an owner can correct a decision.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Together, these five rules turn the Ofqual postmortem into a pre-launch and post-launch engineering checklist. Launches rest on expiring assurance cases, and fairness criteria are governance choices under base-rate conflict. Measured gaps count as evidence only with denominators and subgroups. Every safeguard requires a capacity budget, with machine unlearning planned before training, and every consequential decision needs an accountable owner and a correction path.

  \notecue{Teach}{If short on time, emphasize the first and fifth bullets.}
