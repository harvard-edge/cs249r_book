\begin{frame}{Two jobs holding half a pool each make zero progress}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{gang_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Orchestration, \S8.4.3 and figure 4; War Story 8.1 (Sigler 2021, OpenAI).}
  \note{[3 min] NARRATE: An 8-GPU pool, two jobs that each need all 8. Synchronous training needs every rank in every collective, so 4 of 8 GPUs complete zero steps; they do not complete half as many. Step 1: placed one GPU at a time, J1 gets 4 and waits for the rest. Step 2: J2 gets the other 4 and also waits. Each holds what the other needs. This is a hold-and-wait deadlock, and all 8 GPUs burn power at zero progress. Step 3: gang scheduling grants J1 all 8 at once; J2 holds nothing until J1 ends. At the chapter's \$2 per GPU-hour, a deadlock that strands a 10,000-GPU cluster wastes \$20,000 an hour. OpenAI used exactly this two-job example when it scaled Kubernetes to 7,500 nodes, and added group admission. ENGAGE: Does giving J1 higher priority fix it? (Expected: no; priority orders requests, it does not stop partial holds.) WARN: Atomicity must cover every resource the group needs, including its NVLink domain and network share, or the deadlock moves one layer down. FLEX: [CORE]}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backfill fills the wait without delaying the gang}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{backfill_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Orchestration, \S8.4.3 (after Lifka 1995) and figure 5. Layout illustrative.}
  \note{[2 min] NARRATE: Atomic allocation has a cost. A 1,024-GPU gang at the head of the queue, 900 GPUs free. If every freed GPU goes to a small job, the gang never assembles.   \note{[3 min] NARRATE: Equal GPU counts are not fair when one tenant exhausts CPU and another the network. Dominant resource fairness (DRF) takes each tenant's largest share of any resource, its dominant share, and equalizes that. Step 1: one A replica uses 1 of 8 GPUs, 4 of 32 cores, 32 of 256 GB, and 10 of 80 Gb/s, an eighth of each. Four replicas give A one half everywhere. Step 2: a B worker takes 2 of 8 GPUs and a quarter of the network, so two workers give B a dominant share of one half, though only 0.125 of the CPU and memory. Both tenants sit at 0.50. ENGAGE: Which comes first, the filter or the share? (Expected: the filter, the book's feasibility-before-fairness principle, because a fair share of accelerators a job cannot run on delivers no progress.) WARN: When no equal-share placement exists, the scheduler should report the binding constraint rather than show an infeasible share as service. FLEX: [CORE]}
\end{frame}
