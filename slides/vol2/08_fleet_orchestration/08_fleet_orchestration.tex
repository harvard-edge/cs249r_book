\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  To weigh those four decisions quantitatively, place each one inside Lecture 1's Fleet Law. Placement decides which links carry each collective communication across GPUs, directly setting communication time $T_{\text{comm}}(N)$ on every step. Preemption and failure recovery spread across the run inside synchronization overhead $T_{\text{sync}}(N)$. Queue wait and gang assembly happen before the first training step begins. They never appear in per-step time $T_{\text{step}}(N)$, yet they lengthen end-to-end completion time directly. Waiting 4 hours for a compact placement that speeds steps by 10\% lowers $T_{\text{comm}}$, yet it wins overall only if the total run is long enough to repay the 4-hour delay.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A fast per-step profile can hide hours of queue wait that make end-to-end completion worse.}

  \notecue{Next}{Section 1 turns to admission and explains why large training gangs require all-or-nothing grants.}

\begin{frame}{A scheduler admits, places, then reconciles every job}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=4.7cm,keepaspectratio]{control_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small The scheduler is the fleet's \textbf{allocation control plane}.}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Orchestration, \S8.2 and \S8.2.1.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Whichever platform runs the fleet, coordinating distributed nodes differs from a single-server operating system that observes local hardware directly and fails as one unit. A cluster scheduler therefore separates its allocation control plane into three stages (\S8.2). Admission checks quotas, priorities, and whether an entire gang can enter. Placement assigns nodes matching GPU generation, topology, power, and storage bandwidth. Reconciliation compares desired state against reported state to start, retry, evict, or fence dead nodes, which is essential when a 10,000-GPU fleet fails every few hours (Lecture 7). Separating these stages shows whether a pending job waits on quota, topology fit, or a dead node. For example, losing 1 of 4 nodes before launch strands 24 GPUs during re-planning. Missed heartbeats (Lecture 7) never authorize reuse until epoch fencing revokes the prior lease, reflecting Lecture 1's consistency choice under a partition (CAP) and Lecture 7's fencing tokens.

\begin{frame}{Backfill fills the wait without delaying the gang}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{backfill_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Orchestration, \S8.4.3 (after Lifka 1995) and figure 5. Layout illustrative.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Atomic gang admission removes deadlock yet creates a starvation dilemma. Suppose 900 GPUs sit free while a 1,024-GPU gang waits for 124 more. Continually admitting small jobs starves the gang, whereas leaving 900 GPUs idle wastes capacity. EASY backfill, popularized by Lifka in 1995 for the ANL/IBM SP supercomputer, resolves this by setting a future reservation start time for the gang and opening the 900-GPU gap to short jobs. The timeline admits a 2-hour 128-GPU job that finishes before the reservation, yet holds back a 4-hour 128-GPU job that would delay it. Because users can understate runtimes, backfill requires enforced time limits or preemption contracts, plus reserved exit bandwidth so evicted jobs do not cause priority inversion by blocking higher-priority work (\S8.4.4). With atomic admission, reservations, and backfill preventing deadlock and starvation, the scheduler must next decide which waiting team goes first.

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    When tenants consume different mixes of GPU, CPU, RAM, and network, Dominant Resource Fairness (DRF; Ghodsi et al., NSDI 2011) equalizes each tenant's largest fractional share of any resource. Giving Tenant A four replicas claims 50\% of GPUs, CPUs, RAM, and network for a dominant share of 0.50. Adding Tenant B with two workers uses 50\% of GPUs and network alongside 12.5\% of CPU and RAM, also reaching a dominant share of 0.50. Equal shares still fail unless the allocation can actually run, which is feasibility before fairness. If Tenant B needs a 4-GPU gang inside one 80 GB NVLink node, four scattered or 40 GB GPUs yield zero progress. Filter to feasible placements first, share what passes, and report the limiting constraint when equal shares cannot be placed.

Submitting \texttt{sbatch --gpus=16} starts sooner by scattering across partial nodes, whereas \texttt{--nodes=2 --gpus-per-node=8} preserves intra-node NVLink while still needing local CPU, RAM, and NIC fit. Resolving fragmentation requires draining a node through reservations or migrating small jobs at checkpoint and restart cost, never overcommitting exclusive GPUs by assigning more demand than physical devices hold.

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    An unlabeled dashboard percentage conflates three time-averaged tiers (\S8.12.1). First, allocated capacity counts 500 GPUs held by complete running jobs, excluding gangs still waiting in the queue. Next, active capacity counts 350 GPU-equivalents, or 70\%, running GPU compute kernels on NVIDIA Data Center GPU Manager (DCGM) counters (\texttt{DEV\_GPU\_UTIL} or \texttt{PROF\_SM\_ACTIVE}), which still count communication kernels spinning while waiting on peers and replayed steps as busy. Finally, productive capacity counts 280 GPU-equivalents, or 56\%, delivering retained progress at the fault-free rate. Each gap points to a distinct cause. Low allocated capacity points to quota, fragmentation, or hardware fit, which Lecture 9 develops as scheduling goodput (the share of offered capacity in complete allocations). Low active capacity points to input stalls (Lecture 4) or synchronization barriers. Low productive capacity points to preemption and crash replay, Lecture 7's runtime goodput loss.

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item Admit rigid gangs all at once; pair reservations with enforced backfill.
    \item Filter to feasible allocations first, then share fairly and track slowdown.
    \item Map frequent collectives to fast links; price elastic resizes by completion time.
    \item Spot discounts and borrowed quota pay only after lost work and restart.
    \item Separate allocated, active, and productive GPUs; idle pools usually mean stale policy.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Together, these five takeaways answer our driving question of who gets which accelerators, when, and where, and how operators know the fleet is used well. They combine atomic gang admission with reservations and enforced backfill, apply feasibility before fairness tracked by slowdown, pair topology-aware placement with completion-time elasticity, price spot and borrowed quota after preemption costs, and separate allocated, active, and productive tiers to catch stale policy.

    \notecue{Teach}{If short on time, emphasize items 1 and 5.}

\end{frame}
