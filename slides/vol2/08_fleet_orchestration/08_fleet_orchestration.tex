\begin{frame}{A scheduler admits, places, then reconciles every job}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=4.7cm,keepaspectratio]{control_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small The scheduler is the fleet's \textbf{allocation control plane}.}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Orchestration, \S8.2 and \S8.2.1.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Unlike a single-server OS that sees all hardware locally and fails as one unit, a fleet scheduler separates its allocation control plane into three steps (\S8.2). First, step 1 (Admission) checks quotas, priorities, and whether an entire gang can enter. Second, step 2 (Placement) assigns nodes matching GPU generation, topology, power, and storage bandwidth. Third, step 3 (Reconciliation) compares desired against reported state to start, retry, evict, or fence dead nodes (essential when a 10,000-GPU fleet fails every few hours; Lecture 7). Separating these stages lets operators tell instantly whether a pending job waits on quota, topology fit, or a dead node (e.g., 1 of 4 nodes dying before launch strands 24 GPUs during re-planning); missing heartbeats never authorize reuse until fencing revokes the prior lease (Lecture 1's CAP choice, Lecture 7's fence).

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two jobs holding half a pool each make zero progress}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{1 min}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{gang_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Orchestration, \S8.4.3 and figure 4 (interactive Explore figure: Gang Scheduling Deadlock); War Story 8.1 (Sigler 2021, OpenAI).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    Trace gang scheduling deadlock across three steps on an 8-GPU pool shared by two 8-GPU synchronous jobs (War Story 8.1; Sigler, OpenAI 2021). First, step 1 places J1 incrementally onto 4 GPUs, where it blocks waiting for its remaining 4. Second, step 2 places J2 onto the other 4 GPUs: both hold resources the other needs, burning power at zero progress (a hold-and-wait deadlock worth \$20,000/hour across 10,000 GPUs at \$2/GPU-hour). Third, step 3 enforces atomic gang admission: J1 receives all 8 GPUs at once while J2 waits cleanly with zero GPUs held.

    \notecue{Check}{Pause before step 3 and ask whether simply raising J1's priority solves the deadlock. Priority alone fails because priority orders incoming requests without forbidding partial holds; only atomic all-or-nothing gang admission breaks hold-and-wait.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{Atomicity must cover every coupled resource (GPUs, NVLink domain, and NIC/fabric share), or deadlocks shift one layer down.}

\begin{frame}{Backfill fills the wait without delaying the gang}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{backfill_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Orchestration, \S8.4.3 (after Lifka 1995) and figure 5. Layout illustrative.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Atomic gang admission creates a starvation dilemma: if 900 GPUs sit free while a 1,024-GPU gang waits for 124 more, continually admitting small jobs starves the gang forever, whereas leaving 900 GPUs empty wastes capacity. EASY backfill (Lifka, 1995) sets a future reservation start time for the gang and opens the 900-GPU gap to short jobs. First, step 1 admits a 128-GPU job finishing in 2 hours before the reservation begins. Second, step 2 blocks a 4-hour 128-GPU job that would delay the reservation. Because users have an incentive to understate durations, backfill works only with enforced runtime kills or preemption contracts plus reserved bandwidth so evicted jobs exit cleanly without priority inversion (\S8.4.4).

  }
\end{frame}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    When tenants consume different mixes of GPU, CPU, RAM, and network, Dominant Resource Fairness (DRF; Ghodsi et al., NSDI 2011) equalizes each tenant's largest fractional resource share. First, step 1 gives Tenant A four replicas using 50\% of GPUs, CPUs, RAM, and network (dominant share 0.50). Second, step 2 gives Tenant B two workers using 50\% of GPUs and network alongside 12.5\% of CPU/RAM (dominant share 0.50, equalized). Third, step 3 introduces feasibility before fairness: if B needs a 4-GPU gang inside one 80 GB NVLink node, four scattered or 40 GB GPUs yield zero progress. Always filter to feasible placements first, share what passes, and report the limiting constraint whenever equal shares cannot be placed.

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item Admit rigid gangs all at once; pair reservations with enforced backfill.
    \item Filter to feasible allocations first, then share fairly and track slowdown.
    \item Map frequent collectives to fast links; price elastic resizes by completion time.
    \item Spot discounts and borrowed quota pay only after lost work and restart.
    \item Separate allocated, active, and productive GPUs; idle pools usually mean stale policy.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    These five takeaways span today's arc: atomic gang admission paired with reservations and enforced backfill, feasibility before fairness tracked by slowdown, topology-aware placement and completion-time elasticity, pricing spot and borrowed quota after preemption costs, and auditing allocated/active/productive tiers to catch stale policy. If short on time, emphasize items 1 and 5.
