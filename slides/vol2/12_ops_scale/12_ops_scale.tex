\begin{frame}{Pairwise couplings grow as $N^2/2$ while alerts grow as $N$}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{load_step}{3}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  If adding models only multiplied independent work, operations could scale linearly with headcount. To see why load outruns a fixed team instead, compare three illustrative curves against a 2,500-unit team-capacity line on log-log axes (assumed shapes, not measurements). First, alerts grow linearly at 20 units per model ($20N$) as each model adds monitored metrics. Next, deployment coordination uses an illustrative $N \log N$ shape (not a formal bound) because scheduling releases and verifying cross-model ordering add superlinear overhead beyond deploying each model alone. Finally, once models share features, embeddings, pipelines, and GPU pools, any two can conflict, adding up to $N^2/2$ pairwise couplings. At $N=100$, that reaches 5,000 pairs against 2,000 alert units, and total load crosses the 2,500-unit capacity line near 50 models. Operational load therefore follows coupling rather than model count alone.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Recorded dependencies determine which pairs truly interact. A portfolio of 100 isolated models carries almost no conflict term, whereas a dense graph crosses team capacity early, so no universal cutoff can be read off this sketch.}

  \notecue{Next}{A production case study from Meta's FBLearner Flow quantifies this shared-workflow coupling.}
}

\begin{frame}{A dependency-aware registry turns edges into an impact set}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{registry_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\footnotesize MLflow records versions: \texttt{mlflow.register\_model(...)}; load \texttt{models:/MyModel@champion}}
  \mlsyscredit{After ML Operations at Scale, figure 3 and \S12.3.1. Code: MLflow Model Registry documentation (mlflow.org).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  To control that coupling and enforce consumer evidence before a change ships, a fleet needs an explicit map of who reads what. MLflow, an ML lifecycle platform, tracks versions and aliases in its model registry through \texttt{mlflow.register\_model} and uniform resource identifiers (URIs) such as \texttt{models:/MyModel@champion} atop Lecture 4's versioned artifacts, while fleet operations adds explicit consumer edges. In this recommendation portfolio, embedding model E advances from v3.1 to v3.2 and passes its own tests, which never proves that downstream consumers still work. Traversing registry edges builds the impact set, reaching candidate retrieval (selecting relevant items) at hop 1, three rankers at hop 2, and the ensemble (combining their scores) at hop 3. The release controller re-evaluates every consumer, blocks E until all pass, and promotes one compatible version set with a matched rollback target, because reverting E alone breaks rankers retrained on v3.2. An unrecorded consumer such as the fraud model escapes traversal, leaving an outage to reveal the missing edge.

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Training and serving impose opposite storage demands on the same feature. Training scans terabytes of history in batch, whereas serving reads one entity's latest vector in milliseconds. Feast splits storage under one definition, using a \texttt{FeatureView} to declare name, entity, schema, and TTL (time to live, how long a value stays valid) once before writing computed values into offline and online stores. Sharing one definition makes training-serving skew (divergent values between training and live serving) testable once for every team. Serving 1 billion recommendations/day that each fetch 100 features creates 100 billion lookups/day, or about 1.16M/s before peaks. Within a 50 ms serving budget, the online store receives only 5 to 10 ms. Freshness SLOs match feature volatility, spanning days for demographics, minutes for session context, and seconds for the last click.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A recent write timestamp does not prove freshness because rewriting stale values updates it. Monitor event-time watermark progress (the event time through which the source stream has been processed) instead; stale features still leave endpoints fast and available.}

\begin{frame}{Rollout risk is regression probability $\times$ harm $\times$ exposure}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.36\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{canary-coal-mine.jpg}\\[0.05cm]
      {\footnotesize A 1928 canary for carbon monoxide tests. A staged rollout sends a canary release to a small share of traffic first.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.60\textwidth}
      \centering
      \mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=4.3cm,keepaspectratio]{exposure_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
      \uncover<2->{\footnotesize A silent 0.5-point CTR loss at 5,000 requests/s costs \textbf{\$1.08M per day}.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: George McCaa, U.S. Bureau of Mines, public domain, Wikimedia Commons. ML Operations at Scale, equation 8 and Napkin Math 12.5, 12.6.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Even a candidate that passes offline and feature checks can still fail on live users, so every release needs an exposure budget. Equation 8 decomposes rollout risk into three factors. Pre-release testing lowers regression probability, validated fallbacks lower harm per exposed unit, and traffic caps with fast rollback lower exposed units. Named after the coal-mine practice of using canaries for early carbon monoxide warning (pictured here in 1928), a canary release sends a small slice of live traffic to the candidate first. An all-at-once blue-green cutover shifts 100\% of traffic to a parallel environment immediately, exposing every user until rollback finishes, whereas a 5\% canary cuts exposure 20$\times$ for the same detection time. Shrinking a canary further stops helping if thinner traffic stretches detection time proportionally. In Napkin Math 12.6, a silent 0.5-point CTR drop at 5,000 req/s loses 2.16M clicks/day, reaching \$1.08M/day at \$0.50/click.

  \notecue{Next}{Trace how shadow traffic, canary guardrails, and staged promotion build evidence before full exposure.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Shadow traffic tests a candidate before any user sees it}

  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%

Shadow deployment mirrors live production requests asynchronously to candidate v2 while v1 serves 100\% of users, logging outputs for drift, crashes, and tail latency at zero user harm while trading extra inference compute for safety. Owning costs \$59.8M up front (\$44.8M servers at \$350K/node, \$5M InfiniBand, \$10M facility) plus \$1.5M/yr for power and staff. Renting at \$4/GPU-h starts at zero and rises linearly. At 80\% sustained utilization, rent runs \$2.39M/month and crosses ownership at 26.4 months inside a 3-year life; at 50\% (derived), \$1.50M/month stays cheaper across all 36 months. Break-even shifts with cost scope, from 42.4\% for one node (hardware and power only) to 59.7\% for this cluster and 53.9\% at 10,000 GPUs. On this same 1,024-H100 cluster over 3 years, six two-to-four-week runs/year favor renting at \$1.4M to \$2.8M per run versus \$3.6M per run owned (spreading the 3-year total across eighteen runs), and rollback or canary reserves must justify their idle cost through reliability value.

  \notecue{Next}{Work Napkin Math 12.9 by hand to price owning versus renting 10,000 H100s over three years.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The dominant cost term flips from iteration to inference}
  \centering

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
  Once capacity is priced, optimization targets whichever lifecycle cost dominates. Total cost of ownership (TCO) covers developing, deploying, and operating models. Equation 12 sums lifecycle TCO across four non-overlapping terms covering training, inference, data, and iteration. In the chapter's constructed budgets, a 1-model startup spends \$47.5K/month (84\% iteration), whereas a 50-model company spends \$980K/month where inference leads at 41\%, matching Lecture 10's serving-cost dominance while retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), fetching documents into prompts at inference, raises inference and data costs. Spending \$80K on INT8 integer quantization to cut a \$400K/month inference bill by 40\% pays back in 2 weeks, whereas \$80K cutting the \$150K/month training bill by 30\% saves \$45K/month and pays back in 1.8 months. Platform spend scales likewise. At 50 models, saving 20 engineer-hours per model monthly at \$150/h over a \$120K/month platform team gives a 1.25 benefit-cost ratio ($50 \times 20\text{ h} \times \$150 / \$120\text{K}$), whereas at 20 models the platform loses half its cost.

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item The unit to operate is the dependency graph; a release names its impact set and a compatible version set.
    \item A feature is a versioned interface; join on availability time, version semantic changes.
    \item Exposure is share times time; one versioned contract links all release evidence.
    \item Alerts route by level to owners, and the worst SLO dimension stops promotion.
    \item Owning pays only above a sustained useful utilization; the dominant cost term picks the fix.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Together, these five operating contracts answer how an organization runs hundreds of models without $N^2$ manual coordination. Operate the dependency graph rather than isolated models, version features with availability-time joins, and budget exposure under one versioned evaluation contract. Route alerts hierarchically with worst-SLO promotion freezes, and anchor fleet ownership to sustained useful utilization.

  \notecue{Teach}{If pressed for time, emphasize the first and fourth takeaways.}
