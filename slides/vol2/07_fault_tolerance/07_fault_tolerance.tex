\begin{frame}{A 50,000-hour GPU fails every 5 hours in a 10,000-GPU job}
  \centering
  {\normalsize $R_{\text{sys}}(t) = e^{-N\lambda t}$ \qquad $\text{MTBF}_{\text{sys}} = \dfrac{1}{N\lambda} = \dfrac{\text{MTTF}}{N}$}\quad{\small MTBF: mean time between job failures}\\[0.1cm]
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.95cm,keepaspectratio]{mtbf_ladder_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Fault Tolerance, equations 3 and 4 and table 1. GPU-only basis: 50,000 h per GPU, independent, constant rate.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Under constant failure rate $\lambda = 1 / 50{,}000\text{ h}^{-1}$ and independent devices, survival probability decays exponentially as $R_{\text{sys}}(t) = e^{-N\lambda t}$ with system mean time between failures $\text{MTBF}_{\text{sys}} = \text{MTTF}/N$. First, step 1 shows small jobs failing rarely: every 6,250 h (260 days) at 8 GPUs and 33 days at 64 GPUs. Second, step 2 crosses into sub-week red bars: 4 days at 512 GPUs and 2 days at 1,000. Third, step 3 hits 5 hours at 10,000 GPUs and 2 hours at 25,000 GPUs, meaning a three-month run expects hundreds of stops (mirroring Lecture 1's $0.9999^{10{,}000} = 36.8\%$ availability cliff).

    \notecue{Watch for}{MTBF is a long-run planning mean rather than a fixed alarm schedule; short jobs can finish cleanly while others hit clustered faults.}

    \notecue{Next}{Students next add PCIe links, NICs, and redundant power supplies to build the full-system failure cascade.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Persistence chooses retry, quarantine, or replacement}
  \centering
  {\normalsize Three axes: \textbf{persistence} (recurs?), \textbf{manifestation} (crash, slow, wrong?), \textbf{scope} (blast radius).}\\[0.28cm]
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}M{2.6cm}M{4.2cm}M{4.6cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    Persistence & Signature & Fleet response \\
    \midrule
    \textcolor{GreenLine}{\textbf{Transient}}\newline\tikz[baseline=-2pt]{\draw[GreenLine!35,line width=0.8pt] (0,0)--(1.1,0);\draw[GreenLine,line width=1.4pt] (0.55,0)--(0.55,0.24);} & Single flip or lost packet; retry passes & Retry; roll back if saved state changed \\
    \uncover<2->{\textcolor{OrangeLine}{\textbf{Intermittent}}\newline\tikz[baseline=-2pt]{\draw[OrangeLine!35,line width=0.8pt] (0,0)--(1.1,0);\draw[OrangeLine,line width=1.4pt] (0.2,0)--(0.2,0.24) (0.55,0)--(0.55,0.24) (0.9,0)--(0.9,0.24);}} & \uncover<2->{Recurs under heat, load, or voltage} & \uncover<2->{Drain, quarantine, stress-test under load} \\
    \uncover<3->{\textcolor{RedLine}{\textbf{Permanent}}\newline\tikz[baseline=-2pt]{\draw[RedLine,line width=1.6pt] (0,0.22)--(1.1,0.22);\fill[RedLine!15] (0,0) rectangle (1.1,0.22);}} & \uncover<3->{Repeats at same device, address, or link} & \uncover<3->{Replace hardware; restore prior checkpoint} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \mlsyscredit{Fault Tolerance, \S7.3 and \S7.4.1, table 3; fault, error, failure: Avizienis et al. 2004.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Following Avizienis et al. (2004), a fault is the underlying defect, an error is corrupted internal state, and a failure is externally visible misbehavior, classified across three orthogonal axes: persistence, manifestation (crash, slow, wrong), and scope (blast radius). First, step 1 defines transient faults (e.g., a bit flip or dropped packet): retry cleanly, rolling back only if bad state persisted. Second, step 2 defines intermittent faults (e.g., a cracked solder joint recurring under heat or load): pass-on-reboot checks miss them, so drain, quarantine, and stress-test under load. Third, step 3 defines permanent faults (e.g., stuck multiplier bits): replace hardware and restore from a prior checkpoint.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The three axes are orthogonal rather than mutually exclusive: a single transient fault can either crash a process loudly or corrupt a gradient silently.}

    \notecue{Next}{Students next classify three concrete production incidents on these axes and choose the right response.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A checkpoint exists only when its manifest is published last}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%

First, step 1 shows uncoordinated saves: when W1 rolls back after a fault, W2 and W3 have already consumed messages from W1's rolled-back steps, triggering cascading rollbacks (the domino effect). Second, step 2 uses synchronous step boundaries for coordinated checkpointing across a consistent cut (essential under sharded ZeRO/FSDP from Lecture 6, where no rank holds the whole model). Third, step 3 enforces two-phase commit: in Prepare, every rank writes an immutable shard and digest and confirms; in Commit, the coordinator atomically publishes the manifest naming all shards. \begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item A job's failure interval is MTTF over $N$ at best; name the basis, and expect measured clusters to fail sooner.
    \item Persistence, manifestation, and scope choose the response; liveness checks miss slow and wrong.
    \item Save every $\sqrt{2\delta M}$, and count only a manifest-published, consistent cut as saved.
    \item Each failure costs detection, restart, load, and warmup; fencing and elastic invariants keep recovery correct.
    \item Serving buys availability with replicas, sizes failover by state, and degrades by a validated ladder.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    These five points mirror today's five arcs: naming the MTBF basis and expecting measured clusters to fail faster than $\text{MTTF}/N$, classifying persistence/manifestation/scope because liveness misses slow and wrong, saving at $\sqrt{2\delta M}$ with manifest-last commits, budgeting recovery with fences and elastic invariants, and serving via replicas and validated degradation ladders.

    \notecue{Next}{An optional slide poses three open reliability questions at 100,000-GPU scale.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35cm}
    \item At 100,000 GPUs the optimized waste $\sqrt{2\delta/M}$ reaches about 19\%. Would you cut $\delta$, raise $M$, or shorten recovery first?
    \item Six of 419 interruptions were detected silent corruption. How much compute should a run spend checking for the ones nobody detected?
    \item For a chat service, which state would you replicate, and which would you rebuild?
  \end{itemize}
