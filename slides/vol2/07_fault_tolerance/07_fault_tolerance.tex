\begin{frame}{A 50,000-hour GPU fails every 5 hours in a 10,000-GPU job}
  \centering
  {\normalsize $R_{\text{sys}}(t) = e^{-N\lambda t}$ \qquad $\text{MTBF}_{\text{sys}} = \dfrac{1}{N\lambda} = \dfrac{\text{MTTF}}{N}$}\quad{\small MTBF: mean time between job failures}\\[0.1cm]
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.95cm,keepaspectratio]{mtbf_ladder_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Fault Tolerance, equations 3 and 4 and table 1. GPU-only basis: 50,000 h per GPU, independent, constant rate.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    That 5-hour result is one point on a $1/N$ collapse that turns rare hardware faults into routine stops as cluster size grows. Under a constant failure rate $\lambda = 1 / 50{,}000\text{ h}^{-1}$ and independent devices, survival probability decays exponentially as $R_{\text{sys}}(t) = e^{-N\lambda t}$, giving $\text{MTBF}_{\text{sys}} = \text{MTTF}/N$. Small jobs run for months between stops, dropping from 6,250 h (260 days) at 8 GPUs to 33 days at 64 GPUs. Past a few hundred devices, the interval falls below a week, reaching 4 days at 512 GPUs and 2 days at 1,000 GPUs. At frontier scale, it reaches 5 hours at 10,000 GPUs and 2 hours at 25,000 GPUs. A three-month run at that scale expects hundreds of stops, mirroring Lecture 1's $0.9999^{10{,}000} = 36.8\%$ joint-availability cliff.

    \notecue{Watch for}{MTBF is a long-run planning mean rather than a fixed alarm schedule. Short jobs can finish cleanly while others hit clustered faults.}

    \notecue{Next}{GPUs never run alone in a server, so next add PCIe links, NICs, and redundant power supplies to build the full-system failure cascade.}
  }
\end{frame}

    Independence breaks down because real racks share top-of-rack (ToR) switches, power feeds, cooling loops, and software versions, expanding Lecture 2's physical hierarchy into nested failure domains. \begin{frame}{Persistence chooses retry, quarantine, or replacement}
  \centering
  {\normalsize Three axes: \textbf{persistence} (recurs?), \textbf{manifestation} (crash, slow, wrong?), \textbf{scope} (blast radius).}\\[0.28cm]
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}M{2.6cm}M{4.2cm}M{4.6cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    Persistence & Signature & Fleet response \\
    \midrule
    \textcolor{GreenLine}{\textbf{Transient}}\newline\tikz[baseline=-2pt]{\draw[GreenLine!35,line width=0.8pt] (0,0)--(1.1,0);\draw[GreenLine,line width=1.4pt] (0.55,0)--(0.55,0.24);} & Single flip or lost packet; retry passes & Retry; roll back if saved state changed \\
    \uncover<2->{\textcolor{OrangeLine}{\textbf{Intermittent}}\newline\tikz[baseline=-2pt]{\draw[OrangeLine!35,line width=0.8pt] (0,0)--(1.1,0);\draw[OrangeLine,line width=1.4pt] (0.2,0)--(0.2,0.24) (0.55,0)--(0.55,0.24) (0.9,0)--(0.9,0.24);}} & \uncover<2->{Recurs under heat, load, or voltage} & \uncover<2->{Drain, quarantine, stress-test under load} \\
    \uncover<3->{\textcolor{RedLine}{\textbf{Permanent}}\newline\tikz[baseline=-2pt]{\draw[RedLine,line width=1.6pt] (0,0.22)--(1.1,0.22);\fill[RedLine!15] (0,0) rectangle (1.1,0.22);}} & \uncover<3->{Repeats at same device, address, or link} & \uncover<3->{Replace hardware; restore prior checkpoint} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \mlsyscredit{Fault Tolerance, \S7.3 and \S7.4.1, table 3; fault, error, failure: Avizienis et al. 2004.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Retrying dead silicon wastes cluster time, while replacing a GPU after a one-time bit flip discards healthy hardware. In the standard reliability taxonomy of Avizienis et al. (2004), a fault is the underlying defect, an error is corrupted internal state, and a failure is externally visible misbehavior. Choosing the response rests on three orthogonal (independent) axes, namely persistence, manifestation (crash, slow, or wrong), and scope (blast radius). Transient faults, such as a bit flip or dropped packet, clear on retry, rolling back only if corrupted state escaped. Intermittent faults, such as a cracked solder joint that returns under heat or load, pass cold reboots, so the fleet drains, quarantines, and stress-tests the host under load. Permanent faults, such as stuck multiplier bits, repeat on every attempt and require hardware replacement plus checkpoint restoration.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The three axes are independent rather than mutually exclusive. A single transient fault can either crash a process loudly or corrupt a gradient silently.}

    \notecue{Next}{Test this taxonomy on three production incidents to choose the right first action for each.}

\begin{frame}{A checkpoint exists only when its manifest is published last}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.1cm,keepaspectratio]{cut_step}{2}\\[0.15cm]
  \footnotesize
  \uncover<3->{%
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lP{10.2cm}@{}}
    \textbf{Prepare} & Each rank writes its shard and digest, then confirms. \\
    \textbf{Commit} & The coordinator then publishes the manifest naming every shard. \\
  \end{tabular}}
  \mlsyscredit{Fault Tolerance, \S7.8.5 (coordinated checkpointing, six-step protocol, two-phase commit: Gray 1978; domino effect: Elnozahy et al. 2002).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Saving at the Young-Daly interval helps only if every worker's shard belongs to the same step. When workers save without coordinating, rolling failed worker W1 back invalidates W2 and W3 after they already consumed W1's later messages, triggering cascading rollbacks known as the domino effect (Elnozahy et al., 2002). Coordinated checkpointing avoids that cascade by saving at synchronous step boundaries to capture a consistent cut, which is required under Lecture 6's sharded ZeRO and FSDP training where no rank holds the full model. To make each cut all-or-nothing, distributed saves adapt two-phase commit, formalized by Gray (1978) so all participants either commit or abort together. In Prepare, every rank writes an immutable shard and checksum digest, then confirms. In Commit, the coordinator publishes the manifest naming all shards. If the coordinator dies beforehand, restore ignores orphaned shards and reloads the prior generation.

    \notecue{Watch for}{A directory full of valid-looking shard files without a committed manifest is never a valid checkpoint.}

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide inspects PyTorch Distributed Checkpoint executing this Prepare and Commit ordering in code.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{An epoch fence stops a replaced worker from writing}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{fencing_step}{3}\\[0.08cm]
  {\small\uncover<3->{A lease decides ownership. The fence at the store enforces it.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Fault Tolerance, \S7.9.2 and figure 15 (interactive Explore figure: Epoch Fencing); fencing tokens: Kleppmann 2016.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Replacing an unresponsive worker creates a split-brain hazard (two workers claiming ownership at once) if the old worker was merely paused rather than dead. As Kleppmann (2016) showed for distributed leases, expiring a lease (a time-bounded ownership grant) changes only the coordinator's decision, so storage must enforce strictly increasing (monotonic) fencing tokens (\S7.9.2). In membership epoch 7 (a group counter distinct from a data epoch over the dataset), the old worker writes shards stamped with token 7 before pausing in a long garbage-collection (GC) stall or a network partition cutting it off from the cluster. When its lease expires, the coordinator starts epoch 8 and raises the storage server's fence to $\ge 8$ before allowing the replacement to write with token 8. When the old worker wakes and submits stale token 7, storage rejects it directly, pairing fencing with idempotent retries (safe to repeat) under bounded deadlines.

\begin{frame}{Replicas buy availability, and state decides the failover}
  \centering

      {\scriptsize\textcolor{midgray}{Independent failure zones (Ashburn, VA).}\par}
    \end{column}

  \vspace{0.05cm}
  {\footnotesize\uncover<2->{43 GB: the KV cache, a conversation's attention keys and values.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Fault Tolerance, equation 12, \S7.11, footnote 19 (Llama-family 70B, 128K context, BF16). Photo: Theodore Christopher, CC0, Wikimedia Commons.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Because a live user cannot wait minutes for a checkpoint reload, serving inverts Section 1's series math by composing replicas in parallel, staying up if any replica lives so $A = 1 - (1 - a)^r$ (equation 12). Independent 99\% replicas cut annual downtime from 3.65 days at $r=1$ to 52.6 minutes at $r=2$ and 31.5 seconds at $r=3$, provided replicas span separate power and switch domains such as distinct availability zones in Ashburn, Virginia. Failover speed then hinges on request state. Stateless classifiers retry immediately on another replica, whereas an LLM conversation holds a derived KV cache of about 43 GB for a 70B model at a 128K-token context length in BF16 (Lecture 10 derives this KV-cache sizing). Losing that replica forces either seconds to minutes of recomputation from the durable transcript or advance replication to hold the latency budget.

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item A job's failure interval is MTTF over $N$ at best; name the basis, and expect measured clusters to fail sooner.
    \item Persistence, manifestation, and scope choose the response; liveness checks miss slow and wrong.
    \item Save every $\sqrt{2\delta M}$, and count only a manifest-published, consistent cut as saved.
    \item Each failure costs detection, restart, load, and warmup; fencing and elastic invariants keep recovery correct.
    \item Serving buys availability with replicas, sizes failover by state, and degrades by a validated ladder.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    Five principles capture fault tolerance at fleet scale. Name the MTBF basis and expect measured clusters to fail faster than $\text{MTTF}/N$, classify faults by persistence, manifestation, and scope because liveness misses slow and wrong, and save at $\sqrt{2\delta M}$ with manifest-last commits. Budget full recovery with epoch fences and elastic invariants, and protect serving through replicas, state-sized failover, and validated degradation ladders.

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide poses three open reliability questions for next-generation fleets.}
  }
\end{frame}
