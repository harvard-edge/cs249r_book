\begin{frame}{Each halving of the reducible loss costs a fixed multiple}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.36\textwidth}
      \small
      $L(R) = A\,R^{-\alpha} + L_{\infty}$\\[0.25cm]
      {\footnotesize $R$: parameters, tokens, or compute\\
      $\alpha$: scaling exponent\\
      $L_{\infty}$: irreducible floor}\\[0.3cm]
      \uncover<2->{Halving $A R^{-\alpha}$ needs $2^{1/\alpha}$ times $R$.\\[0.1cm]
      $\alpha = 0.34$: \textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{7.7$\times$}} the parameters.\\[0.2cm]
      \footnotesize Scaling laws are the fleet's demand curve.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.62\textwidth}
      \centering\uncover<2->{\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{powerlaw.pdf}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Introduction, \S1.3.3, theorem 1.1. Curve: parameter term of Hoffmann et al. 2022, eq.~10 ($A = 406.4$, $\alpha = 0.34$), via MLSysIM; 7.7$\times$ derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Organizations keep buying larger fleets because loss falls predictably with scale, though each equal gain grows steeper in price. The power law $L(R) = A R^{-\alpha} + L_{\infty}$ models loss falling in resource $R$ (parameters, tokens, or compute) toward an irreducible floor $L_{\infty}$ that extra scale cannot remove. In DeepMind's Chinchilla scaling study, which showed that proportional parameter and token scaling outperforms larger undertrained models (Hoffmann et al., 2022), the parameter curve has $\alpha = 0.34$. Halving the reducible term $A R^{-\alpha}$ always multiplies $R$ by $2^{1/\alpha}$, so at $\alpha = 0.34$ each halving requires $7.7\times$ as many parameters (derived), traced by the orange staircase steps. If $\alpha$ were 0.1, one halving would cost $2^{10} = 1{,}024\times$ the resource. Predictable returns make larger fleets worth buying, yet their steep price makes allocation critical.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The book denotes the irreducible floor as $C_L$, a fit constant unrelated to the C$^3$ axes. Lower pretraining loss also does not automatically guarantee downstream task gains (\S1.3.4).}

    \notecue{Next}{Because each order of magnitude is expensive, how should a team split a fixed compute budget between parameters and tokens?}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Leaving the node moves the limiting wall to the network}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{discontinuity_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small This shift in the limiting constraint is the scale moment.}
  \mlsyscredit{Redrawn after Introduction, \S1.1, figure 3. Blue: compute ($C_1$); amber: communication ($C_2$); violet: coordination ($C_3$).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Leaving an eight-GPU H100 server drops per-accelerator bandwidth from 450 GB/s inside over NVLink to 50 GB/s out through one newer 400 Gb/s NDR-generation InfiniBand port, a $9\times$ cliff (and an $18\times$ drop to the hook's 25 GB/s HDR link). That cliff changes which physical wall limits performance. On one node, the usual ceiling is the memory wall, because local HBM must feed on-chip compute fast enough with no remote peers to wait on. Across a fleet joined by a shared fabric and scheduler, three shifts arrive together. The fabric becomes the limiting wall, bounded by speed-of-light latency and bisection bandwidth (capacity across the narrowest equal split of the fleet). Slow nodes delay peers, and hardware faults become routine. Together these shifts form the scale moment (\S1.1), while serving billions adds facility energy as a third wall. Adding workers alone cannot widen a saturated bisection cut (Lecture 3).

    \notecue{Next}{Why does a single slow node stall every peer here when classic batch systems tolerate slow tasks?}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A synchronous step waits for its slowest worker}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{coupling_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<3->{\small One worker 10\% slow on the critical path slows the whole job by nearly 10\%.}\\[0.02cm]
  {\scriptsize\textcolor{midgray}{MapReduce: a job split into independent tasks that can be retried one at a time.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Redrawn after Introduction, \S1.2.1, figure 5 (Dean and Ghemawat 2004; interactive Explore figure: Traditional vs.\ ML Fleet).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Independent-task batch frameworks such as MapReduce (Dean and Ghemawat, 2004) split input into map tasks and combine outputs in reduce tasks, so a failed or slow map task can retry alone while peer map tasks finish. Synchronous data-parallel training instead gives each GPU a model replica and a batch slice, requiring every worker to meet at an AllReduce barrier before updating weights. When one slow worker, or straggler (GPU 2), lags behind, GPUs 0 and 1 sit idle waiting for it. Under this synchronous tight coupling, one worker running 10\% slow on the critical path (the dependent chain setting step length) slows all 1,000 workers by nearly 10\%, and recovery must restore consistent model, optimizer, and data state together (Lecture 6).

    \notecue{Watch for}{MapReduce's speculative re-execution, which runs a backup copy of a slow task on a spare node, does not transfer cleanly to synchronous steps because every worker must exchange exact tensors at the barrier.}

    \notecue{Next}{When thousands of machines march in lockstep like this, what happens if we treat the entire data center as one computer?}
  }
\end{frame}

\section{Fleet Law}

\begin{frame}{The C$^3$ taxonomy splits a step by the evidence it leaves}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=4.8cm,keepaspectratio]{c3_step}{3}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<3->{\small After the cluster doubled, step time grew by half. Which counters rose?}
  \mlsyscredit{Redrawn after Introduction, \S1.5.3, figure 14, with the evidence of \S1.5 (definition 1.2).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    An idle accelerator does not announce its bottleneck, so Lecture 0's C$^3$ taxonomy separates causes by measurable counters (\S1.5). Compute ($C_1$) covers intra-node kernels, HBM, and local data paths, tracked by kernel time, model FLOPs utilization (MFU, the fraction of peak compute spent on model math), and memory-bandwidth counters. Communication ($C_2$) covers inter-worker transfers, tracked by link utilization and collective timing (multi-worker exchanges such as AllReduce, developed in Lecture 5). Coordination ($C_3$) covers barriers, stragglers, scheduling, and recovery, tracked by barrier wait time and restart records. If doubling a cluster lengthens a step while kernel time falls, $C_1$ is ruled out and diagnosis turns to $C_2$ or $C_3$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Coordination ($C_3$) is not storage, because storage supports all three axes. Fixing the limiting axis often shifts time onto another axis, so re-measure after every change.}

    \notecue{Next}{Trace where $C_1$, $C_2$, and $C_3$ appear line by line inside real PyTorch distributed training code.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{One PyTorch step computes, then waits on AllReduce}

  \alert<2->{output = model(data)}

\end{semiverbatim}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.37\textwidth}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Each C$^3$ axis maps directly onto specific lines of a training loop, shown here in an excerpt from PyTorch's distributed tutorial. The forward pass, loss, and \texttt{loss.backward()} run locally on each worker's batch slice (each worker is called a rank), which is Compute ($C_1$). Next, \texttt{average\_gradients} calls \texttt{dist.all\_reduce} once per parameter tensor, generating Communication ($C_2$). Because \texttt{all\_reduce} cannot return until every rank joins, waiting on any slow rank enters Coordination ($C_3$). \mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  Those four pieces assemble into the Fleet Law previewed in Lecture 0 (\S1.5.4, equation 3). First, $\Tcomp / N$ ($C_1$) is local compute divided across $N$ devices, the only term that shrinks as the fleet grows. Second, $\Tcomm(N)$ ($C_2$) is wire time for gradients, activations, and shards, which rarely shrinks and often grows with $N$. Third, $\Tsync(N)$ ($C_3$) captures barrier waits, stragglers, scheduling, and failure recovery. Fourth, subtracting $\Tover$ credits only communication or coordination hidden behind concurrent compute. Unlike single-machine iron-law time (Lecture 0 and Appendix A), which sums data over bandwidth, operations over rate, and fixed latency, the Fleet Law explicitly prices cross-node wire and waiting costs.

  \notecue{Watch for}{The Fleet Law is an accounting decomposition for a stated workload and configuration. Weak scaling (growing work alongside $N$) or algorithm changes alter the terms, and subtract only overlap confirmed on a profiler trace.}

  \notecue{Next}{Test the equation by doubling $N$ and watching which bars shrink and which do not.}
}
\mlsysfocus{The Fleet Law}{%
$\Tstep(N) = \underbrace{\dfrac{\Tcomp}{N}}_{C_1} + \underbrace{\Tcomm(N)}_{C_2} + \underbrace{\Tsync(N)}_{C_3} - \underbrace{\Tover}_{\text{hidden}}$%
}

On statement (2), when overhead fills 40\% of the scaled step, the remaining compute fraction is $f = 0.6$, so a $3\times$ plan yields $3 \times 0.6 = 1.8\times$ speedup. A tempting answer of true on (2) assumes linear scaling without checking overhead. Scaling laws price total work; the Fleet Law prices wall-clock time.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{Amdahl's 1967 strong-scaling bound caps speedup only when an unscaled serial fraction stays fixed; in a fleet, changing network topology (how switches wire nodes, Lecture 3), partitioning, or overlap can shift the non-scaling terms (Systems Perspective 1.1).}

    \notecue{Teach}{Poll both statements before revealing the answers.}

    \notecue{Next}{Published efficiency numbers can still look contradictory; see why four different figures answer four different questions.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Four efficiency numbers answer four different questions}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.2cm,keepaspectratio]{measures_step}{2}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Every efficiency claim names its measure and its interval.}
  \mlsyscredit{Introduction, \S1.5.4: Patterson et al. 2021 (GPT-3); Narayanan et al. 2021 (Megatron-LM). Dubey et al. 2024, Table 4 (41\% MFU) and \S3.3.4 (more than 90\%).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Efficiency quotes mislead whenever the denominator or time window goes unnamed. Three arithmetic measures illustrate the difference. GPT-3 averaged roughly 20\% of peak across the run, including idle time (Patterson et al., 2021). In Narayanan et al.'s (2021) Megatron-LM study on 3,072 A100s, 52\% of peak counted all executed operations including recomputed activations (discarded values redone during backward passes to save memory). Llama 3 reached 41\% MFU on 16,384 H100s counting only model FLOPs (Dubey et al., 2024), yet the same run also reported over 90\% effective training time, a goodput measure of time not lost to crashes and restarts. Google's ML productivity goodput extends that idea to the fraction of fleet capacity doing useful work across scheduling, runtime, and program losses (Lecture 9).

    \notecue{Watch for}{Test any bare ``95\% efficiency'' claim by asking what denominator it uses and what time interval it covers.}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item Scaling laws price each gain as a fixed multiple, and most of the compute growth came from more accelerators.
    \item Leaving the node trades memory limits for a 9$\times$ slower link and a shared step.
    \item Bytes per local FLOP against the fabric balance decides whether the network sets the step.
    \item More devices shrink only $\Tcomp/N$; name the term a fix shortens.
    \item A job needing all $N$ parts has availability $a^N$, so recovery replaces prevention.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Together, these five rules answer the driving question across all five sections, linking scaling laws and accelerator-hour growth, the $9\times$ cross-node cliff and synchronous tight coupling, communication intensity against fabric balance, the Fleet Law rule that $N$ shrinks only $\Tcomp/N$, and the $a^N$ reliability gap.
