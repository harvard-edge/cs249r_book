\begin{frame}{A synchronous step waits for its slowest worker}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{coupling_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small One worker 10\% slow on the critical path slows the whole job by nearly 10\%.}\\[0.02cm]
  {\scriptsize\textcolor{midgray}{MapReduce: a job split into independent tasks that can be retried one at a time.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Redrawn after Introduction, \S1.2.1, figure 5 (Dean and Ghemawat 2004).}
  \note{[2.5 min] NARRATE: Step 1: MapReduce. Each map task is independent; when map 3 fails, the scheduler re-runs it alone and the other maps finish. Step 2: synchronous data-parallel training. Every worker holds a copy of the model and processes a different slice of the batch, and the AllReduce (a collective that combines every worker's gradient and returns the sum to all) needs every gradient before the shared update. GPU 2 is slow, so GPUs 0 and 1 wait. This is synchronous tight coupling: one persistent slowdown becomes a step penalty for all, and recovery must restore consistent model, optimizer, and data state on every worker, not re-run one task. ENGAGE: one of 1,000 workers runs 10 percent slow. What happens to a MapReduce job, and to a training job? (Expected: MapReduce loses little; training slows by nearly 10 percent.) WARN: MapReduce's fix, re-running a lagging task on a spare node, does not transfer. LINK: Lecture 6 owns stragglers and synchronization models. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Vote: a worker goes silent. What now?}
  \mlsysactivity{Vote}{2.5 min}
  \normalsize
  \choice{A}{Wait: keep one current model, and halt the step.}\\[0.25cm]
  \choice{B}{Proceed without it: keep going on stale or divergent state.}\\[0.25cm]
  \choice{C}{Both: pick consistency and availability at once.}\\[0.4cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small\textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{The answer is A or B.}} Synchronous training picks A; asynchronous and federated training pick B. Under a partition, the CAP theorem rules out C.}
  \mlsyscredit{Introduction, \S1.4.2 (Brewer 2000; Gilbert and Lynch 2002).}
  \note{[2.5 min] ENGAGE: vote, one minute with a neighbor, vote again. Step 2: under a network partition, an asynchronous distributed system cannot guarantee both atomic consistency and availability (the CAP theorem; Brewer's conjecture, proved by Gilbert and Lynch). Training is not a replicated database, and gradient freshness is not the theorem's consistency, but training faces the same forced choice. Synchronous training keeps one shared current state, so the step halts until the worker returns or is replaced. Asynchronous training keeps going and applies updates computed on stale copies of the model. Federated learning lets devices train local copies that diverge between rounds. WARN: partition tolerance is not a setting an engineer can switch off; it names the network condition under which the choice applies. LINK: Lecture 6 compares synchronous and asynchronous training; Lecture 8 meets the same choice when it schedules. FLEX: [OPTIONAL]. Land the segment aloud: leaving the node buys capacity and charges bandwidth, waiting, and consistency.}
\end{frame}

        16-bit or compressed & $\Tcomm$: fewer bytes \\
        Overlap & $\Tover$: hides time \\
        Faster fabric & $\Tcomm$: per byte \\
        Checkpoint and restart & $\Tsync$: failures \\\bottomrule
      \end{tabular}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Introduction, Napkin Math 1.1 and \S1.5.4. Photo: ChrisDag, CC BY 2.0, Wikimedia Commons.}
  \note{[3 min] NARRATE: back to the hook. Step 1: retrieval, 90 seconds, alone. Step 2: read the table. Faster accelerators shorten only the compute term, worth 2.1 percent here. Fewer bytes (16-bit gradients, compression, a partition that keeps heavy traffic inside the node) lower communication intensity and the communication term. Overlap leaves the bytes and hides time behind compute, and only while compute remains. A faster fabric moves the balance point. Checkpointing and automatic restart bound what each failure costs in the coordination term. Answer the hook's question: the step is 29.2 s because one worker's 700 GB crosses a link 9 times slower than the node's, with nothing hidden; the scaling laws are why the job is that large, and the reliability gap is why it will also stop every few hours. ENGAGE: which two fixes would you try first? (Expected: overlap and fewer bytes; the chip upgrade last.) FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}
