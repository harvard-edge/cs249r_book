\begin{frame}{Each halving of the reducible loss costs a fixed multiple}

      \footnotesize Scaling laws are the fleet's demand curve.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.62\textwidth}

    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Introduction, \S1.3.3, theorem 1.1. Curve: parameter term of Hoffmann et al. 2022, eq.~10 ($A = 406.4$, $\alpha = 0.34$), via MLSysIM; 7.7$\times$ derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    First, the equation $L(R) = A R^{-\alpha} + L_{\infty}$ shows loss falling as a power law in resource $R$ (parameters, tokens, or compute) toward an irreducible floor $L_{\infty}$ that no extra scale removes. Then, step 2 reveals both the multiplier and the plotted curve (Hoffmann et al., 2022, parameter exponent $\alpha = 0.34$). Halving the reducible term $A R^{-\alpha}$ always multiplies $R$ by $2^{1/\alpha}$; at $\alpha = 0.34$, every halving requires $7.7\times$ as many parameters (derived), marked by the orange staircase steps. If $\alpha$ were 0.1, one halving would cost $2^{10} = 1{,}024\times$ the resource. Because gains remain predictable, organizations keep purchasing the next order of magnitude of fleet capacity.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The book denotes the irreducible floor as $C_L$, a fit constant unrelated to the C$^3$ axes. Also remember that lower pretraining loss does not automatically guarantee downstream task gains (\S1.3.4).}

\begin{frame}{Leaving the node moves the limiting wall to the network}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{discontinuity_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small This shift in the limiting constraint is the scale moment.}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    First, step 1 shows a single node where the usual ceiling is the memory wall: HBM must feed on-chip compute fast enough, with no remote peers to wait on. Then, step 2 expands to a multi-node fleet connected through a shared fabric and managed by a scheduler. Three shifts occur simultaneously: the network fabric becomes the limiting wall (bounded by speed-of-light latency and bisection bandwidth, the capacity across the narrowest equal split of the fleet), any slow node delays peers, and hardware faults become routine. This qualitative shift is the scale moment (\S1.1), and serving billions of users adds energy as a third wall. Simply adding workers cannot raise a saturated bisection cut (Lecture 3).

\begin{frame}{A synchronous step waits for its slowest worker}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{coupling_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<3->{\small One worker 10\% slow on the critical path slows the whole job by nearly 10\%.}\\[0.02cm]
  {\scriptsize\textcolor{midgray}{MapReduce: a job split into independent tasks that can be retried one at a time.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Redrawn after Introduction, \S1.2.1, figure 5 (Dean and Ghemawat 2004; interactive Explore figure: Traditional vs.\ ML Fleet).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    First, step 1 shows MapReduce (Dean and Ghemawat, 2004): independent map tasks run without barriers, so a failed or slow task simply retries while peers finish. Second, step 2 depicts balanced synchronous data-parallel training: every GPU holds a model replica, processes its batch slice, and joins an AllReduce barrier before updating weights. Finally, step 3 introduces one straggler (GPU 2): GPUs 0 and 1 sit idle in gray wait blocks until GPU 2 arrives. Under synchronous tight coupling, one worker running 10\% slow on the critical path slows all 1,000 workers by nearly 10\%, and failure recovery must restore consistent model, optimizer, and data state together (Lecture 6).

    \notecue{Watch for}{MapReduce's speculative re-execution on a spare node does not transfer cleanly to synchronous gradient steps because every worker must exchange exact tensors at the barrier.}

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  Read the Fleet Law (\S1.5.4, equation 3) term by term alongside the color-coded braces. First, $\Tcomp / N$ ($C_1$) is the local compute work divided across $N$ devices, the only term that shrinks as the fleet grows. Second, $\Tcomm(N)$ ($C_2$) is wire time for gradients, activations, and shards, which rarely shrinks and often grows with $N$. Third, $\Tsync(N)$ ($C_3$) captures barrier waits, stragglers, scheduling, and failure recovery. Fourth, subtracting $\Tover$ credits only the communication or coordination time genuinely hidden behind concurrent compute. Contrast this with single-machine iron-law time (data over bandwidth plus operations over rate plus latency, Appendix A).

  \notecue{Watch for}{The Fleet Law is an accounting decomposition for a stated workload and configuration; weak scaling or algorithm changes alter the terms, and engineers should subtract only overlap confirmed on a trace.}

    \end{column}

      \centering\only<2|handout:0>{\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{an_step1.pdf}}%
      \only<3-|handout:1>{\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{an_step2.pdf}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Introduction, \S1.4.1, equation 2 and figure 13 (illustrative 99.9\% per node, independent failures).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    When a synchronous job requires every one of $N$ independent nodes to be online simultaneously, joint availability equals $A_{\text{job}} = a^N$ (\S1.4.1, equation 2). First, step 2 reveals choice C (37\%): at $a = 0.999$ per node, $0.999^{1{,}000} = 0.368$, and at 10,000 nodes it collapses to 0.0045\% (choice D). Then, step 3 adds another nine ($a = 0.9999$, just 52.6 minutes down per year): the curve shifts $10\times$ right, giving 36.8\% at 10,000 nodes, so the cliff still arrives. This exponential drop is the reliability gap: component reliability cannot guarantee job reliability, so automated recovery must replace failure prevention.

    \notecue{Check}{Vote on A through D before revealing step 2. Choice C (37\%) holds because multiplying 0.999 by itself 1,000 times decays exponentially ($e^{-1} \approx 0.368$). Choice A (99.9\%) is the classic single-node fallacy that forgets faults compound in series across all $N$ required nodes.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{Availability is a steady-state uptime fraction, not a failure count; interruption frequency requires the MTTF/$N$ failure-rate model developed in Lecture 7.}

  \mlsyscredit{Introduction, Napkin Math 1.1 and \S1.5.4. Photo: ChrisDag, CC BY 2.0, Wikimedia Commons.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    Returning to the 29.2 s opening hook (only 4.1\% compute), step 2 maps five concrete interventions to the exact Fleet Law term each changes. Doubling chip speed affects only $\Tcomp/N$, saving 2.1\%. 16-bit or compressed gradients lower communication intensity and shrink $\Tcomm$. Overlap grows $\Tover$ while compute remains to hide behind. A faster fabric cuts $\Tcomm$ per byte, shifting the balance point. Checkpointing and automatic restart bound failure losses in $\Tsync$. In short, the hook took 29.2 s because 700 GB crossed a 25 GB/s HDR link ($18\times$ slower than 450 GB/s NVLink, whereas a 50 GB/s NDR port is $9\times$ slower) with zero overlap, while scaling laws drove the job's size and the reliability gap guarantees periodic restarts.

    \notecue{Check}{Give 90 seconds of solo retrieval before revealing step 2, then ask which two fixes to deploy first. Overlap and fewer wire bytes yield the largest immediate gain because $\Tcomm$ dominates; buying $2\times$ faster chips is the worst first move because $\Tcomp/N$ is only 4.1\% of the step.}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item Scaling laws price each gain as a fixed multiple, and most of the compute growth came from more accelerators.
    \item Leaving the node trades memory limits for a 9$\times$ slower link and a shared step.
    \item Bytes per local FLOP against the fabric balance decides whether the network sets the step.
    \item More devices shrink only $\Tcomp/N$; name the term a fix shortens.
    \item A job needing all $N$ parts has availability $a^N$, so recovery replaces prevention.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
