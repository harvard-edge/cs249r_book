\begin{frame}{Four walls limit performance at four physical scales}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{walls_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{Compute Infrastructure, \S2.1 (figure 1), \S2.3, \S2.5.1, \S2.6, \S2.7.1. Pod failure interval: 10,000 GPUs, per-GPU MTTF 50,000 h, independent failures.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    These four scales give physical hardware form to Lecture 1's memory, network, energy, and reliability limits. First, at the accelerator scale, 3.35 TB/s of HBM feeds arithmetic cores that can consume far more, setting the memory wall. Second, at the node scale, recalling Lecture 1's $9\times$ cliff, intra-node NVLink delivers 450 GB/s per direction per GPU while inter-node InfiniBand drops to 50 GB/s, setting the communication wall. Third, at the rack scale, four eight-GPU servers draw 33.5 kW versus 12 kW for a standard enterprise rack, setting the power wall. Fourth, at the pod scale, 10,000 GPUs at a 50,000-hour per-GPU MTTF lose a GPU roughly every 5 hours, setting the reliability wall. Relieving one wall by adding GPUs adds links, watts, and failing parts, so every scale exposes the next ceiling.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Which wall limits a run depends on the workload. Hardware sets the ceilings, and workload intensity selects the active limit.}

\begin{frame}{HBM sits beside the die and gives about 17$\times$ DDR5's bandwidth}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{amd-fiji-hbm-package.jpg}\\[0.1cm]

    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \small
      \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
        \toprule
        & DDR5 (host) & HBM3 (H100) \\
        \midrule
        Bandwidth & $\sim$200 GB/s & \textbf{3.35 TB/s} \\
        Interface & 64-bit & 1,024-bit per stack \\
        Energy & $\sim$20 pJ/bit & $\sim$4 pJ/bit \\
        Price & $\sim$\$3/GB & \$10 to \$15/GB \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}\\[0.3cm]
      {\normalsize A 1,024-bit interface per stack supplies the bandwidth at a moderate clock.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: C. Spille/pcgameshardware.de, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons. Table: Compute Infrastructure, \S2.3.1 (table 5); energy per bit: Horowitz 2014; O'Connor et al. 2017 (HBM2).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    HBM achieves roughly $17\times$ host DDR5's bandwidth by widening and shortening the wire path. Jointly developed by AMD and SK Hynix for graphics cards and standardized by JEDEC in 2013, HBM appears here on a 2015 AMD Fiji package with four stacks beside the GPU die on a silicon interposer, a thin on-package wiring layer replacing motherboard traces. Each HBM3 stack exposes a 1,024-bit bus, $16\times$ wider than a 64-bit DDR5 channel, giving an H100 3.35 TB/s versus DDR5's about 200 GB/s. Energy drops to about one-fifth per bit (about 4 picojoules per bit, pJ/bit, versus about 20 pJ/bit), while cost rises roughly $4\times$ (\$10 to \$15/GB versus about \$3/GB). Yet reading one FP16 operand from HBM still costs about 64 pJ against about 1.5 pJ for a 16-bit multiply-add, a $43\times$ ratio, making on-chip data reuse essential.

    \notecue{Watch for}{HBM pushes back the memory wall without eliminating it, because peak arithmetic grew faster than HBM bandwidth from V100 to H100.}

    \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip this slide and move directly to the capacity-versus-bandwidth test.}

    \notecue{Next}{With HBM's speed and cost in view, test memory capacity and memory bandwidth independently.}

\begin{frame}{Capacity decides whether it fits; bandwidth decides how fast}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{two_tests_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Compute Infrastructure, \S2.3.1: Llama-3 70B, FP16 weights, batch-1 decode; floor = weight bytes / HBM bandwidth on one accelerator-equivalent path.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Because HBM is scarce and costly, hardware selection runs two separate checks on Llama-3 70B. Capacity comes first. Its 141.2 GB of FP16 weights exceeds one H100's 80 GB, requiring at least two GPUs before counting the key-value (KV) cache (stored attention state from earlier tokens, developed in Lecture 10) or temporary scratch workspace. Bandwidth comes next, testing batch-1 decode on one accelerator-equivalent path. Streaming 141.2 GB sets a latency floor of 69.2 ms on an A100 at 2.04 TB/s, 42.1 ms on an H100 at 3.35 TB/s, and 17.7 ms on NVIDIA's 2024 Blackwell B200 at 8 TB/s. Doubling peak FP16 FLOP/s without raising HBM bandwidth leaves this decode floor unchanged.

    \notecue{Watch for}{A model that fits in HBM yet misses its latency target fails deployment just as surely as an out-of-memory crash.}

\begin{frame}{Decode sits at 1 FLOP/byte, far left of the H100 ridge at 295}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{roofline_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Attainable FLOP/s $=\min(R_{\text{peak}},\ \text{BW}\times I)$;\quad $I$ = FLOP per byte read from HBM}
  \mlsyscredit{H100: 989 TFLOP/s dense FP16, 3.35 TB/s. Intensities: Compute Infrastructure, \S2.3.2 (table 7, Example 2.2), idealized weight-traffic model. Roofline: Williams et al. 2009; Appendix A.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    To generalize that bandwidth test across batch sizes and training, Williams, Waterman, and Patterson's 2009 roofline model (recapped in textbook Appendix A) plots attainable throughput as $\min(R_{\text{peak}},\ \text{BW} \times I)$. On an H100, the 989 TFLOP/s dense FP16 ceiling meets the 3.35 TB/s HBM slope at a ridge point of 295 FLOP/byte ($989 / 3.35$). Under this idealized weight-only model, reusing each fetched weight across $B$ tokens multiplies arithmetic intensity $I$ by $B$. Batch-1 decode sits at $I = 1$ FLOP/byte, reaching 3.35 TFLOP/s, or 0.34\% of peak. Batching to 64 requests reaches 64 FLOP/byte and roughly 22\% of peak while staying left of the ridge. Training with 2,048 tokens per GPU lands past 295 on the flat arithmetic roof. Bandwidth upgrades help left of 295; compute upgrades help to its right.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The roofline sets an idealized upper bound for comparing hardware upgrades rather than a forecast of achieved MFU, covered in Lecture 9.}

    \notecue{Next}{Examine how specialized silicon trades generality for a higher arithmetic share of the die.}
  }
\end{frame}

    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Cutting the generality tax (silicon area and energy spent on control and caches rather than math) raises arithmetic die share. Within five years of 2015, Transformers displaced convolutional neural networks (CNNs), showing the risk of betting on operator stability.

    Beyond NVSwitch, paths to host memory and the network cross PCIe trees rooted at two CPU sockets, splitting the eight-GPU server into two non-uniform memory access (NUMA) domains where each socket reaches local dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) fastest. First, intra-node GPU collectives cross NVSwitch at 450 GB/s without touching PCIe. Second, local data ingest streams from DRAM 0 through CPU 0 and PCIe switch 0 into GPU G0. Third, GPUDirect remote direct memory access (RDMA) moves gradients directly between NIC N0 and GPU G0 across PCIe switch 0, bypassing host CPU and DRAM (Lecture 3). Fourth, reading DRAM 1 or binding remote NIC N4 for G0 forces traffic across the inter-socket link (labeled UPI) between the two CPUs, adding latency and contention. Pinning each data-loading worker (data loader), DRAM bank, NIC, and GPU to one CPU socket avoids that penalty.

    \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip this slide and note that data loaders, NICs, and GPUs should be pinned to matching CPU sockets.}
