\begin{frame}{Four walls limit performance at four physical scales}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{walls_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{Compute Infrastructure, \S2.1 (figure 1), \S2.3, \S2.5.1, \S2.6, \S2.7.1. Pod failure interval: 10,000 GPUs, per-GPU MTTF 50,000 h, independent failures.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    These four scales give physical hardware form to Lecture 1's memory, network, energy, and reliability limits. First, at the accelerator scale, 3.35 TB/s of HBM feeds arithmetic cores that can consume far more, setting the memory wall. Second, at the node scale, recalling Lecture 1's $9\times$ cliff, intra-node NVLink delivers 450 GB/s per direction per GPU while inter-node InfiniBand drops to 50 GB/s, setting the communication wall. Third, at the rack scale, four eight-GPU servers draw 33.5 kW versus 12 kW for a standard enterprise rack, setting the power wall. Fourth, at the pod scale, 10,000 GPUs at a 50,000-hour per-GPU MTTF lose a GPU roughly every 5 hours, setting the reliability wall. Relieving one wall by adding GPUs adds links, watts, and failing parts, so every scale exposes the next ceiling.

\begin{frame}{HBM sits beside the die and gives about 17$\times$ DDR5's bandwidth}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{amd-fiji-hbm-package.jpg}\\[0.1cm]

    \end{column}

        & DDR5 (host) & HBM3 (H100) \\
        \midrule

        Energy & $\sim$20 pJ/bit & $\sim$4 pJ/bit \\
        Price & $\sim$\$3/GB & \$10 to \$15/GB \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}\\[0.3cm]
      {\normalsize A 1,024-bit interface per stack supplies the bandwidth at a moderate clock.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: C. Spille/pcgameshardware.de, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons. Table: Compute Infrastructure, \S2.3.1 (table 5); energy per bit: Horowitz 2014; O'Connor et al. 2017 (HBM2).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    HBM achieves $17\times$ host DDR5's bandwidth by widening and shortening the wire path. Standardized in 2013 by AMD and SK Hynix for graphics cards, HBM appears here on a 2015 AMD Fiji package with four stacks beside the GPU die on a silicon interposer, a thin on-package wiring layer replacing long motherboard traces. Each HBM3 stack exposes a 1,024-bit bus, $16\times$ wider than a 64-bit DDR5 channel, giving an H100 3.35 TB/s versus DDR5's about 200 GB/s. Energy drops to about one-fifth per bit (about 4 picojoules per bit, or pJ/bit, versus about 20 pJ/bit), while cost rises roughly $4\times$ (\$10 to \$15/GB versus about \$3/GB). Yet reading one FP16 operand from HBM still costs about 64 pJ against about 1.5 pJ for a 16-bit multiply-add, a $43\times$ ratio, forcing on-chip data reuse.

    \notecue{Watch for}{HBM pushes back the memory wall without eliminating it, because peak arithmetic grew faster than HBM bandwidth from V100 to H100.}

\begin{frame}{Capacity decides whether it fits; bandwidth decides how fast}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{two_tests_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Compute Infrastructure, \S2.3.1: Llama-3 70B, FP16 weights, batch-1 decode; floor = weight bytes / HBM bandwidth on one accelerator-equivalent path.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Because HBM is scarce, hardware selection separates capacity from bandwidth into two independent tests on Llama-3 70B. Capacity comes first. Its 141.2 GB of FP16 weights exceeds one H100's 80 GB, requiring at least two GPUs before counting the key-value (KV) cache (stored attention state from earlier tokens, developed in Lecture 10) or temporary scratch workspace. Bandwidth comes next, testing batch-1 decode on one accelerator-equivalent path. Streaming 141.2 GB sets a latency floor of 69.2 ms on an A100 at 2.04 TB/s, 42.1 ms on an H100 at 3.35 TB/s, and 17.7 ms on NVIDIA's 2024 Blackwell B200 at 8 TB/s. Doubling peak FP16 FLOP/s without raising HBM bandwidth leaves this decode floor unchanged.

    \notecue{Watch for}{A model that fits in HBM yet misses its latency target fails deployment just as surely as an out-of-memory crash.}

\begin{frame}{Decode sits at 1 FLOP/byte, far left of the H100 ridge at 295}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{roofline_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Attainable FLOP/s $=\min(R_{\text{peak}},\ \text{BW}\times I)$;\quad $I$ = FLOP per byte read from HBM}
  \mlsyscredit{H100: 989 TFLOP/s dense FP16, 3.35 TB/s. Intensities: Compute Infrastructure, \S2.3.2 (table 7, Example 2.2), idealized weight-traffic model. Roofline: Williams et al. 2009; Appendix A.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    To generalize that bandwidth test across batch sizes and training, the Appendix A roofline serves as a procurement screen. Attainable throughput equals $\min(R_{\text{peak}},\ \text{BW} \times I)$, where the H100's 989 TFLOP/s dense FP16 ceiling meets its 3.35 TB/s HBM slope at a ridge point of 295 FLOP/byte ($989 / 3.35$). First, batch-1 decode sits at $I = 1$ FLOP/byte, reaching only 3.35 TFLOP/s, or 0.34\% of peak. Raising the batch size to 64 reaches roughly 22\% of peak while staying left of the ridge. Training at 2,048 tokens per GPU lands past the ridge on the flat arithmetic roof. Upgrading bandwidth helps workloads left of 295; upgrading FLOP/s helps workloads to the right.

    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Raising the arithmetic roof cuts the generality tax, the silicon spent on control and caches rather than math. Arithmetic die share climbs from 5 to 10\% on CPUs, 50 to 60\% on GPUs, 70 to 80\% on Tensor Processing Units (TPUs), and over 90\% on custom application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs). \begin{frame}{Inside a node, NUMA affinity decides whether bytes cross sockets}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{node_numa_step}{4}\\[0.04cm]
  \uncover<4->{\footnotesize Pin each data loader, DRAM bank, NIC, and GPU to the same CPU socket.}
  \mlsyscredit{Compute Infrastructure, \S2.5.1 (interactive Explore figure: Node PCIe and NUMA Topology).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Beyond NVSwitch, every path to host memory or the network runs through PCIe trees rooted at two host CPU sockets, dividing the eight-GPU server into two non-uniform memory access (NUMA) domains where each socket reaches its local host dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) fastest. First, intra-node GPU collectives cross NVSwitch at 450 GB/s without touching PCIe. Second, local data ingest streams from DRAM 0 through CPU 0 and PCIe switch 0 into GPU G0. Third, GPUDirect remote direct memory access (RDMA) moves gradients directly between NIC N0 and GPU G0 across PCIe switch 0, bypassing host CPU and DRAM (Lecture 3). Fourth, reading DRAM 1 or binding remote NIC N4 for G0 forces traffic across the inter-socket link between the two CPUs, adding latency and contention.

Finally, operational allowances for checkpoints ($\times 1.03$), failures ($\times 1.10$), and maintenance ($\times 1.05$) bring the total to 25.5 hours. Only 21\% ($5.4 / 25.5$) of purchased FP8 peak becomes training progress.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The 0.45, 0.56, and 3/10/5\% figures are stated scenario inputs. Real calendar time stretches further once scheduler queuing and failed experiments are included.}

    Those regimes show why single-number procurement rules fail. Before reading on, mark each of the four claims from \S2.10 true or false, and identify the calculation from today that tests it.

    \notecue{Check}{All four claims are false. Each tempting claim collapses a multi-dimensional limit onto one scalar, namely GPU count, average watts, capacity alone, or raw GPU-hours. (1) Modeled scaling efficiency drops from 0.83 at 1,024 GPUs to 0.56 at 8,192, so $8\times$ the GPUs yield $8 \times 0.56 / 0.83 = 5.4\times$ speed. (2) Synchronized barriers push GPUs to 700 W together, causing thermal throttling (clock slowdown) if cooling sizes only for average draw. (3) 192 GB of slower 2.04 TB/s HBM2e gives a 69.2 ms decode floor versus 21 ms on 80 GB HBM3 with integer (INT8) precision. (4) One H100 delivers $7.9\times$ a V100's FP16 peak, so GPU-hours are not interchangeable across generations.}
