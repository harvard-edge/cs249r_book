  \note{[2.5 min] [OPTIONAL] A real HBM package: the large GPU die in the middle and four memory stacks beside it, all on a silicon interposer, a thin slab of silicon that wires them together over millimeters instead of centimeters of board. ENGAGE: reading one FP16 operand from HBM costs about 64 pJ, and a 16-bit multiply-add costs about 1.5 pJ; what does that ratio, 42 times, tell a chip designer? (Expected: spend silicon on reuse, so each fetched byte feeds many operations.) WARN: HBM moves the memory wall; it does not remove it. Peak arithmetic grew faster than HBM bandwidth from V100 to H100.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Capacity decides whether it fits; bandwidth decides how fast}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{two_tests_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Compute Infrastructure, \S2.3.1: Llama-3 70B, FP16 weights, batch-1 decode; floor = weight bytes / HBM bandwidth on one accelerator-equivalent path.}
  \note{[2.5 min] [CORE] Two separate procurement tests. Step 1, capacity: Llama-3 70B's FP16 weights are 141.2 GB against one H100's 80 GB, so the weights alone need two GPUs, before any key-value cache or workspace. Step 2, bandwidth: at batch 1 every token streams every weight, so the time per token is at least bytes divided by bandwidth: 69.2 ms at A100-class 2.04 TB/s, 42.1 ms at H100-class 3.35 TB/s, 17.6 ms at B200-class 8 TB/s. WARN: a model that fits but misses its latency target is as unusable as one that does not fit. The chapter's pitfall: 192 GB of HBM2e at 2.04 TB/s holds the 141 GB payload at a 69.2 ms floor, while an 80 GB HBM3 part needs INT8 weights to fit and then reaches 21 ms. \end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Decode sits at 1 FLOP/byte, far left of the H100 ridge at 295}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{roofline_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Attainable FLOP/s $=\min(R_{\text{peak}},\ \text{BW}\times I)$;\quad $I$ = FLOP per byte read from HBM}
  \mlsyscredit{H100: 989 TFLOP/s dense FP16, 3.35 TB/s. Intensities: Compute Infrastructure, \S2.3.2 (table 7, Example 2.2), idealized weight-traffic model. Roofline: Williams et al. 2009; Appendix A.}
  \note{[3 min] [CORE] The roofline from the course prerequisites (Appendix A), now used as a purchase screen. Attainable throughput is the lower of two ceilings: the flat peak, 989 TFLOP/s, and the sloped line, bandwidth times arithmetic intensity $I$, the operations done per byte fetched. They meet at the ridge, $989/3.35 = 295$ FLOP per byte. Step 1: batch-1 decode does two FLOPs per two-byte weight, $I = 1$, so the bound is $3.35$ TFLOP/s, 0.34 percent of peak. Step 2: batching 64 requests reuses each weight 64 times; the bound rises to about 22 percent, still left of the ridge. Step 3: training with 2,048 tokens per GPU sharing each weight read crosses the ridge and meets the arithmetic roof. ENGAGE: which upgrade helps each point? (Expected: bandwidth for the two decode points, FLOP/s for training.) WARN: the roofline rules out upgrades that raise a ceiling that is not binding; it does not forecast throughput. A measured kernel can sit well below either line. MFU, the measured share of peak, is Lecture 9's measure.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
