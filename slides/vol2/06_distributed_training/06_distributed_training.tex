\section{Replicas}

\begin{frame}{Data parallelism averages $N$ local gradients into one update}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{dp_flow_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<3->{$g = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k g_k$ equals the gradient of the combined batch when local batches are equal.}}\\[0.1cm]
  {\footnotesize\texttt{model = DistributedDataParallel(model, device\_ids=[rank])} \textcolor{midgray}{\# PyTorch}}
  \mlsyscredit{Distributed Training, \S6.3 and figure 3. Code: PyTorch torch.nn.parallel (BSD license).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Trace data parallelism across three build steps. First, step 1 places a full model copy and a distinct batch slice on each GPU. Second, step 2 runs local forward and backward passes to produce local gradients $g_k$. Third, step 3 executes an AllReduce averaging $g = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k g_k$ so every replica applies the exact same optimizer update and never drifts; PyTorch's \texttt{DistributedDataParallel} hooks launch this AllReduce during backward. Mathematically, synchronous data parallelism is not a different optimizer; it is identical to single-device SGD at global batch $N \times b$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Unweighted averaging equals the combined-batch gradient only when local batch sizes match; variable-length sequences or uneven valid-token counts require weighted reductions, and optimizer state and step counts must stay identical across replicas.}

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide derives scaling efficiency directly from Lecture 1's Fleet Law.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Scaling efficiency is the Fleet Law's overhead over compute}
  \centering
  {\normalsize Fleet Law, per step on $N$ devices:}\\[0.2cm]
  {\Large $\Tstep(N) = \dfrac{\Tcomp}{N} + \Tcomm(N) + \Tsync(N) - \Tover$}\\[0.3cm]
  \uncover<2->{%
  {\normalsize Scaling efficiency, measured against one device:}\\[0.2cm]
  {\Large $\etascale = \dfrac{\Tcomp}{N\,\Tstep(N)} = \dfrac{1}{1 + \dfrac{N\,(\Tcomm + \Tsync - \Tover)}{\Tcomp}}$}}\\[0.2cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<3->{The overhead is multiplied by $N$; the compute is not.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Distributed Training, equations 1 and 2; the Fleet Law: Introduction.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    First, step 1 writes Lecture 1's Fleet Law where only $T_{\text{compute}}/N$ shrinks with $N$. Second, step 2 substitutes it into scaling efficiency $\etascale = T_{\text{compute}} / (N\,T_{\text{step}}(N)) = 1 / [1 + N(T_{\text{comm}} + T_{\text{sync}} - T_{\text{overlap}})/T_{\text{compute}}]$: 1.0 denotes ideal linear scaling, while 0.5 means half of all GPU-hours buy overhead. Third, step 3 highlights the scaling wall in the denominator: exposed per-step overhead is multiplied by $N$ against fixed single-device compute, so constant ring traffic near $2M/\beta$ drives efficiency toward zero as $N$ doubles.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Do not treat this like a static single-node Amdahl serial fraction: even a 1\% single-device residual fills about 84\% of each step at 512 GPUs (\S6.4.2).}

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide plugs our 175B running case into this equation at 1,024 GPUs.}
  }
\end{frame}

\section{Batches}

\begin{frame}{One slow GPU in a hundred delays almost every step at 1,000}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{straggler.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \normalsize
      Each GPU is late on 1\% of steps.\\[0.25cm]
      A step is on time only if \textbf{all} are on time:\\[0.15cm]
      {\Large $0.99^{1000} \approx 0.004\%$}\\[0.3cm]
      {\small A dead worker is cut off by a timeout:}\\[0.05cm]
      {\scriptsize\ttfamily init\_process\_group("nccl",\\[-0.05cm]
      \hspace*{1em}timeout=timedelta(minutes=10))}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Distributed Training, \S6.3.1 (barrier semantics and failure modes); curve derived. Code: PyTorch torch.distributed docs (10 minutes is the NCCL default).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    A straggler computes valid results slowly due to thermal throttling, link congestion, or OS jitter. If each GPU is independently late on 1\% of steps, an entire synchronous step avoids stragglers with probability $0.99^N$: 92\% at 8 GPUs, 37\% at 100, and just 0.004\% at 1,000, because one late GPU stalls the barrier regardless of how fast the other 999 finish. PyTorch's \texttt{init\_process\_group} timeout (10 minutes default for NCCL) aborts truly dead workers yet cannot detect a slow-but-alive GPU (Lecture 7 treats stragglers as gray failures).

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide compares strict barriers (BSP) against relaxed synchronization models (SSP and ASP).}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{BSP waits at a barrier; SSP and ASP trade waiting for staleness}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{sync_step}{3}\\[0.1cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<2->{Staleness: updates between computing a gradient and applying it.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Distributed Training, \S6.3.1, \S6.4.4, figure 5, tables 2 and 4; Ho et al. 2013; Dutta et al. 2018. Timelines schematic.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Trace four workers with W3 (red) acting as a straggler across three synchronization models (\S6.3.1, \S6.4.4). First, step 1 shows Bulk Synchronous Parallel (BSP): all ranks read the same weights and block at the violet barrier (gray idle gaps), preserving exact single-device SGD convergence while letting W3 pace every step. Second, step 2 shows Stale Synchronous Parallel (SSP; Ho et al., 2013): fast workers advance up to $s$ updates ahead before blocking, cutting idle wait at the cost of gradients computed on weights up to $s$ versions stale. Third, step 3 shows Asynchronous Parallel (ASP): removing global barriers eliminates waiting with unbounded staleness. Final production training runs almost always choose BSP for predictable convergence, judging any relaxed protocol strictly by wall-clock time to matched validation quality.

\begin{frame}{Megatron splits columns, then rows, then reduces once}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.2cm}
    \item Replicas reproduce one-device SGD; their AllReduce stays near $2M$, so efficiency hits a wall.
    \item The slowest worker sets each synchronous step, and the critical batch caps useful replicas.
    \item ZeRO and FSDP fit the state by sending up to 1.5$\times$ the bytes and gathering every layer.
    \item Pipelines idle $(p-1)/(m+p-1)$; tensor parallelism needs the NVLink domain.
    \item A plan is one point $DP \times PP \times TP$ that fits memory, batch, and every tier.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    These five points recap one headline per segment: data-parallel scaling walls from constant $2M$ AllReduces, stragglers and $\Bcrit$, ZeRO/FSDP trading memory for up to $1.5\times$ bytes, $(p-1)/(m+p-1)$ bubbles and intra-node NVLink for TP, and joint $DP \times PP \times TP$ feasibility.
