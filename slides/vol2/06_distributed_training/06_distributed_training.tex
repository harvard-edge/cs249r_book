  \mlsysactivity{Retrieval}{1.5 min}

  \uncover<2->{%
  {\Large $\dfrac{2(N-1)}{N}\,M \;\approx\; 2M$: \;699 GB per GPU at $N = 1{,}024$}\\[0.3cm]
  {\normalsize $\approx$ \textbf{14 s} per step on one 50 GB/s port, however many GPUs join.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Ring cost: Collective Communication; Distributed Training, \S6.1 and Napkin Math 6.5.}

    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Before choosing a layout for those 1,024 GPUs, apply Lecture 5's ring AllReduce volume formula to the 175B model's 350 GB of FP16 gradients. Before reading on, write the bytes each of $N$ workers sends for an $M$-byte payload and compute the transfer time at 50 GB/s for $N = 1{,}024$.

    \notecue{Check}{Each GPU sends $\frac{2(N-1)}{N}M \approx 2M$ bytes, totaling 699 GB per GPU at $N = 1{,}024$. \section{Replicas}

\begin{frame}{Data parallelism averages $N$ local gradients into one update}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{dp_flow_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<3->{$g = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k g_k$ equals the gradient of the combined batch when local batches are equal.}}\\[0.1cm]
  {\footnotesize\texttt{model = DistributedDataParallel(model, device\_ids=[rank])} \textcolor{midgray}{\# PyTorch}}
  \mlsyscredit{Distributed Training, \S6.3 and figure 3. Code: PyTorch torch.nn.parallel (BSD license).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Replicating a model across $N$ GPUs preserves single-device optimization when the combined update matches single-device training. Each GPU holds a full model copy, processes a distinct local slice of size $b$ from the global batch, and runs forward and backward passes to compute a local gradient $g_k$. An AllReduce then averages those gradients into $g = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k g_k$, so every replica applies the exact same optimizer update and stays in sync. PyTorch's \texttt{DistributedDataParallel} (introduced in Lecture 1) launches this AllReduce during the backward pass. Mathematically, synchronous data parallelism is therefore not a different optimizer; it reproduces single-device SGD at global batch $N \times b$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Unweighted averaging equals the combined-batch gradient only when local batch sizes match. Variable-length sequences or uneven valid-token counts require weighted reductions, and optimizer state and step counts must stay identical across replicas.}

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide derives scaling efficiency directly from Lecture 1's Fleet Law.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Scaling efficiency is the Fleet Law's overhead over compute}
  \centering
  {\normalsize Fleet Law, per step on $N$ devices:}\\[0.2cm]
  {\Large $\Tstep(N) = \dfrac{\Tcomp}{N} + \Tcomm(N) + \Tsync(N) - \Tover$}\\[0.3cm]
  \uncover<2->{%
  {\normalsize Scaling efficiency, measured against one device:}\\[0.2cm]
  {\Large $\etascale = \dfrac{\Tcomp}{N\,\Tstep(N)} = \dfrac{1}{1 + \dfrac{N\,(\Tcomm + \Tsync - \Tover)}{\Tcomp}}$}}\\[0.2cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<3->{The overhead is multiplied by $N$; the compute is not.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Distributed Training, equations 1 and 2; the Fleet Law: Introduction.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Because replicas match single-device SGD mathematically, hardware overhead decides whether adding GPUs speeds up each step. In Lecture 1's Fleet Law, only $T_{\text{compute}}/N$ shrinks as $N$ grows. Substituting that step time into Lecture 1's scaling efficiency ratio gives $\etascale = T_{\text{compute}} / (N\,T_{\text{step}}(N)) = 1 / [1 + N(T_{\text{comm}} + T_{\text{sync}} - T_{\text{overlap}})/T_{\text{compute}}]$, where 1.0 is ideal linear scaling and 0.5 means half of all GPU-hours go to overhead. Because exposed per-step overhead is multiplied by $N$ against fixed single-device compute, constant ring traffic near $2M/\beta$ (at link bandwidth $\beta$) pushes efficiency toward zero as $N$ doubles, creating the scaling wall.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Unlike Amdahl's fixed single-node serial fraction (the unparallelizable share of single-device time), fleet communication and sync overhead is external and can grow with $N$. Even a constant 1\% single-device residual sees its share of each step reach $512 \times 1\% / (1 + 512 \times 1\%) \approx 84\%$ at 512 GPUs (\S6.4.2).}

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide evaluates this equation on the 175B running case at 1,024 GPUs.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One slow GPU in a hundred delays almost every step at 1,000}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Under a synchronous barrier, a step finishes on time only when every GPU does. Recall from Lecture 1 that a straggler is a live worker computing valid results slowly, for example from thermal throttling (clock slowdowns from heat), link congestion, or operating-system (OS) jitter (brief host pauses). If each GPU is independently late on 1\% of steps, a clean step occurs with probability $0.99^N$, falling from 92\% at 8 GPUs to 37\% at 100 GPUs and 0.004\% at 1,000 GPUs, because even one late GPU stalls the barrier while the other 999 wait. PyTorch's \texttt{init\_process\_group} defaults to a 10-minute NCCL timeout, which aborts dead workers yet misses a slow live GPU, a gray failure whose detection Lecture 7 develops.

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide compares strict barriers (BSP) against relaxed synchronization models (SSP and ASP).}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{BSP waits at a barrier; SSP and ASP trade waiting for staleness}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{sync_step}{3}\\[0.1cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<2->{Staleness: updates between computing a gradient and applying it.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Distributed Training, \S6.3.1, \S6.4.4, figure 5, tables 2 and 4; Ho et al. 2013; Dutta et al. 2018. Timelines schematic.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Because waiting for one straggler stalls almost every step at scale, distributed frameworks choose along a spectrum of synchronization models (\S6.3.1, \S6.4.4), shown across four workers with slow worker W3. Under Lecture 3's Bulk Synchronous Parallel (BSP) model, all ranks compute on identical weights and wait at each barrier, preserving exact single-device SGD convergence while letting W3 pace every step. Stale Synchronous Parallel (SSP), introduced by Ho et al. (2013) as a bounded-staleness middle ground, lets fast workers advance up to $s$ updates ahead before waiting, trimming idle time at the cost of gradients computed on weights up to $s$ versions old. Asynchronous Parallel (ASP) removes global barriers entirely, trading global-barrier waiting for unbounded staleness. Final production training runs almost always choose BSP for predictable convergence, and any relaxed protocol must win on wall-clock time to matched validation quality.

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide turns from barriers to the statistical ceiling on batch size, the critical batch $\Bcrit$.}
  }
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Past the critical batch, more samples buy few fewer steps}
  \begin{columns}[c]

      Critical batch, from gradient noise:\\[0.15cm]
      {\Large $\Bcrit \approx \dfrac{\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)}{\lVert\nabla \mathcal{L}\rVert^2}$}\\[0.2cm]
      {\small noise in one sample's gradient over the true gradient's strength}\\[0.3cm]

    \end{column}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Even when synchronous barriers under BSP are fast, adding replicas grows the global batch until extra samples stop cutting optimizer steps. To locate that statistical ceiling, McCandlish et al. (2018) modeled the critical batch $\Bcrit \approx \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma) / \lVert \nabla\mathcal{L} \rVert^2$, comparing per-sample gradient variance (noise) against squared true gradient magnitude (signal). Below $\Bcrit$ ($B < \Bcrit$), doubling batch size nearly halves optimizer steps with few extra samples. At $B = \Bcrit$, both steps and total samples sit at twice their minimum; past $\Bcrit$, step count flattens while total samples climb quickly. This ceiling bounds useful data-parallel width at $N \approx \Bcrit / b$ for local batch $b$. Pushing $N$ further forces smaller $b$, exposing more communication, or requires large-batch optimizers that adapt step sizes per layer.

    \notecue{Watch for}{$\Bcrit$ shifts across models, datasets, and training phases, typically rising later in training. Always validate by wall-clock time to matched target quality.}

\begin{frame}{Full sharding gathers every layer's weights twice per step}

\begin{frame}{Microbatches keep pipeline stages busy except at fill and drain}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.5cm,keepaspectratio]{pipe_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Distributed Training, \S6.5.1--\S6.5.2, figures 12--13 (interactive Explore figure: Pipeline Parallelism). F: forward; B: backward; red: idle.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Recall from Lectures 2 and 5 that pipeline parallelism assigns consecutive layer blocks to stages and sends only boundary activations point-to-point across nodes. Sending one whole batch across four stages leaves 6 of 8 time slots idle while stages wait. In 2019, Google's GPipe schedule introduced synchronous microbatch pipelining, splitting one global update into $m = 4$ smaller slices called microbatches so stages run concurrently. By slot 4 every stage is busy, cutting idle time on GPU 0 to 6 of 14 slots (43\%). However, because GPipe finishes all $m$ forward passes before starting any backward pass, stage 0 must keep activations for all $m$ microbatches live at once.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Microbatches slice one global batch inside the pipeline without changing the optimizer update math.}

\begin{frame}{1F1B caps live activations at $p$ stages instead of $m$ microbatches}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{pipe_1f1b_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Distributed Training, \S6.5.2 (interactive Explore figure: 1F1B Pipeline Schedule); Narayanan et al. 2019.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    To eliminate GPipe's activation buildup, Narayanan et al.'s (2019) one-forward-one-backward (1F1B) schedule starts backward passes early, shown here for $p = 4$ stages and $m = 8$ microbatches. During warm-up, stage $s$ launches $p - s$ forward passes (4, 3, 2, and 1 across stages) until microbatch 1 reaches stage 3. In steady state, every stage alternates one backward pass and one forward pass, freeing each microbatch's activations right after its backward pass. Peak live activations stay capped at $[4, 3, 2, 1]$ rather than GPipe's 8 on every stage, with stage 0 holding $p$ during the round trip from stage 3. Cooldown then drains the remaining backward passes, leaving six of twenty-two slots idle on each stage, the same $3/11$ (27.3\%) bubble as GPipe at half the activation memory, with larger savings as $m$ grows.

\begin{frame}{Megatron splits columns, then rows, then reduces once}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{tp_step}{3}\\[0.15cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<3->{Two AllReduces per transformer block, inside every layer of every microbatch.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Distributed Training, \S6.5.3 and figure 15; Shoeybi et al. 2019 (Megatron-LM).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Pipeline stages keep every layer whole on one GPU, so when a single layer is too large or slow, tensor parallelism (previewed in Lecture 2) cuts inside its matrix multiplications. NVIDIA's Megatron-LM (the 512-GPU run from Lecture 0; Shoeybi et al., 2019) minimizes collectives by pairing column and row splits. Splitting the first linear layer by columns lets each GPU multiply the full input $X$ by its weight columns to produce a local intermediate slice $Y$ with zero communication. Splitting the second matrix by rows lets each GPU multiply $Y$ by matching weight rows into a partial output, and one AllReduce sums those partial outputs. Pairing attention (query, key, and value, or QKV, plus output projection) with the multilayer perceptron (MLP) needs two AllReduces per transformer block in forward and two in backward.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Unlike data parallelism, tensor parallelism changes model code by replacing standard linear layers with column-parallel and row-parallel layers.}

    \notecue{Next}{The next slide checks whether tensor-parallel exchanges fit on NVLink versus cross-node InfiniBand.}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.2cm}
    \item Replicas reproduce one-device SGD; their AllReduce stays near $2M$, so efficiency hits a wall.
    \item The slowest worker sets each synchronous step, and the critical batch caps useful replicas.
    \item ZeRO and FSDP fit the state by sending up to 1.5$\times$ the bytes and gathering every layer.
    \item Pipelines idle $(p-1)/(m+p-1)$; tensor parallelism needs the NVLink domain.
    \item A plan is one point $DP \times PP \times TP$ that fits memory, batch, and every tier.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    Five laws govern every distributed training plan in this lecture. Data-parallel replicas hit scaling walls from constant $2M$ AllReduces, while stragglers and $\Bcrit$ cap useful width. ZeRO and FSDP trade memory for up to $1.5\times$ communication bytes, pipelines pay $(p-1)/(m+p-1)$ idle bubbles, tensor parallelism requires the fast NVLink domain, and a feasible plan picks one joint $DP \times PP \times TP$ point that fits memory, batch, and every hardware tier.
