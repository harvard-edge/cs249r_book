\begin{frame}{Each adaptation method shrinks a different budget term}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{terms_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\footnotesize LoRA rank 4, $768{\times}768$ layer: 589,824 $\rightarrow$ 6,144 parameters.}%
  \uncover<3->{\footnotesize\enspace TinyTL: up to 6.5$\times$ less memory.}
  \mlsyscredit{Edge Intelligence, equation 1 (schematic), \S11.4.2 and \S11.4.3; Hu et al. 2021 (LoRA); Cai et al., NeurIPS 2020 (TinyTL, as reported in the chapter).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Because full fine-tuning exceeds both background RAM and mobile DRAM budgets, each adaptation method shrinks one of Equation 1's three memory terms alongside OS and app reserves, namely resident weights, trainable state (parameters, gradients, and optimizer moments), and peak saved activations plus workspace. Bias-only updates, inserted adapters, and low-rank adaptation (LoRA) shrink trainable state. Introduced by Hu et al. (2021) for transformers, LoRA freezes each base weight matrix and learns its update as the product of two thin matrices. Rank-4 LoRA on a $768 \times 768$ layer cuts trainable parameters by 99\%, from 589,824 down to 6,144, yet leaves the 200 MB activation bar untouched. Quantization, pruning (removing weights), and distillation (training a smaller model) shrink resident weights, while head-only training and Tiny Transfer Learning (TinyTL) directly shrink saved activations, cutting training memory by up to 6.5$\times$ (Cai et al. 2020).

  \notecue{Next}{Next, open TinyTL's architecture to see how it cuts that dominant activation memory bar.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{TinyTL freezes heavy weights and adds lite-residual modules}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=4.65cm,keepaspectratio]{tinytl_step}{3}\\[0.08cm]
  {\footnotesize Bias updates skip conv activations; lite-residual modules recover accuracy at up to 6.5$\times$ less RAM.\par}
  \mlsyscredit{Edge Intelligence, \S11.4.3; Cai et al., NeurIPS 2020 (TinyTL). Explore: TinyTL Architecture (mlsysbook.ai).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  TinyTL (Cai et al. 2020) showed on image-classification transfer that shrinking saved activations cuts memory far more than shrinking trainable parameters alone. Full fine-tuning updates every convolutional weight $W$, forcing the backward pass to retain full-resolution input activations $A$ at every layer; in our 10M stack, those activations take 200 MB and cause the 360 MB out-of-memory (OOM) kill. Freezing $W$ and updating only biases drops weight gradients and convolutional input activations, lowering peak RAM, although bias-only updates lack capacity to recover full accuracy. TinyTL therefore keeps the heavy backbone frozen, updates biases, and attaches parallel low-resolution, grouped lite-residual side modules whose compact intermediate activations restore accuracy while cutting training RAM by up to 6.5$\times$.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Nonlinearities and normalization layers still require compact saved state for gradient flow, and head-only tuning remains cheapest whenever its accuracy suffices.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, an optional worked example measures how adapters reduce on-device storage when personalizing one backbone across ten user contexts.}
}
\end{frame}

  Beyond runtime RAM, a frozen backbone also shrinks device storage across multiple contexts. Napkin Math 11.2 personalizes a 10M-parameter FP32 camera model (40 MB) across 10 contexts such as home, office, and car. \begin{frame}{Masks hide each update and cancel in the sum}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.4cm,keepaspectratio]{secagg_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Edge Intelligence, figure 16 (redrawn; values illustrative). Bonawitz et al., CCS 2017.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  To hide individual updates without losing their sum, secure aggregation (Bonawitz et al., CCS 2017) lets the server compute the exact aggregate while seeing only noise per phone. First, phones A and B hold private updates $\Delta\theta_A$ and $\Delta\theta_B$ (blue) and agree on a shared random mask $s$ unknown to the server (gray). Next, phone A uploads $\Delta\theta_A + s$ and phone B uploads $\Delta\theta_B - s$, so each masked upload (such as $+7.5$) looks uniformly random to the server. Finally, summing the uploads cancels $+s$ and $-s$ identically, leaving the exact aggregate ($+0.3, +0.2, +0.2, +0.4$). Across many phones, pairwise masks cancel across the whole cohort. That design raises an immediate failure question, because any phone that drops out before uploading leaves its masks uncanceled.

\begin{frame}{A dropout leaves masks that survivors must rebuild}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{secagg_drop_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Gboard's protocol: four message rounds; cost grows with users squared, so groups of hundreds.}
  \mlsyscredit{Edge Intelligence, \S11.7.4. Bonawitz et al., CCS 2017; Bonawitz et al., MLSys 2019, \S6.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  With routine field dropouts, pairwise masking faces an immediate hazard. When phone C agrees on masks with phones A, B, D, and E and then loses Wi-Fi, its uncanceled masks make the four surviving uploads unreadable. Secret-sharing recovery solves this (Bonawitz et al. 2017, 2019). Every phone splits its mask seeds into threshold shares among peers, cryptographic pieces such that any $t$ survivors can rebuild a dropout's seed in an extra message round so the server can subtract C's masks. If fewer than $t$ survive, the round aborts because summing too few updates weakens privacy, and over-selection helps survivors clear $t$. Because Gboard's four-round protocol has quadratic terms in cohort size, production groups stay in the hundreds, with at least $k$ phones per secure group before combining group sums.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Hiding individual updates prevents the server from inspecting single submissions for poisoning, where malicious updates corrupt the model. Lecture 13 covers poisoning and robust aggregation, combining rules that resist faulty updates.}

Each update fits its resource budget, yet repeated updates risk catastrophic forgetting, where learning new patterns degrades older skills, such as an activity tracker forgetting walking after learning cycling. The illustrative curves show that replaying a compact local buffer of tens to hundreds of stored examples or compressed features preserves baseline accuracy, provided the buffer follows the same retention and privacy rules as raw data. In FedPer (Arivazhagan et al. 2019), the shared backbone aggregates across the fleet while a personal head stays on the device so global averaging never overwrites personal behavior.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Freezing the backbone does not freeze predictions when the head changes, and real-world data shifts can resemble forgetting. Lecture 14 classifies distribution shifts.}

  \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip this slide and note that replay buffers and personal heads limit forgetting before moving straight to release governance.}

\begin{frame}{Once models learn after release, the release is a policy}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=4.7cm,keepaspectratio]{gate_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small $\tau$: disagreement tolerance. The release ships the shadow check with the model.\par}
  \mlsyscredit{Edge Intelligence, \S11.9, \S11.10, figure 20 (redrawn; letters illustrative), table 9.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Once phones adapt locally, shipped weights no longer describe field behavior. On-device shadow execution runs a frozen validated copy beside the adapted candidate on live inputs without labels; here 2 disagreements in 8 inputs (25\%) exceed tolerance $\tau = 10\%$ and hold the candidate out of service. Only validated outcomes such as delayed labels, trusted user corrections, or safety invariants can clear it or confirm rollback, because disagreement proves change rather than which model is right. A release therefore ships a complete policy covering base version, per-tier adaptation (rank-32 LoRA on flagships, rank-16 on mid-tier, bias-only on budget), admission rules, recovery point, and cohort holds. Returning updates tag base version and round so servers reject stale work across up to 50 live versions, using the version catalog (model registry) and wave-by-wave staged rollout of Lecture 12.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A staged edge rollout can appear healthy while failing because older, thermally constrained phones drop out and report diagnostic telemetry least often.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, an optional war story from Williston (2016) illustrates why every accepted update must preserve a validated recovery path.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.21cm}
    \item Learn on the phone only for a measured gain, and fit the update to the background allowance.
    \item Admit an update only when energy, heat, and the foreground deadline all have slack; charging relaxes energy only.
    \item A round waits on its slowest kept phone; local steps trade uplink rounds for client drift; masks hide each update.
    \item A federated model learns from admitted, fast phones; measure coverage per cohort and ship a checked, versioned policy.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Answering the driving question, these four takeaways pair one engineering rule with each section of the lecture. First, justify local learning with a measured gain and fit every update inside background RAM. Second, enforce energy, thermal, and foreground admission tests, noting that charging relaxes energy only. Third, budget federated rounds for stragglers, client drift, uplink break-even, and secure masks. Fourth, audit cohort coverage and ship versioned release policies.

  \notecue{Teach}{When pressed for time, emphasize the first and fourth lines.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, an optional slide poses three open design and certification questions.}
