\begin{frame}{TinyTL freezes heavy weights and adds lite-residual modules}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=4.65cm,keepaspectratio]{tinytl_step}{3}\\[0.08cm]
  {\footnotesize Bias updates skip conv activations; lite-residual modules recover accuracy at up to 6.5$\times$ less RAM.\par}
  \mlsyscredit{Edge Intelligence, \S11.4.3; Cai et al., NeurIPS 2020 (TinyTL). Explore: TinyTL Architecture (mlsysbook.ai).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Trace TinyTL across three steps (Cai et al. 2020). Step 1: full fine-tuning updates every convolutional weight $W$, forcing the backward pass to retain full-resolution input activations $A$ at every layer (200 MB in our 10M stack, causing the 360 MB OOM kill). Step 2: freezing $W$ and updating only biases eliminates weight gradients and heavy conv-input activations, slashing peak RAM, yet bias-only updates lack capacity to recover full accuracy. Step 3: TinyTL keeps the heavy backbone frozen, updates biases, and attaches parallel low-resolution, grouped lite-residual side modules whose tiny intermediate feature maps restore accuracy while cutting training RAM up to 6.5$\times$.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Nonlinearities and normalization layers still require compact saved state for gradient flow; head-only tuning remains cheapest whenever its accuracy suffices.}

\begin{frame}{Masks hide each update and cancel in the sum}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.4cm,keepaspectratio]{secagg_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Edge Intelligence, figure 16 (redrawn; values illustrative). Bonawitz et al., CCS 2017.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Secure aggregation (Bonawitz et al., CCS 2017) lets the coordinator compute the exact sum of updates without inspecting any individual phone's update. Step 1: phones A and B hold private updates $\Delta\theta_A$ and $\Delta\theta_B$ (blue) and agree on a shared random mask $s$ unknown to the server (gray). Step 2: phone A uploads $\Delta\theta_A + s$ and phone B uploads $\Delta\theta_B - s$; each masked upload (like $+7.5$) looks uniformly random to the server. Step 3: summing the uploads cancels $+s$ and $-s$ identically, leaving the exact aggregate ($+0.3, +0.2, +0.2, +0.4$). Across many phones, pairwise masks cancel across the whole cohort, raising an immediate failure question: what happens if phone B drops out before uploading?

  \notecue{Next}{Real production session logs first quantify how frequently phones drop out or miss deadlines in the field.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A dropout leaves masks that survivors must rebuild}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{secagg_drop_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Gboard's protocol: four message rounds; cost grows with users squared, so groups of hundreds.}
  \mlsyscredit{Edge Intelligence, \S11.7.4. Bonawitz et al., CCS 2017; Bonawitz et al., MLSys 2019, \S6.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Step 1 shows the dropout hazard: phone C agreed on masks with A, B, D, and E, then lost Wi-Fi, leaving uncanceled masks that render the four surviving uploads unreadable. Step 2 shows the secret-sharing recovery (Bonawitz et al. 2017, 2019): every phone splits its mask seeds into threshold shares among peers so any $t$ survivors can reconstruct a dropout's seed in an extra message round, letting the server subtract C's masks. If fewer than $t$ survive, the round aborts because summing too few updates weakens privacy. Over-selection helps ensure survivors clear $t$. Because Gboard's four-round protocol has quadratic terms in cohort size, production groups stay in the hundreds (at least $k$ phones per secure group) before combining group sums.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Hiding individual updates prevents the server from inspecting single submissions for poisoning; Lecture 13 covers poisoning and robust aggregation.}

\begin{frame}{True or false: local data means private?}

  {\normalsize\choice{T}{true}\qquad\answerat{2}{F}{false}}\\[0.4cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Updates can be inverted into examples. Secure aggregation hides each update from the server; differential privacy bounds what the released sum reveals.}

\begin{frame}{Once models learn after release, the release is a policy}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.21cm}
    \item Learn on the phone only for a measured gain, and fit the update to the background allowance.
    \item Admit an update only when energy, heat, and the foreground deadline all have slack; charging relaxes energy only.
    \item A round waits on its slowest kept phone; local steps trade uplink rounds for client drift; masks hide each update.
    \item A federated model learns from admitted, fast phones; measure coverage per cohort and ship a checked, versioned policy.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Review the four takeaways, one per segment: justify local learning with a measured gain and fit background RAM, enforce energy, thermal, and foreground admission tests (charging relaxes energy only), budget federated rounds for stragglers, client drift, uplink break-even, and secure masks, and audit cohort coverage while shipping versioned release policies. When pressed for time, emphasize the first and fourth lines.
