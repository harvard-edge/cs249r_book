\begin{frame}{A threat model writes down asset, boundary, adversary, and control}

      \uncover<3->{\begin{mlsyscard}{errorstroke}\footnotesize

      \end{mlsyscard}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Security \& Privacy, \S13.4 and \S13.4.1 (threat vector classification).}
  \note{[3 min] [CORE] The chapter's four fields: asset (what must stay confidential, intact, or available), boundary (where trust changes), adversary (capability and goal), and control (a permission, isolation, or signal that constrains the attack). Step 1: one federated round. The coordinator follows the protocol but can read messages; one enrolled client is compromised. The same update crosses two boundaries. Step 2: boundary A. The coordinator could invert a client's update to recover its data; hiding individual updates from it is confidentiality, the job of secure aggregation (Lecture 11 owns the masking). Step 3: boundary B. The client can submit an arbitrary update; checking or bounding contributions is integrity. Authenticating the client proves who sent the update and says nothing about whether it is benign. WARN: defense comparisons are meaningful only after the actors and collusions are stated. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\section{Poisoning}

\begin{frame}{A poisoner optimizes through the defender's own training}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.97\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{bilevel.pdf}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small $\max_{P}\ \mathcal{L}\big(f_{S \cup P},\, S_{\text{test}}\big)$, where $f_{S \cup P}$ is trained on $S \cup P$}
  \mlsyscredit{Security \& Privacy, \S13.5.3, equations 1 and 2, figure 9 (redrawn); Biggio et al. 2012.}
  \note{[2.5 min] [CORE] Poisoning needs a write capability: something the model learns from, such as scraped web pages, user feedback, or labels. Read the loop. The outer loop is the attacker, who chooses poison records $P$ to maximize loss on a target set. The inner loop is the defender's ordinary training, which minimizes training loss on the clean data $S$ plus the poison. The attacker cannot change the training code; it changes the data and lets the defender's own optimizer carry the effect. Evaluating a poison means estimating what training does with it, which is why the chapter calls the problem bilevel and why evaluation can need repeated training or a surrogate. The targeted form lowers the loss on one chosen input and label instead. Example from the chapter: an attacker with accounts on an email service reports campaign-style spam as ``not spam,'' so the retrained filter lets the campaign through while accuracy on other mail looks normal. ENGAGE: why does a targeted attack barely move validation accuracy? (Expected: it changes the model only near the target; the validation set has almost no inputs there.) LINK: poisoning changes the model before deployment; a sticker on a stop sign changes only the input at inference (Lecture 14 owns adversarial inputs). FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Differential privacy bounds what one person can change}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{dp_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small $\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D) \in S] \le e^{\epsilon}\,\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D') \in S] + \delta$ \quad for adjacent $D$, $D'$}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Adjacency names the protected unit: one record, or all of one person's records.}
  \mlsyscredit{Security \& Privacy, Definition 13.5 and figure 24 (redrawn, illustrative); Dwork et al. 2006; Dwork and Roth 2014.}
  \note{[2 min] [CORE] A randomized mechanism $\mathcal{A}$ releases a noisy answer. Step 1: run it on dataset $D$ and on an adjacent dataset $D'$ that differs in one protected contribution; the two output distributions overlap without matching. Step 2: the guarantee. For any set of outcomes $S$, the probability under $D$ is at most $e^{\epsilon}$ times the probability under $D'$, plus a small slack $\delta$. $\epsilon$ is the privacy budget: smaller means the two releases are harder to tell apart. It is not an accuracy percentage. The guarantee holds against any adversary, with any side information, because it is a property of the algorithm. Adjacency decides what it protects. Per-example clipping protects one record, so a person who contributes many records needs person-level adjacency and a bound on that person's total contribution. ENGAGE: querying a fixed DP-trained model a million times. Does it spend more budget? (Expected: no; it is post-processing of one release, though extraction controls still matter.) Federated learning keeps raw data off the server; secure aggregation (Lecture 11 owns the pairwise masks) hides each update from the coordinator, if enough clients do not collude and dropouts are handled; homomorphic encryption hides inputs from the server; synthetic data hides the source table but can leak what the generator memorized. k-anonymity, not shown, constrains only the shape of a released table. Only differential privacy bounds the release. The chapter's Gboard point is that a deployment claim must name which contracts its training run satisfies; the reports do not establish that every model combined secure aggregation with formal DP. Land the segment aloud: privacy is an accounted budget on releases. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
