  Meta reports two efficiency figures for training Llama 3 405B on 16,384 H100 GPUs over a 54-day snapshot (Dubey et al. 2024). \begin{frame}{Capacity crosses three boundaries before it becomes work}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{goodput_chain_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\footnotesize\textcolor{midgray}{Gang: the full set of devices a synchronous job needs before any step can run.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Efficiency, \S9.2 and figure 1; Wongpanich et al. 2025. Widths schematic.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Read each row as capacity-time passing through one boundary. First, scheduling goodput ($S$) is the share of offered capacity-time held by complete allocations, meaning every device a synchronous step requires; idle devices, fragments too small for queued jobs, and a partial gang holding 8 of its 16 nodes all fall here. Second, runtime goodput ($R$) is the share of held time spent on steps preserved by a checkpoint; startup, input waits, checkpoint pauses, recovery, and discarded rework are charged here. Third, program goodput ($P$) compares the ideal time of retained steps against their actual duration; exposed communication, stragglers, pipeline bubbles, memory stalls, and kernel dispatch fall here. Because each denominator equals the previous numerator, the three fractions multiply and every lost hour is charged exactly once at the first boundary it fails.

  \notecue{Watch for}{The boundary that records a loss does not always name the team that fixes it. For example, a slow node lowers $P$ even though the reliability layer replaces it.}

  \notecue{Next}{The focus slide formalizes this three-boundary chain as the ML Productivity Goodput equation.}
}
\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Read the equation slowly: offered capacity multiplies three boundary fractions, $S$, $R$, and $P$. Google introduced this decomposition for its TPU fleet under the name ML productivity goodput (Wongpanich et al. 2025). Useful output equals peak offered capacity multiplied by MPG, which immediately sets economics as well: the effective cost of one ideal-rate GPU-hour is the raw price divided by MPG.

  \notecue{Next}{An optional reference table distinguishes MPG from four other efficiency measures used across this volume so denominators never get mixed.}
}
\mlsysfocus{ML Productivity Goodput}{%
$\text{MPG} = \underbrace{S}_{\text{held}/\text{offered}} \times
     \underbrace{R}_{\text{retained}/\text{held}} \times
     \underbrace{P}_{\text{ideal}/\text{retained}}$\rule[-1.4cm]{0pt}{0pt}%
}

  \note{%

  A measurement contract fixes four fields so before-and-after runs differ only in the change under test: workload (model revision, input and sequence mix, batch, quality check), system state (device count, placement, clocks, power cap, software versions), timing boundary (separating warmup and cold start from steady state), and reporting (factor, denominator, window, and trial spread). \begin{frame}{Fleet profiling reads a hierarchy, cheapest record first}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.85cm,keepaspectratio]{ladder_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Efficiency, \S9.3.2, table 3, and figure 4.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Read the profiling ladder from top to bottom, cheapest record first. Step 1: scheduler records are written by the control plane for admission and billing at near-zero overhead; they log submission, device assignments, gang completion, and release, settling $S$ and the held-time denominator for $R$, though a wedged full gang looks healthy here. Step 2: the job timeline logs a few fields per step (first step, step times, checkpoint commits, restarts, input waits), settling $R$ and revealing whether retained steps run slowly without explaining why. Step 3: step traces record every kernel and collective on sampled ranks for seconds, settling $P$ and isolating the exact term to shorten. For the running job, scheduler records exposed partial holds, the timeline exposed slow steps from day one, and only then did a targeted trace inspect one data-parallel group.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Opening a detailed profiler first always reveals small gaps inside a step while potentially missing a week lost to queues or restarts.}

  \notecue{Next}{The next slide explains why fleets cannot run full step traces continuously across thousands of GPUs.}
}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Scheduler logs and job timelines scale by bytes per job, whereas traces scale with every device. Capturing a 5-second Nsight Systems trace produces about 500 MB per GPU, or 512 GB across 1,024 GPUs, while perturbing the step under measurement. Fleets therefore split telemetry into two tiers: lightweight always-on counters on every rank detect that a factor shifted, and triggered traces diagnose why on the affected ranks and a healthy peer. ByteDance's MegaScale draws cluster-wide heat maps from per-rank timers and traces only anomalous groups, identifying roughly 0.5\% of machines running markedly slower (Lecture 7 covers replacing those gray failures). Stratified sampling must cover job sizes, pipeline stages, and hours rather than rank 0 alone.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Periodic fixed-interval tracing misses intermittent stragglers between samples; threshold-triggered capture catches the spike while it occurs.}

  On July 2, 2019, Cloudflare deployed a single web application firewall rule globally whose regular expression returned correct results yet drove edge CPUs to roughly 100\%, causing a 27-minute outage and losing about 82\% of traffic. The highlighted tail \texttt{.*(?:.*=.*)} nests wildcards around an equals sign, forcing catastrophic backtracking on non-matching strings. Missing CPU guards, absent performance tests, and a global rollout turned a local cost into an outage.   \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Give pairs 90 seconds to name the first record for each symptom, then reveal step 2. For a weekly step shortfall, start at the top: scheduler records test whether the gang waited or held partial nodes, and the timeline then isolates startup, restarts, and input waits. \begin{frame}{A trade pays only if the product of ratios exceeds one}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{isogoodput_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<2->{$P \times 1.10$ and $R \times \tfrac{11}{12}$: product $\times 1.008$, a gain.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Efficiency, \S9.9.1 and figure 11 ($S$ held at 0.65).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Each curve holds $R \times P$ constant (and thus MPG for fixed $S$). Because a change multiplies MPG by the product of each factor's after-to-before ratios, separate teams can measure their own factor and multiply. Step 1: from the running job ($P = 0.48, R = 0.90$), eliminating all runtime loss adds at most 11\%. Step 2: lifting $P$ to 0.65 adds 35\% and still pays off even if $R$ drops up to 26\%. Bottom line: $P \times 1.10$ and $R \times 11/12$ yield $1.008$, a net gain without knowing $S$. Spending $R$ to buy $P$ merits testing; the reverse warrants skepticism.

  \notecue{Watch for}{One lever often moves two factors: faster compiled steps raise $P$ while lowering $R$ as fixed startup becomes a larger share. Check the product (or pool product for preemption) before calling a dip a regression.}

\begin{frame}{Twice the GPUs cut Llama 3's time to 0.52 at 4.9\% more cost}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.90\textwidth,height=4.55cm,keepaspectratio]{frontier_llama3.pdf}\\[0.05cm]
  {\normalsize $T_{\text{result}} = T_{\text{queue}} + \dfrac{T_{\text{ideal}}}{N \times R \times P}$}
  \mlsyscredit{Derived from Dubey et al. 2024, Table 4 (BF16 MFU 43\% at 8,192 and 41\% at 16,384 GPUs), assuming equal $R$; Fleet Efficiency, equation 7.}

  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%

Equation 7 writes time to result as queue wait plus ideal work divided by $N \times R \times P$; $S$ does not appear because idle devices elsewhere do not slow a running gang. Dubey et al. Neither point dominates: a release deadline selects 16,384 GPUs for speed, whereas a busy shared fleet selects 8,192 for cost efficiency.

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item Useful output is offered capacity $\times S \times R \times P$; each lost hour is charged at the first boundary it fails.
    \item Fix the contract and window first; read scheduler records, then the timeline, then traces.
    \item Held time leaks through partial gangs and five runtime terms; their order depends on job size.
    \item MFU or MBU measures $P$; a fix helps only through the exposed term it shortens.
    \item Rank levers and trades by the product; time to result and cost place a job on the frontier.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Walk concisely down the five takeaways, one per segment: multiplicative decomposition with single-charge attribution, fixed contracts and top-down profiling, held-time losses whose dominant owner shifts with scale, MFU/MBU and exposed Fleet Law terms, and product-ratio ranking alongside the cost-time frontier.
