\begin{frame}{Capacity crosses three boundaries before it becomes work}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{goodput_chain_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\footnotesize\textcolor{midgray}{Gang: the full set of devices a synchronous job needs before any step can run.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Efficiency, \S9.2 and figure 1; Wongpanich et al. 2025. Widths schematic.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  To charge every lost hour once without overlapping subsystem metrics, follow offered capacity-time across three sequential boundaries. First, scheduling goodput ($S$) is the share held by complete allocations, meaning every device a synchronous step requires; idle devices, fragments, and a partial gang holding 8 of its 16 nodes fall here. Next, runtime goodput ($R$) is the share of held time spent on steps preserved by a checkpoint, excluding startup, input waits, checkpoint pauses, recovery, and discarded rework. Finally, program goodput ($P$) compares ideal retained-step time against actual duration, losing exposed communication, stragglers, pipeline bubbles, memory stalls (waits on HBM data), and host kernel dispatch (CPU launch overhead per GPU kernel). Because each denominator equals the previous numerator, the three fractions multiply and charge every lost hour once at the first boundary it fails.

  \notecue{Watch for}{The boundary that records a loss does not always name the team that fixes it. For example, a slow node lowers $P$ even though the reliability layer replaces it.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, formalize this three-boundary chain as the ML Productivity Goodput equation.}
}
\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Multiplying those three boundary fractions gives the equation previewed in Lecture 1, ML productivity goodput, or MPG. Google introduced this decomposition to measure its production Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) fleet across training and serving, framing it as a fleet-level iron law of performance (Wongpanich et al. 2025). Because the three boundaries name no specific hardware, MPG applies to any accelerator fleet. Adjacent numerators and denominators cancel, so useful output equals peak offered capacity multiplied by MPG. That relationship sets economics directly, because the effective cost of one ideal-rate GPU-hour is the raw price divided by MPG.

  \notecue{Next}{Next, an optional reference table distinguishes MPG from four other efficiency measures used across this volume so denominators never get mixed.}
}
\mlsysfocus{ML Productivity Goodput}{%
$\text{MPG} = \underbrace{S}_{\text{held}/\text{offered}} \times
     \underbrace{R}_{\text{retained}/\text{held}} \times
     \underbrace{P}_{\text{ideal}/\text{retained}}$\rule[-1.4cm]{0pt}{0pt}%
}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
  With MPG defined, keep the five efficiency measures separate because both ``goodput'' and ``utilization'' carry distinct meanings across this course. Applying Lecture 1's rule, every claim must state its measure, denominator, and measurement window. Utilization tiers count devices (Lecture 8), and scaling efficiency compares one device against $N$ devices (Lecture 6). MPG counts capacity-time at the ideal rate (today), while service-level objective (SLO) goodput counts requests meeting latency contracts on top of MPG (Lecture 10). Statistical goodput weights throughput by training progress per example, so raising batch size past the critical batch size (where extra examples yield diminishing progress) can raise $P$ while lowering statistical goodput. When a dashboard reports ``goodput 0.9'', always ask which measure it uses and over what window.

\begin{frame}{Fleet profiling reads a hierarchy, cheapest record first}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.85cm,keepaspectratio]{ladder_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Efficiency, \S9.3.2, table 3, and figure 4.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Once a fixed contract confirms a slowdown, localize which boundary lost capacity before opening a kernel profiler, reading cheapest record first. At the top, scheduler records come from the control plane for admission and billing at near-zero overhead. They log submission, assignments, gang completion, and release, settling $S$ and $R$'s held-time denominator, though a stalled full gang still looks healthy. Next, the job timeline logs a few fields per step, including first step, step times, checkpoint commits, restarts, and input waits, settling $R$ and flagging slow retained steps without explaining why. At the bottom, step traces record every kernel and multi-GPU communication collective on sampled ranks for seconds, settling $P$ and isolating the term to shorten. On the running job, scheduler records exposed partial holds, the timeline exposed slow steps from day one, and a targeted trace then inspected one data-parallel group.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Opening a detailed profiler first always reveals small gaps inside a step while missing a week lost to queues or restarts.}

  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
  Even with fleet telemetry in place, a functionally correct software change can collapse fleet performance if rolled out untested. Cloudflare, which operates a global edge network for content delivery, proxying, and web application firewall filtering, illustrates this risk. On July 2, 2019, it deployed one firewall rule globally whose regular expression returned correct results yet drove edge CPUs to roughly 100\%, causing a 27-minute outage that lost about 82\% of traffic. The highlighted tail \texttt{.*(?:.*=.*)} nests wildcards around an equals sign, triggering exponential retry work called catastrophic backtracking on non-matching inputs. Missing CPU guards, absent performance tests, and a global rollout turned a local cost into an outage. In an ML fleet, a compiler, kernel library, or input-shape change can likewise preserve accuracy while lowering $P$ or lengthening compilation startup ($R$), so staged canaries (small pilot rollouts measuring step time and MFU per version, Lecture 12) catch regressions before wide rollout.

\begin{frame}{Fusion and FlashAttention keep intermediates off HBM}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=4.95cm,keepaspectratio]{kernel_fusion_flash_step}{4}\\[0.06cm]

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  To see how fusion and tiling raise arithmetic intensity by keeping intermediates off HBM, compare unfused kernels against fused execution and tiled attention. Unfused Linear, Activation, and Normalization kernels write and reread two intermediate tensors $I_1$ and $I_2$ across off-chip HBM, adding four intermediate transfers. Fusing them into one kernel keeps $I_1$ and $I_2$ in on-chip registers and SRAM, leaving only the input load and output store on HBM. Tiled attention in FlashAttention (Dao et al. 2022) avoids storing the quadratic sequence-by-sequence ($N \times N$) attention score matrix in HBM by holding one query tile in SRAM while streaming key-value tiles. Block 1, with score 1 and value 2, sets running max $m=1$, denominator $l=1$, and unnormalized sum $a=2$. When Block 2 arrives with score 3 and value 6, online softmax rescales prior $l$ and $a$ by $\alpha = \exp(1 - 3) = 0.135$ before adding Block 2, giving $m=3$, $l=1.135$, and $a=6.271$. Dividing $a/l$ once at the end yields 5.523, the exact softmax output, with HBM savings growing quadratically with sequence length.

  An organization pays for every offered accelerator-hour whether it stays idle, restarts, or stalls. Dividing the raw internal rate of \$2 per GPU-hour by the unrounded MPG of 0.282 yields \$7.09 per useful ideal-rate GPU-hour. That is 3.55$\times$ the raw price (rounded to 3.5$\times$ in the chapter), a multiplier carried by every trained and served token. Billing teams only for held hours divides by $R \times P$ alone (2.30$\times$), leaving the 35\% scheduling loss unbilled and forcing an explicit internal chargeback policy to bill teams for unallocated capacity (Lecture 12). \begin{frame}{A trade pays only if the product of ratios exceeds one}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{isogoodput_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<2->{$P \times 1.10$ and $R \times \tfrac{11}{12}$: product $\times 1.008$, a gain.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Fleet Efficiency, \S9.9.1 and figure 11 ($S$ held at 0.65).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Many real levers raise one factor while lowering another, so iso-goodput curves hold $R \times P$ constant and keep MPG constant for fixed $S$. Because a change multiplies MPG by the product of each factor's after-to-before ratios, separate teams can measure their own factor and multiply the results. Starting from the running job ($P = 0.48, R = 0.90$), eliminating all runtime loss adds at most 11\%. By contrast, lifting $P$ to 0.65 adds 35\% and still pays off even if $R$ drops by up to 26\%. In the example beneath the plot, $P \times 1.10$ and $R \times 11/12$ yield $1.008$, a net gain that holds without knowing $S$. Spending $R$ to buy $P$ merits testing, whereas the reverse warrants skepticism.

  \notecue{Watch for}{One lever often moves two factors, because faster compiled steps raise $P$ while lowering $R$ as fixed startup becomes a larger share. Check the product (or the pool product for preemption) before calling a dip a regression.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, an optional worked example applies this $R$-versus-$P$ trade to model compilation and shared build caches.}

\end{frame}

  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  While MPG governs cost efficiency, teams also care when a job finishes. Returning to Llama 3 405B's two scales (Dubey et al. 2024, Lecture 6), doubling from 8,192 to 16,384 GPUs lowers BF16 MFU from 43\% to 41\%. Neither point beats the other on both cost and time, so both sit on the efficiency frontier. A deadline chooses 16,384 GPUs for speed, whereas a busy fleet chooses 8,192 for cost efficiency.

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item Useful output is offered capacity $\times S \times R \times P$; each lost hour is charged at the first boundary it fails.
    \item Fix the contract and window first; read scheduler records, then the timeline, then traces.
    \item Held time leaks through partial gangs and five runtime terms; their order depends on job size.
    \item MFU or MBU measures $P$; a fix helps only through the exposed term it shortens.
    \item Rank levers and trades by the product; time to result and cost place a job on the frontier.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  The five takeaways capture one rule from each segment. First, useful output multiplies $S$, $R$, and $P$ with single-charge attribution, and measurements depend on fixed contracts and top-down profiling. Next, held-time losses shift their dominant owner with scale, while program goodput uses MFU or MBU and improves only through exposed Fleet Law terms. Finally, product ratios rank levers and trades alongside the cost-time frontier. Note the one rule you would have missed before this lecture.
