\begin{frame}{Power caps each instant; energy accumulates over the run}

      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{power_energy_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
      {\footnotesize Llama 3.1 405B, 16,384 H100s (derived, TDP basis; PUE 1.12 = facility $\div$ IT power)}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: Fukumoto, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons (TSUBAME 4.0). Dubey et al. 2024, \S3.3.1; Llama 3.1 model card. Method: Sustainable AI, \S15.3.1, Napkin Math 15.6, table 7.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  To size both limits for Llama 3.1 405B, start with instantaneous power (shown on four liquid-cooled H100 GPUs in a TSUBAME 4.0 node). At 700 W each, 16,384 GPUs draw 11.47 MW. Multiplying by the DGX H100 rack factor of 1.375$\times$ for hosts, switches, and conversion yields 15.77 MW of IT load. Applying power usage effectiveness (PUE, facility over IT power) of 1.12 lifts facility power to 17.66 MW (all derived), capping active servers and pricing Lecture 2's rack power wall. Integrating over time gives energy. Spreading 30.84M GPU-hours across 16,384 GPUs takes 1,882 hours, about 78 days at full scale, consuming $17.66\text{ MW} \times 1{,}882\text{ h} = 33.2\text{ GWh}$ of facility energy (derived). A short burst breaks power, whereas a long run grows energy.

  \notecue{Watch for}{TDP is a cooling design ceiling rather than metered average draw, making 17.66 MW and 33.2 GWh upper-bound planning estimates. Meta did not publish facility energy.}

\begin{frame}{A rack draws 1.5 times its GPU power}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=4.0cm,keepaspectratio]{rack_step}{3}\\[0.1cm]
  \uncover<3->{\small PUE (power usage effectiveness) $=$ facility power $\div$ IT power}\\[0.1cm]
  \uncover<3->{\footnotesize Losses upstream of the IT meter (transformer, UPS, distribution) raise PUE; server supplies and voltage regulators stay in IT load.}
  \mlsyscredit{Sustainable AI, table 7 (four-node DGX H100 rack budget, PUE about 1.09 allocation) and \S15.6.2.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Where does that GPU-to-facility multiplier come from? In one four-node DGX H100 rack (table 7), 32 GPUs at 700 W total 22.4 kW (kilowatts). Supporting IT adds 8.4 kW, lifting IT load to 1.38$\times$ GPU power (derived), from host CPUs and DRAM (3.2 kW), NVSwitch fabric (1.6 kW), InfiniBand adapters (0.8 kW), and 2.8 kW of internal conversion losses in server supplies and board voltage regulators (step-down converters). Upstream overhead at PUE about 1.09, including transformers and uninterruptible power supplies (UPS), adds 2.7 kW to reach 33.5 kW, or 1.50$\times$ GPU power (GPUs are 67\%). Sizing a 10 MW feed for 14,000 GPUs at 700 W (9.8 MW) fails because IT load alone reaches about 13.5 MW before PUE. Lecture 2 covered air and liquid heat removal; this breakdown places each electrical loss on its boundary.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Apply PUE once at the facility meter. Never multiply PUE onto a rack total already including facility allocation.}

  \notecue{Next}{Steady draw is only half the grid problem; next, examine synchronized tens-of-megawatts power swings during collective barriers.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Carbon is IT energy times PUE times grid intensity}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.97\textwidth,height=4.2cm,keepaspectratio]{chain_step}{4}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Grid intensity: grams of CO$_2$ the grid emits per kWh delivered.}
  \mlsyscredit{Derived for Llama 3.1 405B from the model card's 30.84M GPU-hours; rack profile and PUE from Sustainable AI, table 7 and Napkin Math 15.6; US grid factor from the chapter (MLSysIM).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Operational carbon converts electricity across separate accounting boundaries via equation 10 (with equation 9), where operational $\text{CO}_2 = E_{\text{IT}} \times \text{PUE} \times \text{grid intensity}$. Applied to Llama 3.1 405B, 30.84M GPU-hours $\times$ 700 W gives 21,588 MWh (megawatt-hours) of GPU energy (derived). Multiplying by 1.375 for hosts, switches, and conversion yields 29,684 MWh of IT energy. Multiplying once by PUE 1.12 reaches 33,246 MWh of facility energy. Multiplying by the US annual average grid intensity of 429 g/kWh yields 14,262 t (metric tons) of $\text{CO}_2$ (all derived). Each tier owns a distinct engineering lever. Efficiency cuts IT energy, cooling cuts PUE, and placement cuts grid intensity.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Each factor acts at a separate boundary with its own units, extending Lecture 1's energy boundary. Never multiply percentage savings from different tiers into one combined headline figure.}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Isolating PUE shows how much electricity cooling and power distribution add before any code changes. On a 2 MW IT cluster (Napkin Math 15.3), legacy air-cooled PUE 1.58 draws 3.16 MW, whereas state-of-the-art PUE 1.10 draws 2.20 MW. That 0.96 MW drop saves 8,409.6 MWh/yr (\$588,672/yr at \$70/MWh) without touching model code. Measured industry anchors bound that range. The Uptime Institute's 2022 survey of data-center operators averaged about 1.55, while Google reported a 1.09 fleet PUE in its 2025 fleet environmental study (Elsworth et al. 2025). Cooling failures waste energy too, because replaying 30 to 60 minutes on 10,000 accelerators repeats 3.5 to 7 MWh (\S15.6.3).

  \notecue{Watch for}{Halving idle server power does not lower PUE. Idle servers sit in the IT denominator, so PUE can rise even as total energy falls. A PUE = 1.0 indicates zero non-IT overhead rather than useful compute.}

  \notecue{Check}{Total fleet energy equals energy per query times queries, so $0.5 \times 4 = 2\times$ total energy (derived). In the chapter's fallacy, a 30$\times$ cheaper query with 100$\times$ volume lifts energy 3.3$\times$. \begin{frame}{US data-center electricity rose 3.3 times from 2014 to 2024}

  \mlsyscredit{LBNL (Shehabi et al. 2024; Smith et al. 2026), as reported in Sustainable AI, \S15.6.1 and figure 8. Kaplan et al. 2020 via \S15.3.3. Masanet et al., Science 2020.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  At national scale, demand outruns efficiency through two compounding forces. First, recalling Lecture 1's power-law scaling, Kaplan et al.'s (2020) neural language-model scaling study fitted a compute exponent of 0.05, so each 10\% loss reduction costs $0.9^{-1/0.05} = 8.2\times$ compute (68$\times$ for two steps, derived). Second, rebound turns cheaper inference into heavier usage. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) estimates that US data-center electricity rose 3.3$\times$ from 58 TWh (terawatt-hours) in 2014 to 192 TWh in 2024 (derived), ending the 2010s plateau documented by Masanet et al. (2020), when efficiency held global data-center electricity nearly flat even as computing output grew several-fold. LBNL's 2030 reference scenario projects 649 TWh inside a 521 to 843 TWh range.

  \notecue{Watch for}{The 2030 figure is a demand scenario range rather than a measurement or a guaranteed grid-supply ceiling.}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item Power caps what runs at an instant; energy adds up over the run.
    \item Carbon is IT energy $\times$ PUE $\times$ grid factor, comparable only with five declared fields.
    \item Manufacturing is paid before boot; its share rises on clean grids.
    \item Placement and time-shifting change the grid factor, each at a price.
    \item Demand can outrun efficiency, so efficiency needs an absolute budget.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  Together, these five rules answer the driving question, one per section. Separate instantaneous power ceilings from cumulative energy accounts, and declare all five contract fields before comparing carbon. Account for pre-boot embodied carbon on clean grids, price geographic placement, time-shifting, and water explicitly, and pair efficiency targets with absolute budgets against rebound.

  \notecue{Teach}{If short on time, stress the first and fifth bullets.}

  \notecue{Next}{Accounting rules clarify the numbers, yet siting and disclosure still demand policy choices; consider three open questions next.}
}
\end{frame}
