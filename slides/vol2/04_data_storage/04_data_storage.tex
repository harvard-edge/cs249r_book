\begin{frame}{Training streams large shards; databases seek small rows}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.36\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{hdd-head-platter.jpg}\\[0.1cm]
      {\footnotesize\textcolor{midgray}{A disk head must move for every random read.}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.62\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
      \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
        \toprule
        & \textbf{Database} & \textbf{Pretraining} \\
        \midrule
        Reads & random 4 KB & sequential shards \\
        Working set & hot 10\% in cache & whole corpus, once \\
        Writes & small, continuous & 2,450 GB bursts \\
        Serving & per query & bulk model loads \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}\\[0.25cm]
      {\small One NVMe flash drive: \textbf{7 to 14 GB/s} sequential,\\about \textbf{0.5 GB/s} on random 4 KB reads.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: Matthew Field, CC BY-SA 3.0, Wikimedia Commons. Data Storage, \S4.2 and table 1.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Storage engineered for databases breaks down under full-corpus pretraining because every access assumption inverts. Databases issue random 4 KB reads over a hot 10\% working set of repeatedly accessed records alongside continuous small writes. Pretraining instead streams large sequential files, called shards, once across the whole corpus, which defeats least-recently-used (LRU) caches that expect recent items to recur. It then bursts 2,450 GB checkpoints every 10 minutes and bulk-loads whole models for serving. Even modern NVMe flash drops from 7 to 14 GB/s sequentially down to about 0.5 GB/s on random 4 KB reads, so buying storage for random input/output operations per second (IOPS) wastes budget.

    \notecue{Watch for}{This inversion holds for single-epoch pretraining. Fine-tuning, repeatedly revisiting difficult training samples, and recommender embedding tables do create hot working sets.}

    \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip this slide and open Section 2 directly with the I/O wall.}

    \notecue{Next}{Even with sequential streaming, a fixed storage path caps throughput no matter how fast accelerators become.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{With delivery fixed, a 16$\times$ faster accelerator runs 4$\times$ faster}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=4.8cm,keepaspectratio]{io_wall_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Delivery takes a quarter of the original compute time and overlaps it.}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Storage, Systems Perspective 4.1 (normalized example); curve derived: throughput $=\min(s, 4)$.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    When storage delivery stays fixed while accelerators get faster, training hits the I/O wall (Systems Perspective 4.1). Suppose batch delivery takes one-quarter of the baseline compute time and overlaps computation, so step duration equals the slower of compute and delivery. Up to $4\times$ faster hardware, throughput climbs linearly until compute shrinks to match delivery time. Extending to $16\times$ faster hardware flattens throughput at $4\times$ ($\min(s, 4)$), leaving three-quarters of the faster chip idle. Deeper prefetch queues, which load upcoming batches ahead of time, cannot lift this plateau because the steady-state deficit recurs every step. Raising the ceiling requires faster storage, denser representations, or more parallel paths.

    \notecue{Watch for}{A larger model doing more FLOPs per input byte pushes the plateau higher. In Fleet Law terms, hitting the I/O wall creates idle time that no collective explains.}

    \notecue{Next}{Moving past the I/O wall requires matching data to the right storage layer, so the next slide maps all six physical tiers.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Six tiers trade bandwidth for capacity, each at its own scope}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.99\textwidth,height=5.9cm,keepaspectratio]{tiers_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Storage, \S4.3, figure 1, and table 2.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    No single medium delivers both accelerator-speed bandwidth and petabyte capacity, so ML infrastructure stacks six storage tiers by physical distance from Tier 0 to Tier 5. They run from on-package HBM, through intra-node host DRAM and NVMe, to a rack-scale parallel file system (PFS), data-center object storage, and offline archive. Comparing tiers requires reading capacity, bandwidth, and latency alongside each tier's sharing scope. HBM delivers 3.35 TB/s to one GPU, DRAM supplies 200 GB/s across an eight-GPU node, and a 1 TB/s PFS serves the entire cluster. Memory latency stays in nanoseconds, at about 273 ns for HBM and about 100 ns for DRAM, before jumping roughly $1{,}000\times$ to about 100 \textmu s (microseconds) on NVMe. Because an aggregate 1 TB/s PFS exceeds local NVMe's 7 to 25 GB/s, tiers do not rank by raw aggregate bandwidth alone.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Tier bandwidth ratios are not feed-rate requirements because HBM reuses weights and activations many times per batch. Section 3 derives exact feed demand.}

    \notecue{Next}{To compare tiers fairly, the next slide converts these aggregate specifications into per-client bandwidth.}
  }
\end{frame}

\section{Delivery}

\begin{frame}{Required bandwidth is consumers $\times$ batch bytes $\div$ compute time}
  \centering
  \vspace{0.3cm}
  {\Large $\text{BW}_{\text{required}} =
    \underbrace{N_{\text{GPU}}}_{\text{\normalsize\uncover<2->{consumers}}} \times
    \underbrace{\eta_{\text{target}}}_{\text{\normalsize\uncover<3->{busy fraction}}} \times
    \dfrac{\overbrace{D_{\text{vol,batch}}}^{\text{\normalsize\uncover<4->{bytes per batch}}}}
          {\underbrace{T_{\text{iteration}}}_{\text{\normalsize\uncover<4->{compute time}}}}$}\\[0.6cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<5->{Delivered rate $=$ the slowest stage: read, network, decode, host copy, PCIe.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Storage, \S4.4, equations 2 and 3.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Sizing a storage path starts by turning accelerator consumption into required bytes per second (equations 2 and 3). First, $N_{\text{GPU}}$ counts distinct batch consumers, so GPUs sharing one batch via tensor or pipeline parallelism count as one reader. Next, $\eta_{\text{target}}$ is the desired active compute-time fraction, typically 0.8 to 0.95, which differs from MFU. The ratio $D_{\text{vol,batch}} / T_{\text{iteration}}$ then gives per-consumer demand. Faster chips shrink $T_{\text{iteration}}$ and directly raise storage demand, while doubling consumers doubles total bandwidth. Finally, the pipeline law sets delivered throughput to the slowest stage across storage read, network, CPU decode, host copy, and PCIe. Because compression makes on-disk files smaller than decoded tensors, each stage must be expressed in a common unit, such as batches per second or decoded output bytes, before comparing rates.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Do not multiply by $\eta_{\text{target}}$ if the measured $T_{\text{iteration}}$ already includes I/O stalls, because that double-counts the slowdown. Verify also whether heavier compression slows CPU decode more than it speeds disk reads.}

    \notecue{Next}{Next, apply this equation to compare text and image bandwidth demand across 2,048 GPUs.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Overlap hides I/O only when upstream stages beat compute}

  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=4.8cm,keepaspectratio]{stall_step}{4}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Data stall $= (T_{\text{step}} - T_{\text{compute}}) / T_{\text{step}}$: the share of each step spent waiting for input.}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Storage, Napkin Math 4.5, \S4.4.1, and equation 4 (interactive Explore figure: Data Pipeline Overlap).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%

The data stall ratio $(T_{\text{step}} - T_{\text{compute}}) / T_{\text{step}}$ quantifies input wait; in Napkin Math 4.5, GPU compute takes 200 ms. Sequential loading with 250 ms of I/O stretches each step to 450 ms, stalling GPUs 55.6\% of the time. Resizing Storage Read and CPU Prep below 200 ms enables a three-stage pipeline with three batches in flight, reaching steady state at zero data stall. Profiler GPU idle time blames storage only when traces confirm waiting on the input queue rather than collectives.

\begin{frame}{At a commit boundary, a replay is safe only with keyed writes}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{ackgap_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Storage, Example 4.2 (the acknowledgment gap) and figure 9; Akidau et al. 2015.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    A mid-stage crash exposes the acknowledgment gap at a commit boundary (Example 4.2). Suppose a pipeline worker reads click event \texttt{e42}, appends its output row, and crashes before committing its progress cursor. A replacement worker cannot tell whether \texttt{e42} landed, so skipping loses data while replaying an append duplicates \texttt{e42} in every downstream dataset. Replacing blind appends with a stable deterministic key, \texttt{(feature\_version, e42)}, makes retries idempotent, meaning every replay overwrites the same row and leaves the same final state. As Akidau et al. formalized in their 2015 paper ``The Dataflow Model,'' effectively exactly-once semantics require replayable inputs plus idempotent writes rather than a reliable network transport alone (Lecture 12 builds feature-consistency contracts on this rule).

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item Tiers quote bandwidth at different scopes; 5\% misses at 100$\times$ cost 83\% of the rate.
    \item Required bandwidth is consumers $\times$ batch bytes $\div$ compute time, at the slowest stage.
    \item Shards amortize file opens; the metadata server saturates before the bytes do.
    \item A checkpoint writes each byte once, pauses for the local write, and counts at its manifest.
    \item Versioned, keyed writes make replay safe; price placement by the whole delivery bill.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    These five takeaways match each segment of the lecture. They recap scoped tier bandwidth and the 5\% miss penalty, the input-demand equation at the slowest stage, sharding to prevent metadata meltdowns, write-once staged checkpoints committed by manifest, and versioned keyed writes priced by total delivery cost.
