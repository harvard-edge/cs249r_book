\begin{frame}{Training streams large shards; databases seek small rows}

        Reads & random 4 KB & sequential shards \\

        Writes & small, continuous & 2,450 GB bursts \\

      \end{tabular}\\[0.25cm]
      {\small One NVMe flash drive: \textbf{7 to 14 GB/s} sequential,\\about \textbf{0.5 GB/s} on random 4 KB reads.}

  \mlsyscredit{Photo: Matthew Field, CC BY-SA 3.0, Wikimedia Commons. Data Storage, \S4.2 and table 1.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Conventional database instincts invert during full-corpus pretraining. Walking the table: databases perform random 4 KB reads over a hot 10\% working set with continuous small writes, whereas pretraining streams sequential shards once across the entire corpus (defeating LRU caches), bursts 2,450 GB checkpoints every 10 minutes, and bulk-loads whole models for serving.     \notecue{Watch for}{This inversion holds for single-epoch pretraining; fine-tuning, hard-example mining, and recommender tables do create hot working sets.}

\begin{frame}{With delivery fixed, a 16$\times$ faster accelerator runs 4$\times$ faster}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=4.8cm,keepaspectratio]{io_wall_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Delivery takes a quarter of the original compute time and overlaps it.}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Storage, Systems Perspective 4.1 (normalized example); curve derived: throughput $=\min(s, 4)$.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    This two-step chart illustrates the I/O wall (Systems Perspective 4.1) when batch delivery overlaps computation at one-quarter of the baseline compute time, so step duration equals the slower of compute and delivery. First, step 1 scales accelerator speed up to $4\times$: throughput climbs linearly until compute shrinks to match delivery time. Then, step 2 pushes hardware to $16\times$: the curve flattens at $4\times$ throughput ($\min(s, 4)$), leaving three-quarters of the faster chip idle. Deeper prefetch queues cannot lift this plateau because the steady-state deficit recurs every step; raising it requires faster storage, denser representations, or more parallel paths.

    \notecue{Watch for}{A larger model doing more FLOPs per input byte pushes the plateau higher; in Fleet Law terms, hitting the I/O wall creates idle time that no collective explains.}

  {\normalsize\choice{A}{95\% of the hot rate}\qquad\choice{B}{about half}\qquad\choice{C}{about one sixth}}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<2->{\safeimg[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{cache_hit.pdf}}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    Modeling tiered storage as a cache with hit rate $h$ gives expected service time $\mathbb{E}[T] = h\,T_{\text{hot}} + (1 - h)\,T_{\text{miss}}$ (equation 1). Step 2 reveals choice C (about one-sixth): with $h = 0.95$ and $T_{\text{miss}} = 100\,T_{\text{hot}}$, $\mathbb{E}[T] = 0.95(1) + 0.05(100) = 5.95\,T_{\text{hot}}$, cutting effective rate to 17\% of hot speed because the 5\% misses cost five times more than the 95\% hits. Choice A (95\%) is the intuitive trap of averaging speeds directly rather than averaging service times.}

\mlsysdivider{How much bandwidth must storage deliver, and where does it fall short?}{Data Storage, \S4.3.1, \S4.4, and \S4.5}
\section{Delivery}

\begin{frame}{Required bandwidth is consumers $\times$ batch bytes $\div$ compute time}
  \centering
  \vspace{0.3cm}
  {\Large $\text{BW}_{\text{required}} =
    \underbrace{N_{\text{GPU}}}_{\text{\normalsize\uncover<2->{consumers}}} \times
    \underbrace{\eta_{\text{target}}}_{\text{\normalsize\uncover<3->{busy fraction}}} \times
    \dfrac{\overbrace{D_{\text{vol,batch}}}^{\text{\normalsize\uncover<4->{bytes per batch}}}}
          {\underbrace{T_{\text{iteration}}}_{\text{\normalsize\uncover<4->{compute time}}}}$}\\[0.6cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<5->{Delivered rate $=$ the slowest stage: read, network, decode, host copy, PCIe.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Storage, \S4.4, equations 2 and 3.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Walk through equations 2 and 3 across five reveals. First, step 2 defines $N_{\text{GPU}}$ as distinct batch consumers (GPUs sharing one batch via tensor or pipeline parallelism count as one reader). Second, step 3 multiplies by $\eta_{\text{target}}$, the desired active compute-time fraction (typically 0.8--0.95, distinct from MFU). Third, step 4 forms per-consumer demand $D_{\text{vol,batch}} / T_{\text{iteration}}$: faster chips shrink $T_{\text{iteration}}$, directly raising storage demand, and doubling consumers doubles total bandwidth. Fourth, step 5 states the pipeline law: delivered throughput equals the slowest stage across storage read, network, CPU decode, host copy, and PCIe, measured in matched output-equivalent bytes.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Do not multiply by $\eta_{\text{target}}$ if your measured $T_{\text{iteration}}$ already includes I/O stalls, or you double-count the slowdown. Also verify whether heavier compression slows CPU decode more than it speeds disk reads.}

    \notecue{Next}{Students next apply this equation to compare text and image bandwidth demand across 2,048 GPUs.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Overlap hides I/O only when upstream stages beat compute}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=4.8cm,keepaspectratio]{stall_step}{4}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Data stall $= (T_{\text{step}} - T_{\text{compute}}) / T_{\text{step}}$: the share of each step spent waiting for input.}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Storage, Napkin Math 4.5, \S4.4.1, and equation 4 (interactive Explore figure: Data Pipeline Overlap).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    This four-step build visualizes the data stall ratio $(T_{\text{step}} - T_{\text{compute}}) / T_{\text{step}}$ for 200 ms of GPU compute (Napkin Math 4.5). First, step 1 loads sequentially with 250 ms I/O: steps take 450 ms, stalling GPUs 55.6\% of the time. Second, step 2 overlaps batch $i+1$ loading with batch $i$ compute: step time drops to $\max(200, 250) = 250$ ms, yet a 50 ms stall (20\%) persists every step because I/O trails compute. Third, step 3 resizes Storage Read and CPU Prep below 200 ms, launching a three-stage pipeline with three batches in flight. Fourth, step 4 reaches steady state at zero data stall. Note that profiler GPU idle time blames storage only when traces confirm waiting on the input queue rather than collectives.

    \notecue{Check}{Give pairs two minutes to compute both annual bills before revealing step 2. Staging wins by \$282k/yr because paying \$60k once for NVMe eliminates 76 of the 80 cross-region reads ($76 \times \$4.5\text{k} = \$342\text{k}$). Students who favor streaming look only at the \$60k NVMe line item without multiplying \$4.5k by 80 total epochs.}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item Tiers quote bandwidth at different scopes; 5\% misses at 100$\times$ cost 83\% of the rate.
    \item Required bandwidth is consumers $\times$ batch bytes $\div$ compute time, at the slowest stage.
    \item Shards amortize file opens; the metadata server saturates before the bytes do.
    \item A checkpoint writes each byte once, pauses for the local write, and counts at its manifest.
    \item Versioned, keyed writes make replay safe; price placement by the whole delivery bill.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    These five takeaways match each segment of the lecture: scoped tier bandwidth and the 5\% miss penalty, the input-demand equation at the slowest stage, sharding to prevent metadata meltdowns, write-once staged checkpoints committed by manifest, and versioned keyed writes priced by total delivery cost.
