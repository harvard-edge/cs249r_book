\begin{frame}{Six principles sit on the four bands of the fleet stack}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{principles_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Conclusion, \S17.2, figure 1 and table 1, redrawn at slide size.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{4 min}{Core}%
  The four bands of the fleet stack organize all six principles from bottom to top. In the physical infrastructure band, infrastructure determines capability through memory, power, storage, and bisection bandwidth, Lecture 3's minimum aggregate bandwidth across any cut splitting the cluster into two equal halves. In the distribution band, communication can dominate because transferred bytes need not shrink with fleet size $N$, and failure is routine, recalled from Lecture 7's 419 unexpected Llama 3 interruptions in 54 days. Above them, the serving band is bounded by its latency, availability, and cost contract. At the top, the governance band adds responsibility across security, privacy, robustness, and fairness, and sustainability, recalled from Lecture 15's 41$\times$ coal-versus-hydro carbon spread. Crossing Lecture 1's scale moment, marked by the red arrow, turns every principle into a fleet-wide constraint.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Band ordering shows which layer constrains which rather than ranking bottlenecks. Infrastructure is rarely the sole limiting constraint.}

  \notecue{Teach}{After crossing the red scale arrow, invite students to supply one anchor number for each remaining principle.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, revisit Lecture 1's Fleet Law to see how every principle spends one of its four terms.}
}
\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Those six principles need no separate step-time equations because every one spends terms of Lecture 1's Fleet Law (\S17.2, eq. 1), which returns unchanged. Infrastructure sets per-device compute rates ($C_1$) and link bandwidths ($C_2$). Exposed communication and barrier waits populate $T_{\text{comm}}(N)$ and $T_{\text{sync}}(N)$ without shrinking as fleet size $N$ grows. Failures land in the same terms. Synchronous checkpoint pauses, timeouts, and restarts add to amortized $T_{\text{sync}}$, whereas asynchronous checkpointing shifts part of that pause into $T_{\text{overlap}}$ and rollbacks add lost steps back to total work. Serving applies the same accounting per request, with queueing and routing entering $T_{\text{sync}}$. Responsibility and sustainability add no fifth term. Instead, verification spends compute, approvals spend coordination, and every second draws joules at average fleet power alongside external evidence such as subgroup error gaps, differential privacy (DP) budgets, and energy boundaries.

  \notecue{Next}{Next, test that Fleet Law mapping on a synchronous training job whose scaling efficiency collapses at scale.}
}
\mlsysfocus{The Fleet Law}{%
$\Tstep(N) = \underbrace{\dfrac{\Tcomp}{N}}_{C_1} + \underbrace{\Tcomm(N)}_{C_2} + \underbrace{\Tsync(N)}_{C_3} - \underbrace{\Tover}_{\text{hidden}}$\\[0.4cm]
{\normalsize Responsibility and sustainability add no term; they spend these.}%
}

\begin{frame}{A job that needs N GPUs is interrupted N times as often}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{mtbi_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]

  \mlsyscredit{Model: Conclusion, \S17.3 (MTTF 50,000 h per GPU, independent failures, GPU failures only). Measured: Dubey et al.\ 2024, \S3.3.4. Projection: Kokolis et al.\ 2025 via Fault Tolerance, \S7.2.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3.5 min}{Core}%
  The same scale compounding governs hardware reliability during synchronous training, where a single fault can interrupt the entire job. Under Lecture 7's independent GPU-only failure model with a mean time to failure (MTTF) of 50,000 h per GPU, mean time between interruptions (MTBI) falls as $50{,}000 / N$, giving 48.8 h at 1,024 GPUs, 24.4 h at 2,048 GPUs, and 3.05 h at 16,384 GPUs (all derived).   When the fixed-work reference job doubles from 1,024 to 2,048 GPUs while retaining the same network fabric (illustrated by the InfiniBand switch and cables) and checkpoint tier, each Fleet Law term responds differently. First, ideal compute time $T_{\text{compute}}/N$ halves at unchanged per-device speed. That 24.4 h interval is an optimistic upper bound because switches, NICs, and software add further faults, yet interruption frequency alone cannot reject the expansion before checkpoint overhead is priced.

\begin{frame}{Checkpoint overhead grows as $\sqrt{N}$, from 1.7\% to 2.4\%}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.86\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{overhead_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Overhead at the Young-Daly interval $\approx \sqrt{2\delta / M}$:\enspace $\delta$ = 24.5 s write, $M$ = time between interruptions}
  \mlsyscredit{Conclusion, \S17.3 (2.45 TB checkpoint, 24.5 s write; Young 1974, Daly 2006). Curve and points derived.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  To price recovery, apply Lecture 7's Young-Daly optimal checkpointing formula using Lecture 7's 24.5 s write time for Lecture 4's 2.45 TB checkpoint. At the optimal interval, wall-clock overhead is approximately $\sqrt{2\delta / M}$, where $\delta$ is the write time and $M$ is the mean time between interruptions. At 1,024 GPUs ($M = 48.8\text{ h}$), overhead is 1.7\% (derived). Doubling to 2,048 GPUs ($M = 24.4\text{ h}$) raises overhead only to 2.4\% while shortening the checkpoint interval from about 49 to about 35 minutes (derived). Because $M \propto 1/N$, overhead grows as $\sqrt{N}$, increasing by $1.41\times$ per doubling. Because overhead also scales as $\sqrt{\delta}$, cutting it in half requires a 4$\times$ faster write path.

  \notecue{Watch for}{These numbers count GPU failures only and assume checkpoint writes do not contend with data loading.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, calculate the break-even step speedup and accelerator-hour impact of doubling to 2,048 GPUs.}
}
\end{frame}

  From those 1.7\% (1,024 GPUs) and 2.4\% (2,048 GPUs) overheads, fixed-work run time scales as $T_{\text{step}}(N) / (1 - \text{overhead})$, and accelerator-hours equal GPUs $\times$ run time. Calculate the break-even step speedup and accelerator-hour change at an ideal $2\times$ speedup first.

  \notecue{Check}{Break-even step speedup is $(1 - 0.017) / (1 - 0.024) = 1.007\times$ (rounded to 1.01 in the chapter, derived), showing that recovery alone does not block expansion despite the tempting worry about doubled failures. Accelerator-hours multiply by $2 \times 1.007 / \text{speedup}$, so an ideal $2\times$ speedup still adds $+0.7\%$ accelerator-hours (derived). Saving accelerator-hours would demand a superlinear speedup $> 2.014\times$. Doubling buys calendar time with extra accelerator-hours and energy, and every lost point of scaling efficiency raises the cost directly.}

  \notecue{Watch for}{Apply the retained fraction $(1 - \text{overhead})$ once to step times excluding recovery, never again onto whole-run wall time.}

  \notecue{Teach}{Give pairs two minutes for both questions before revealing 1.007$\times$ and $+0.7\%$.}

  \notecue{Next}{Because recovery barely moves the bar, exposed communication sets the real bill; next, inspect measured speedup from a production 175B run.}
