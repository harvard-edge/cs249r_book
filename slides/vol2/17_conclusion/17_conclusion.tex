\begin{frame}{Six principles sit on the four bands of the fleet stack}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{principles_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Conclusion, \S17.2, figure 1 and table 1, redrawn at slide size.}
  \note{[3.5 min] NARRATE: the six principles of distributed ML systems, each condensing one part of the course. Step 1: the physical band. Infrastructure determines capability: memory, bisection bandwidth, power, and storage set the rates every higher layer inherits. The distribution band carries two: communication can dominate, because the exchange of gradients and activations need not shrink as N grows, and failure is routine, because a job that needs every component at once meets interruptions often enough that recovery is normal operation. Step 2: serving is bounded by its contract; useful capacity is the work done within latency, availability, and cost targets. Step 3: the governance band carries responsibility (security, privacy, robustness, fairness) and sustainability (energy and carbon). The red arrow is the scale moment, the premise of the course: crossing it turns each principle from a local concern into a fleet-wide constraint. ENGAGE: which principle would you have named first in week one? (Expected: most students name infrastructure; the course's argument is that the other five bind as often.) WARN: the band order shows which layer constrains which; it does not rank bottlenecks. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{[2 min] [CORE] NARRATE: the Fleet Law from Lecture 1, unchanged (Conclusion, \S17.2, equation 1). Infrastructure sets the per-device rates behind the compute term and the link bandwidths behind the communication term. Communication and waiting fill the second and third terms, and neither is guaranteed to shrink as N rises. Failure lands in the same terms: checkpoint pauses, detection timeouts, and restarts add to the synchronization term averaged over a run; asynchronous checkpointing moves part of that into overlap; a rollback adds lost steps back to the work. Responsibility and sustainability add no new term but spend the existing ones: verification occupies compute, approval gates add coordination, and every second of step time costs joules at the fleet's average power. Their acceptance also needs evidence the law cannot supply: subgroup error rates, privacy accounting, energy over a stated boundary. ENGAGE: which term does a checkpoint pause land in? (Expected: synchronization, or overlap if asynchronous.) FLEX: [CORE].}

\begin{frame}{How often does a 100-server request run slow?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{2.5 min}
  \centering
  {\normalsize A request fans out to 100 servers and waits for all of them.\\Each server is beyond its p99 latency 1\% of the time, independently.}\\[0.45cm]
  {\large\choice{A}{1\%}\qquad\choice{B}{10\%}\qquad\choice{C}{37\%}\qquad\answerat{2}{D}{63\%}}\\[0.45cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize $1 - 0.99^{100} = 63.4\%$ of requests wait on at least one slow server (derived).}
  \mlsyscredit{Conclusion, \S17.2 and footnote 2; Dean and Barroso 2013. p99: the 99th-percentile latency.}
  \note{[2.5 min] ENGAGE: write a guess, then vote. Most students pick A, reasoning that a 1 percent event stays a 1 percent event. Step 2: every server must be fast for the request to be fast. The chance all 100 are fast is 0.99 to the power 100, 36.6 percent, so 63.4 percent of requests wait on at least one slow server (derived; independent delays). C is the trap of answering the complement. A latency that is rare for one server becomes the common case for the request. WARN: shared congestion correlates server delays, so a real fleet can sit above or below this figure; the independence assumption must be checked against traces (\S17.2, footnote 2). LINK: Lecture 10 owns tail latency under fan-out and state-aware routing. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Checkpoint overhead grows as $\sqrt{N}$, from 1.7\% to 2.4\%}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.86\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{overhead.pdf}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Overhead at the Young-Daly interval $\approx \sqrt{2\delta / M}$:\enspace $\delta$ = 24.5 s write, $M$ = time between interruptions}
  \mlsyscredit{Conclusion, \S17.3 (2.45 TB checkpoint, 24.5 s write; Young 1974, Daly 2006). Curve and points derived.}
  \note{[2.5 min] NARRATE: the Young-Daly interval balances the time spent writing checkpoints against the work redone after a failure. At that interval, checkpoint-plus-rework overhead is about the square root of twice the write time over the interval between interruptions. The 2.45 TB checkpoint takes 24.5 s to write. At 1,024 GPUs, with 48.8 hours between interruptions, overhead is 1.7 percent of wall-clock time; at 2,048 GPUs, 2.4 percent (derived). Because the interval falls as 1/N, overhead grows as the square root of N: each doubling multiplies it by 1.41. The interval itself shrinks from about 49 to about 35 minutes (derived). LINK: Lecture 7 owns the Young-Daly interval; Lecture 4 owns the checkpoint write path. ENGAGE: what would halve this overhead? (Expected: a write four times faster, since overhead goes as the square root of write time.) WARN: these figures count GPU failures only and assume the write does not contend with data loading. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What speedup must 2,048 GPUs reach to pay off?}
  \mlsysactivity{Estimate}{3.5 min}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \begin{mlsyscard}{mlsysaccent}\small
        Overhead: 1.7\% at 1,024; 2.4\% at 2,048\\[0.1cm]
        Run time $\propto \Tstep(N) / (1 - \text{overhead})$\\[0.1cm]
        Accelerator-hours $=$ GPUs $\times$ run time
      \end{mlsyscard}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      \small
      1. Break-even step speedup?\enspace\uncover<2->{$\dfrac{1 - 0.017}{1 - 0.024} \approx$ \textbf{1.007$\times$}}\\[0.3cm]
      2. Accelerator-hours at an ideal 2$\times$?\\[0.1cm]
      \uncover<3->{\hspace*{1em}$2 \times \dfrac{1.007}{2}$: \textbf{+0.7\%} accelerator-hours}\\[0.3cm]
      \uncover<4->{Recovery does not block the expansion. Short of a superlinear speedup, doubling never saves accelerator-hours.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Conclusion, \S17.3 (decision rule). Values derived in images/calcs.py.}
  \note{[3.5 min] ENGAGE: pairs, two minutes, both questions. Step 2: for fixed work, run time is proportional to step time over the retained fraction, one minus overhead. The larger cluster shortens the run only if its measured step speedup exceeds the ratio of retained fractions: 0.983 over 0.976, about 1.007, which the chapter rounds to 1.01 (derived). Recovery alone does not block the expansion. Step 3: accelerator-hours are GPUs times run time. Doubling the GPUs multiplies them by 2 times 1.007 divided by the achieved speedup. Even at the ideal 2 times, they rise 0.7 percent (derived). Step 4: so the expansion buys calendar time with accelerator-hours and energy, and every point of scaling efficiency lost to exposed communication raises that price. \begin{frame}{Takeaways}
