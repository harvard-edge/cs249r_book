  \mlsyscredit{Photo: foam from Elgaland Vargaland, CC BY-SA 2.0, Wikimedia Commons (not the crash vehicle). Timeline: NTSB HAR-19/03, \S1.5.6.1 and ADS data table; Robust AI, \S14.2.2.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  In March 2018 in Tempe, Arizona, an Uber self-driving test vehicle, a modified Volvo XC90 (the photo shows the vehicle class, not the crash car), struck and killed a pedestrian, documented in the National Transportation Safety Board's final investigation report (NTSB HAR-19/03, 2019). The perception stack, which detects and classifies objects from sensors, spotted her 5.6 seconds before impact, yet oscillated among vehicle, other, and bicycle, discarding tracking history on each flip. The system recognized imminent collision 1.2 seconds before impact, or 4.4 s after detection (derived; the May 2018 preliminary report gave 1.3 s). It then suppressed braking for 1.0 second without alerting the operator and began gradual braking 0.2 seconds before impact. Every component ran on time without error.

  \notecue{Watch for}{This failure was not a missed detection. The pedestrian was detected 5.6 seconds out, and the failure lay in unstable classifications and suppressed fallback while dashboards stayed green.}

  \notecue{Teach}{Present this case plainly because a pedestrian died.}

  \notecue{Next}{Organize silent failures into three robustness pillars plus software defects that imitate them.}
}

\begin{frame}{Three failure classes keep every health signal green}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=6.0cm,keepaspectratio]{pillars_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{After Robust AI, \S14.3.2 and figure 2.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  A green-dashboard failure can come from the world, an attacker, or our own machinery, so robustness engineering separates silent failures into three pillars by their source and evidence. Environmental shift covers snow, new scanners, or changing users, evidenced by held-out subgroup slices (subsets such as weather conditions or user groups), drift statistics, and observed outcomes. Input-level attacks cover crafted stickers or training poison, evidenced by threat-matched attack suites. System-level faults cover hardware or code computing wrong values, extending Lecture 7's fault classes to silent errors; evidence comes from replay, canaries (fixed test inputs with known outputs), and live telemetry. Software and pipeline bugs originate as system faults yet cut across all three pillars by imitating shift or attacks. A preprocessing bug moves feature statistics like covariate shift (a change in input distribution), whereas a flipped weight bit requires range checks or canaries rather than drift monitors.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Replication gives zero protection against shared model weaknesses, because a thousand healthy replicas reproduce the same wrong answer together.}

  \notecue{Next}{Estimate how often independent silent hardware errors strike a 10,000-accelerator fleet.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three shifts change three different probabilities}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{shift_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\footnotesize $x$: the input.\quad $y$: its label.\quad $P(y \mid x)$: the labeling rule. The shift taxonomy names what moved.}
  \mlsyscredit{Redrawn from Robust AI, figure 3 (illustrative points).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Choosing the right response to environmental shift starts by identifying which probability moved, because each shift calls for a different remedy. Under covariate shift, inputs $P(x)$ move into a new region (shown in amber) while the labeling rule $P(y \mid x)$ and its decision boundary stay fixed, as with snow on a stop sign; the remedy is reweighting existing samples or collecting data in the new region. Under label shift, class frequencies $P(y)$ change while each class still looks similar, as in hospital respiratory case mix during COVID-19; the remedy is re-estimating class priors and recalibrating predicted probabilities. Under concept drift, the labeling rule $P(y \mid x)$ itself moves, as with evolving fraud tactics or new loan-repayment policies for unchanged applicants. Points between the old and new boundaries (shown in red) become errors, and fixing concept drift requires fresh labels, making it the costliest shift.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Input monitors deployed across fleets (Lecture 12) watch $P(x)$ only and stay blind when $P(y \mid x)$ shifts.}

  \notecue{Next}{Measured ImageNet-V2 results show how even an unintentional shift drops accuracy across every model.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A rebuilt ImageNet test set cost every model 7 to 14 points}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{imagenet_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\footnotesize Same collection protocol, a decade later. Every point lies below the dashed line.}
  \mlsyscredit{Taori et al. 2020 testbed, ImageNet-V2 from Recht et al. 2019; models with at least 55\% on ImageNet. Robust AI, figure 4.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Even a shift meant to be no shift at all erodes accuracy. In 2019, Recht et al. rebuilt the ImageNet validation set as ImageNet-V2 by following the original collection and labeling protocol a decade later to test whether benchmark gains hold on fresh data. Taori et al. (2020) assembled a larger distribution-shift testbed evaluating 212 models across both sets. Every model falls below the dashed equality line, losing 7 to 14 percentage points with a median drop of 12 points. From Lecture 0's 2012 AlexNet (about 57\% to 43\%) to ResNet-50 (about 76\% to 63\%), stronger models stay stronger along the straight trend line, yet no architecture or training recipe removes the gap. Held-out test accuracy is therefore an optimistic upper estimate on future data rather than a guarantee.

  \notecue{Watch for}{No obvious visual corruption like snow separates ImageNet from ImageNet-V2, yet subtle shifts still cost double-digit accuracy.}

  \notecue{Next}{Predict which monitoring signal catches pure concept drift first.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict: which monitor fires under pure concept drift?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{1.5 min}
  \begin{columns}[c]

      {\small $P(x)$ is unchanged. $P(y \mid x)$ moved. Which signal shows it first?}\\[0.25cm]
      \small
      \choice{A}{Population stability index on every input feature}\\[0.16cm]
      \choice{B}{A two-sample KS test on every input feature}\\[0.16cm]
      \answerat{2}{C}{Error on recent predictions, once labels arrive}\\[0.16cm]
      \choice{D}{Latency and error-rate dashboards}\\[0.25cm]
      \uncover<2->{\footnotesize Input monitors stay silent; outcome evidence waits for labels.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.39\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{concept_drift_mini.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Robust AI, Definition 14.2 and \S14.4.1 (Widmer and Kubat 1996).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  Because held-out accuracy cannot guarantee live performance, fleets run drift monitors. Recall the population stability index (PSI) from Lecture 12, which compares binned feature shares against a baseline. The two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test compares empirical cumulative distribution functions (CDFs, the fraction of samples at or below each value). Under pure concept drift, $P(x)$ stays fixed while $P(y \mid x)$ moves. Before reading on, choose which signal among A, B, C, and D reveals it first.

  \notecue{Check}{The answer is C, error on recent predictions once labels arrive. Tempting answers are A and B because teams often equate drift detection with input statistics, yet unchanged inputs keep both tests quiet. Option D stays green because latency and error-rate dashboards check operational health rather than prediction correctness. Only comparing predictions against ground-truth outcomes exposes pure concept drift, often after weeks of label delay in fraud detection. Proxy metrics can trigger containment, yet cannot confirm concept drift without labels.}

  \notecue{Teach}{Collect votes on A through D before revealing C.}

  \notecue{Next}{Compute a z-test to separate a real statistical shift in $P(x)$ from routine sampling noise.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimate: is a 0.05 shift in the mean real?}
  \mlsysactivity{Estimate}{2 min}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
      \begin{mlsyscard}{mlsysaccent}
        \small\textbf{Given: one input feature}\\[0.05cm]
        Baseline mean 0.50, $\sigma = 0.3$\\
        Last 1,000 requests: mean 0.55
      \end{mlsyscard}
      \small
      \wstep{2}{$0.05 / 0.0095 = \mathbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{5.3}}$ standard errors; $p < 0.001$}
      \uncover<2->{\codebox{se = 0.3 / 1000**0.5\ \# 0.0095\\ z = (0.55 - 0.50) / se\ \# 5.3}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      \centering\uncover<2->{\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{meantest.pdf}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Robust AI, Napkin Math 14.1 (standard error 0.0095 and 5.3 derived; the chapter rounds to 0.009).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  When an input monitor moves, first test whether the shift exceeds sampling noise. A feature with baseline mean 0.50 and $\sigma = 0.3$ reaches 0.55 over 1,000 requests. Before reading on, compute how many standard errors separate 0.55 from 0.50 and judge whether 0.05 is noise.

  \notecue{Check}{The standard error of the sample mean is $0.3 / \sqrt{1{,}000} = 0.0095$ (derived; rounded to 0.009 in the chapter). Dividing 0.05 by 0.0095 places the shift at $z = 5.3$ standard errors ($p < 0.001$), assuming a known baseline and independent requests. A tempting reaction is to dismiss ``only a 10\% move'' by eye, which ignores sample size. Across 1,000 independent requests, 0.05 lies over five standard errors from chance.}

  \notecue{Watch for}{Statistical significance does not prove operational harm, because tiny harmless shifts test significant at high volume. Correlate alerts with precision, recall, and business metrics, using Bonferroni correction (tighter cutoffs across many features).}

  \notecue{Teach}{Give one minute to work the estimate before revealing the calculation and plot.}

\begin{frame}{Randomized smoothing certifies a radius at a sampling cost}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
  Because empirical attack tests cannot prove that no bypass exists, Cohen et al. (ICML 2019) introduced randomized smoothing to certify a mathematical radius on deep classifiers. The method classifies many copies of an input corrupted with Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ and returns the majority class with probability $p_A$. Using the inverse standard normal cumulative distribution function $\Phi^{-1}$, at $\sigma = 0.5$ and $p_A = 0.999$ it certifies that no Euclidean ($\ell_2$) perturbation inside radius $R = \sigma\Phi^{-1}(p_A) = 0.5 \times 3.09 \approx 1.55$ changes the prediction. That proof guarantees prediction stability rather than ground-truth accuracy, and still falls short of the $32 \times 32 \times 3$ per-pixel maximum ($\ell_\infty = 8/255$) box corner at $8/255 \times \sqrt{3{,}072} = 1.74$ (derived). Because certification needs many forward passes per input, fleets run it offline on traffic samples or only on safety-critical triggers such as braking.

Once the yellow trigger appears, stop signs flip to speed limit 90.3\% of the time, and in an outdoor test a real Post-it note on a stop sign read speed limit at 95\% confidence. \begin{frame}{Poisoning defense runs in four layers along the data path}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.05cm,keepaspectratio]{layers_step}{5}\\[0.05cm]
  {\footnotesize Huber loss grows linearly past a threshold, so one extreme residual cannot dominate the gradient.}
  \mlsyscredit{Robust AI, \S14.7 (four layers; figures 11 to 13); Lighthouse 14.1 (Archetype B). Lecture 13: breakdown point of the gradient mean.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Because every poisoning detector has a blind spot, defense runs in four layers plus a lineage loop across five stages. First, ingress controls restrict write permissions, stamp batch identities, and watch interaction patterns to catch fake-account click rings in Archetype B recommenders, though compromised trusted sources remain a blind spot. Second, sanitization runs outlier, clustering, and spectral (feature-covariance) checks, leaving clean-label poisons (corrupted records with valid-looking labels) as a blind spot. Third, robust training uses Huber or trimmed losses (which cap or drop extreme residuals) and replaces Lecture 13's zero-breakdown gradient mean with coordinate-wise median, trimmed-mean, or Krum aggregation, shifting cost onto $T_{\text{comm}}$ through AllGather (collecting each worker's full gradient). Fourth, release checks run triggers, canaries, and target slices to close the BadNets gap. Finally, lineage records batch provenance so poisoned sources can be traced and removed, completing Segment 4's threat-matched defense budget.

  \mlsyscredit{Robust AI, \S14.9.1 and figure 14 (bit 30 of an FP32 word; derived from the IEEE 754 layout). Photo: Mister rf, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Recall from Lecture 7 that in the Llama 3 run, 6 of 419 interruptions were caught silent data corruptions. Where Lecture 7 covered training recovery, serving faces persistent silent impact. An FP32 weight of 0.042 holds 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, and 23 mantissa bits. Flipping bit 30, the top exponent bit, multiplies it by $2^{128}$ to yield a finite $1.4 \times 10^{37}$, passing NaN and infinity checks. ECC memory (Lecture 7) catches DRAM flips yet misses silent logic flips, and a dense 175B model reads all of its weights, including that corrupted one, on every request. Three behavioral checks trade off along the $C^3$ axes. Range checks compare weights against training bounds at compute cost yet miss in-range flips, golden canaries replay fixed requests per replica at compute and communication cost, cross-replica comparison doubles sampled work, and deciding which replicas to trust adds coordination cost.

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.18cm}
    \item Shift, attacks, poisoning, and faults keep health signals green; claim robustness per failure class.
    \item A drift alert opens an investigation; labels and a root cause justify a model update.
    \item Answering fewer requests lowers error only if fallback capacity absorbs the rest.
    \item Attacker access sets the test; defenses cost training passes or serving samples.
    \item Read release timing, replay, and telemetry before choosing a defense.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Together, the five takeaways answer the driving question across all five segments. Claim robustness per failure class, wait for labels and root cause before retraining on drift alerts, bound selective abstention by fallback capacity, match adversarial defenses to attacker access and compute budgets, and check release timing, replay, and telemetry before buying a fix.

  \notecue{Teach}{If short on time, emphasize the first and fifth takeaways.}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3cm}
    \item A fraud model changes how fraudsters behave, which shifts the data it will be retrained on. How should monitoring change when the model causes its own drift?
    \item Self-supervised pretraining on uncurated web data may resist shortcuts and widens the poisoning surface. Is the trade worth it?
    \item The top CIFAR-10 defense fails on a quarter of attacked inputs after six years of work. Where should a fleet spend instead: model, sensor, or system?
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Optional}%
  Three open questions push beyond settled engineering recipes. The first asks how to monitor drift when a fraud model reshapes attacker behavior, an instance of sociotechnical feedback (a deployed model changing the human environment it observes) that Lecture 16 develops. The second weighs self-supervised pretraining (learning representations from unlabeled data, such as predicting masked tokens or matching views, before task fine-tuning) on uncurated web data against a wider poisoning surface (\S14.7.4).
