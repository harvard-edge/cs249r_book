\begin{frame}{One neuron is a dot product followed by an activation}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{book_perceptron.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \normalsize
      \uncover<1->{$z_j = \sum_i x_i\, w_{ij} + b_j$}\\[0.35cm]
      \uncover<2->{$\hat{y}_j = f(z_j)$}\\[0.5cm]
      \uncover<3->{\textbf{Cost:} $n$ inputs $\rightarrow$ $n$ MACs\\[0.1cm]
      {\small\textcolor{midgray}{784 for a first-layer unit}}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{[2 min] The perceptron (Rosenblatt 1958). Step 1: each input is multiplied by its weight and the products are summed, plus a bias that shifts the threshold. That is a dot product. Step 2: an activation function f turns the sum into the neuron's output. Step 3: the machine's view. A neuron with n inputs costs n MACs; a first-layer unit in the MNIST network weighs all 784 pixels, so 784 MACs. Without reuse, each MAC reads two values (an input and a weight), which is why moving data, not multiplying, often limits speed. ENGAGE: how many MACs do the 128 first-layer units need together? Expected: 784 times 128, 100,352. WARN: the biological analogy is only an analogy; nothing here behaves like a real neuron.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two ReLU units compute XOR; no single weighted sum can}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \centering\normalsize
      \begin{tabular}{@{}cc|cc|c@{}}
        \toprule
        $x_1$ & $x_2$ & \uncover<2->{$h_1$} & \uncover<2->{$h_2$} & \uncover<3->{$y$} \\
        \midrule
        0 & 0 & \uncover<2->{0} & \uncover<2->{0} & \uncover<3->{\textbf{0}} \\
        0 & 1 & \uncover<2->{1} & \uncover<2->{0} & \uncover<3->{\textbf{1}} \\
        1 & 0 & \uncover<2->{1} & \uncover<2->{0} & \uncover<3->{\textbf{1}} \\
        1 & 1 & \uncover<2->{2} & \uncover<2->{1} & \uncover<3->{\textbf{0}} \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}\\[0.3cm]
      {\small $h_1 = \text{ReLU}(x_1{+}x_2)$, \ $h_2 = \text{ReLU}(x_1{+}x_2{-}1)$, \ $y = h_1 - 2h_2$}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.45\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=3.4cm,keepaspectratio]{book_nonlinear.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{[1.5 min] [OPTIONAL] Why a network needs the activation at all. XOR outputs 1 when its inputs differ. No single weighted sum of x1 and x2 can produce 0, 1, 1, 0; Minsky and Papert proved this in 1969. Step 2: two hidden ReLU units (ReLU passes positive values and zeroes negatives). Step 3: the output h1 minus twice h2 gives exactly XOR; on input (1,1) the second unit cancels the first. The figure on the right shows the same idea on data: without a nonlinearity, any stack of layers collapses to one linear map and draws a straight boundary. WARN: students who know linear algebra may say "but it has three layers". Three linear layers multiply into one matrix; the nonlinearity is what stops the collapse.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict: how much gradient survives 20 sigmoid layers?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{2 min}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.60\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{slopes_step}{2}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.37\textwidth}
      \normalsize
      Each layer multiplies the learning signal by the activation's slope.\\[0.4cm]
      \uncover<2->{{\large\bfseries\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{$0.25^{20} = 9.1\times10^{-13}$}}\\[0.25cm]
      {\small AlexNet: ReLU reached 25\% training error 6$\times$ sooner than tanh.}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Neural Computation, \S5.3.2 and footnote 1; AlexNet: Krizhevsky et al. 2012 (CIFAR-10, four-layer network).}
  \note{[2.5 min] Training adjusts each weight by its gradient, how much the error would change if the weight moved slightly. That signal travels backward and is multiplied at each layer by the slope of the activation (Section 4 of today does this by hand). Step 1: the sigmoid's slope, at most 0.25 at its center and near zero when the input is large in either direction (saturation). ENGAGE: after 20 sigmoid layers, at most how much of the signal is left? Give 30 seconds. Step 2: 0.25 to the 20th, about 9.1 times ten to the minus 13, before counting the weights. Early layers stop learning: the vanishing-gradient problem. ReLU's slope is exactly 1 for positive inputs, so that branch passes the signal unchanged. Published evidence: in the AlexNet paper, a network with ReLU reached 25 percent training error six times sooner than the same network with tanh. WARN: ReLU does not guarantee healthy gradients; weights still multiply in, and a unit whose input is always negative passes nothing and stops learning (a dead ReLU). Keep the number 9.1e-13; it returns in Section 4.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A sigmoid unit costs 50 times the transistors of a ReLU}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{transistor_tax_step}{2}\\[0.3cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize ReLU: a sign check and a select. Sigmoid: an exponential.}
  \mlsyscredit{Neural Computation, \S5.3.2: a stylized teaching ratio (illustrative), not a constant across manufacturing processes.}
  \note{[1.5 min] Small choices, large costs. Step 1: a ReLU in hardware is a comparator on the sign bit (is the number negative?) and a multiplexer (a switch that passes either the input or zero), about 50 transistor equivalents. Step 2: a sigmoid or tanh needs an exponential approximation, modeled in the chapter as about 2,500, fifty times more. The chapter calls this the transistor tax. WARN: the ratio is a teaching budget, not a measured constant; say "an order of magnitude or more". Two further costs: run as its own step, an activation reads and writes every value once for one cheap operation, so compilers fuse it into the preceding matrix multiply, applying it while the results are still on chip (Lecture 7); and ReLU's backward pass needs only one bit per unit, whether it was active. For students who know architecture: this is a datapath area and energy argument, and accelerators devote that area to multipliers instead (Lecture 11).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A layer is one matrix multiply; a batch reuses each weight}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.98\textwidth,height=4.7cm,keepaspectratio]{book_neuron_to_machine.pdf}\\[0.2cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize Layer 1 at batch 32: $(32{\times}784)\cdot(784{\times}128)$ = 3.2 million MACs.}
  \note{[2 min] Read the book figure left to right. Left: one neuron is a dot product of n inputs and n weights. Middle: stack all of a layer's neurons as the columns of a weight matrix W, and stack a batch of B examples as the rows of X; the layer becomes one matrix multiply, B times n-in times n-out MACs. Right: the hardware holds a tile of weights in an array of multiply-accumulate units while rows of X stream past, so each weight fetched from memory serves every example in the batch. \mlsysnote{[1.5 min] The counting rule for any dense layer, from the exercise. n-in and n-out are the layer's input and output widths, B the batch size. Parameters: one weight per connection plus one bias per output unit. FLOPs: two per MAC, one MAC per weight per example. Saved activations: training keeps each layer's output for every example in the batch. The consequence: widen both sides of a layer by 2 and its parameters and FLOPs go up 4 times (quadratic), but add a layer and they go up linearly. Ask students to copy the three lines; they will use them for the rest of the lecture and in Lectures 6 and 8.}
\mlsysfocus{Counting a dense layer}{%
  $\text{Parameters} = (n_{\text{in}} + 1)\, n_{\text{out}}$\\[0.35cm]
  $\text{FLOPs} = 2 \, B \, n_{\text{in}} \, n_{\text{out}}$\\[0.35cm]
  $\text{Saved activations} \propto B \, n_{\text{out}}$\\[0.5cm]
  {\small\textcolor{white!75}{Width costs quadratically; depth costs linearly.}}%
}

\begin{frame}{Width costs quadratically, depth costs linearly}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.82\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{book_depth_width_scaling.png}
  \mlsyscredit{Neural Computation, \S5.3.5, Figure 12 (computed from the layer equations).}
  \note{[1.5 min] [OPTIONAL] The focus-slide rule, plotted. Left: two hidden layers of equal width; doubling the width from 2,048 to 4,096 multiplies MACs per example by 3.4, not quite 4 because the 784-wide input layer only grows linearly. Right: width fixed at 512; doubling depth from 16 to 32 hidden layers multiplies MACs by 2.0 and saved activations by 1.9. The trade: width lands inside one matrix multiply that parallel hardware can spread out; depth adds a sequential step, more saved activations, and a longer path for the gradient. \section{Backpropagation}

\begin{frame}{Cross-entropy punishes confident mistakes hardest}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.86\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{book_cross_entropy_loss.png}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Loss $= -\log p_{\text{correct}}$: \ $p = 0.8 \rightarrow 0.22$, \ $p = 0.1 \rightarrow 2.30$}
  \note{[2 min] [OPTIONAL] The forward pass ends in 10 scores; softmax turns them into probabilities. Left: the network's probabilities for an image of a seven, against the true label, a one at seven and zeros elsewhere. The cross-entropy loss is minus the log of the probability on the correct digit. Right: at 0.8 the loss is 0.22; at 0.9, 0.11; a uniform guess over 10 digits gives log 10, about 2.30, which is where a freshly initialized network starts; a confident mistake, 0.1 on the true digit, also 2.30, and the curve keeps climbing toward zero probability. For the machine, the loss is nearly free, a few operations per score against 109,184 MACs per image. What must be protected is its range: log of zero is minus infinity, so libraries compute the loss directly from the scores in a stable fused form. The gradient of loss with respect to each score is simply predicted probability minus target. That number starts the backward pass.}
\end{frame}

Step 2: hidden unit 1: 1.0 times 0.5 plus 0.5 times 0.8 = 0.9, and ReLU keeps it. Hidden unit 2: 1.0 times minus 0.3 plus 0.5 times 0.2 = minus 0.2, and ReLU sets it to 0. \begin{frame}{Backward pass: every gradient reads a saved forward value}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      \centering

        \bpnet

        \node[bwdval, right=0.15cm of y] {$-0.46$};
        \uncover<2->{
          \node[bwdval, fill=white, inner sep=1pt] at (5.4,1.2) {$-0.414$};

        }

          \node[bwdval, fill=white, inner sep=1pt] at (3.6,1.22) {$\delta_1{=}{-}0.276$};
          \node[bwdval, fill=white, inner sep=1pt] at (3.6,0.78) {$\delta_2{=}0$};
        }
        \uncover<4->{

      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}

      \small
      \only<1|handout:0>{\textbf{1.} Output error: $\hat{y} - y = -0.46$}%
      \only<2|handout:0>{\textbf{2.} $\partial L/\partial W^{(2)} = a^{T}\cdot(-0.46)$\\[0.1cm]reads saved $a = [0.9,\ 0]$}%
      \only<3|handout:0>{\textbf{3.} Send back $-0.46 \cdot [0.6,\ -0.4]$\\$= [-0.276,\ 0.184]$\\[0.1cm]ReLU mask reads saved $z$:\\unit 2 was off, so $\delta_2 = 0$}%
      \only<4-|handout:1>{\textbf{4.} $\partial L/\partial W^{(1)} = x^{T}\delta$\\reads saved $x$:\\[0.1cm]

    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \note{[4 min] Same network, now backward; forward values are gray. Step 1: the gradient of the loss with respect to the output is prediction minus target, 0.54 minus 1.0, minus 0.46. Step 2: each output weight's gradient is the hidden activation times that error: 0.9 times minus 0.46 is minus 0.414 for the top weight, and 0 for the bottom, because unit 2's activation was 0. That needed the saved activation a. Step 3: the error sent back to the hidden layer is minus 0.46 times each output weight: minus 0.276 and 0.184. The ReLU mask then zeroes unit 2's share, because its input was negative in the forward pass; that needed the saved z (or one saved bit). Step 4: each first-layer weight's gradient is its input times the hidden error: 1.0 times minus 0.276 and 0.5 times minus 0.276, minus 0.138; unit 2's weights get 0. Gradient descent then moves each weight against its gradient: w11 = 0.5 minus 0.1 times minus 0.276 = 0.5276, nudging the output toward 1. ENGAGE: ask which forward value each step read. That is the systems lesson: the backward pass cannot run without the forward pass's values, so training keeps them in memory. WARN: backpropagation computes gradients; the optimizer applies the update. They are separate steps with separate memory. LINK: frameworks automate this bookkeeping (autograd, Lecture 7).}

\begin{frame}{Training holds 3.4 times the memory of inference}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25cm}
    \item A dense layer costs one MAC per weight per example; widths fix it.
    \item Matrix multiply is over 99\% of the work; batching reuses each weight.
    \item Backpropagation reads saved forward values; a step is about 3 forward passes.
    \item Training holds weights, gradients, optimizer state, and activations: 3.4$\times$ inference here.
    \item Networks fail by range or memory, not by logic.
  \end{itemize}
