      \normalsize
      \uncover<1->{$z_j = \sum_i x_i\, w_{ij} + b_j$}\\[0.35cm]
      \uncover<2->{$\hat{y}_j = f(z_j)$}\\[0.5cm]

  \end{columns}
  \note{[2 min] The perceptron (Rosenblatt 1958), drawn with three inputs; a real unit has as many inputs as the layer before it. Step 1: each input is multiplied by its weight and the products are summed, plus a bias that shifts the threshold. That is a dot product. Step 2: an activation function f turns the sum into the neuron's output. Step 3: the machine's view. A neuron with n inputs costs n MACs; a first-layer unit in the MNIST network weighs all 784 pixels, so 784 MACs. Without reuse, each MAC reads two values (an input and a weight), which is why moving data, not multiplying, often limits speed. ENGAGE: how many MACs do the 128 first-layer units need together? Expected: 784 times 128, 100,352. WARN: the biological analogy is only an analogy; nothing here behaves like a real neuron.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict: how much gradient survives 20 sigmoid layers?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{2 min}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.60\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{slopes_step}{2}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.37\textwidth}
      \normalsize
      Each layer multiplies the learning signal by the activation's slope.\\[0.4cm]
      \uncover<2->{{\large\bfseries\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{$0.25^{20} = 9.1\times10^{-13}$}}\\[0.25cm]
      {\small AlexNet: ReLU reached 25\% training error 6$\times$ sooner than tanh.}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Neural Computation, \S5.3.2 and footnote 1; AlexNet: Krizhevsky et al. 2012 (CIFAR-10, four-layer network).}
  \note{[2.5 min] Training adjusts each weight by its gradient, how much the error would change if the weight moved slightly. That signal travels backward and is multiplied at each layer by the slope of the activation (Section 4 of today does this by hand). Step 1: the sigmoid's slope, at most 0.25 at its center and near zero when the input is large in either direction (saturation). ENGAGE: after 20 sigmoid layers, at most how much of the signal is left? Give 30 seconds. Step 2: 0.25 to the 20th, about 9.1 times ten to the minus 13, before counting the weights. Early layers stop learning: the vanishing-gradient problem. ReLU's slope is exactly 1 for positive inputs, so that branch passes the signal unchanged. Published evidence: in the AlexNet paper, a network with ReLU reached 25 percent training error six times sooner than the same network with tanh. WARN: ReLU does not guarantee healthy gradients; weights still multiply in, and a unit whose input is always negative passes nothing and stops learning (a dead ReLU). Keep the number 9.1e-13; it returns in Section 4.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A sigmoid unit costs 50 times the transistors of a ReLU}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{transistor_tax_step}{2}\\[0.3cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize ReLU: a sign check and a select. Sigmoid: an exponential.}
  \mlsyscredit{Neural Computation, \S5.3.2: a stylized teaching ratio (illustrative), not a constant across manufacturing processes.}
  \note{[1 min] Step 1: a ReLU in hardware is a comparator on the sign bit (is the number negative?) and a multiplexer (a switch that passes either the input or zero), about 50 transistor equivalents. Step 2: a sigmoid or tanh needs an exponential approximation, modeled in the chapter as about 2,500, fifty times more. The chapter calls this the transistor tax. WARN: the ratio is a teaching budget, not a measured constant; say "an order of magnitude or more". Two further costs: run as its own step, an activation reads and writes every value once for one cheap operation, so compilers fuse it into the preceding matrix multiply, applying it while the results are still on chip (Lecture 7); and ReLU's backward pass needs only one bit per unit, whether it was active. Accelerators spend that area on multipliers instead (Lecture 11).}
\end{frame}

\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{[1.5 min] The counting rule for any dense layer, from the exercise. n-in and n-out are the layer's input and output widths, B the batch size. Parameters: one weight per connection plus one bias per output unit. FLOPs: two per MAC, one MAC per weight per example. Saved activations: training keeps each layer's output for every example in the batch. The consequence: widen both sides of a layer by 2 and its parameters and FLOPs go up 4 times (quadratic), but add a layer and they go up linearly. Ask students to copy the three lines; they will use them for the rest of the lecture and in Lectures 6 and 8.}

  $\text{Parameters} = (n_{\text{in}} + 1)\, n_{\text{out}}$\\[0.35cm]
  $\text{FLOPs} = 2 \, B \, n_{\text{in}} \, n_{\text{out}}$\\[0.35cm]
  $\text{Saved activations} \propto B \, n_{\text{out}}$\\[0.5cm]

\begin{frame}{Cross-entropy punishes confident mistakes hardest}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.86\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{cross_entropy.pdf}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Loss $= -\log p_{\text{correct}}$: \ $p = 0.8 \rightarrow 0.22$, \ $p = 0.1 \rightarrow 2.30$}
  \note{[2 min] [OPTIONAL] The forward pass ends in 10 scores; softmax turns them into probabilities that sum to 1. The true label is stored as a one-hot vector: ten entries, all zero except a one at the correct digit. Cross-entropy is minus the sum of label times log probability, and with a one-hot label only one term survives: minus the log of the probability the network gave the correct digit. Read the curve: at 0.9 the loss is 0.11; at 0.8, 0.22; a uniform guess over 10 digits gives log 10, about 2.30, which is where a freshly initialized network starts; a confident mistake, 0.1 on the true digit, also costs 2.30, and the curve keeps climbing toward zero probability. For the machine, the loss is nearly free, a few operations per score against 109,184 MACs per image. What must be protected is its range: log of zero is minus infinity, so libraries compute the loss directly from the scores in a stable fused form. The gradient of loss with respect to each score is simply predicted probability minus target. That number starts the backward pass.}
\end{frame}

  \note{[2 min] The running task, deployed. In 1989 LeCun and colleagues read handwritten ZIP-code digits with a network of about 10,000 parameters. It did not classify every digit: a confidence threshold on the top score sent the 12.1 percent it was least sure of to people, and the error on the remaining 87.9 percent was 1 percent. The plot is a risk-coverage curve: error among accepted digits against the fraction the machine accepts. Its shape here is a sketch; the one point is the paper's. Moving the threshold left accepts fewer digits, usually with fewer errors, and sends more work to reviewers. Step 2, the rule from the chapter (Chow 1970): pick the threshold that minimizes the review fraction times the cost of one review plus the error fraction times the cost of one wrong digit, where both fractions count against every arriving digit, and keep the review queue within what people can handle. ENGAGE: a misread check costs far more than a misrouted letter; which way does the threshold move for a bank? Expected: stricter, more review. WARN: softmax scores are not calibrated probabilities, and drift moves both counts, so the threshold and the review rate need monitoring (Lecture 14b).}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25cm}
    \item A dense layer costs one MAC per weight per example; widths fix it.
    \item Matrix multiply is over 99\% of the work; batching reuses each weight.
    \item Backpropagation reads saved forward values; a step is 2 to 3 forward passes.
    \item Training holds weights, gradients, optimizer state, and activations: 3.4$\times$ inference here.
    \item Without branches, a network's common failures are numerical range and memory.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{[1 min] One line per section. Ask students to star the one they would least be able to explain tomorrow and reread that section of the chapter first.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4cm}
    \item If hardware made exponentials free, would sigmoid come back?
    \item Storing activations saves arithmetic; recomputing them saves memory. When would you pay the arithmetic?
    \item A network reaches 99.5\% accuracy on its training batches and 85\% on held-out digits. Is more data the fix?
  \end{itemize}
