    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    Every training image costs the same arithmetic, so example value must be measured as learning gained per FLOP spent, called the information-compute ratio (ICR). On ResNet-50 over ImageNet, a full epoch of 1.28 million images at 24.6 GFLOP (billion FLOPs) per image costs $3.15\times 10^{16}$ FLOPs. With an assumed 5-point gain in epoch 1, that baseline returns $1.6\times 10^{-16}$ points per FLOP. Cutting to a 50 percent coreset ranked by Error L2-Norm (EL2N, defined in Section 3, where higher scores mark larger prediction errors and low scores mark easy examples to drop) cuts work to $1.57\times 10^{16}$ FLOPs. With an assumed 4.5-point gain (90 percent of the full gain), the coreset returns $2.9\times 10^{-16}$ points per FLOP, delivering 1.8 times the learning per FLOP for half a point less single-epoch gain.

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Raising learning per FLOP or diagnosing redundancy is still not enough unless the saved training time beats the cost of choosing the subset. This selection inequality is the lecture's central accounting rule. Selection pays only when selection time plus subset training time is strictly less than full-dataset training time, at matched output quality and deployment coverage (here $D_{\text{subset}}$ and $D_{\text{total}}$ are the retained and full dataset sizes).

  \notecue{Watch for}{Teams routinely omit $T_{\text{selection}}$ because data scoring runs before training starts and rarely appears on training dashboards.}

  \notecue{Next}{Test how scoring frequency changes this inequality on a 100-epoch ResNet-50 run.}
}
\mlsysfocus{The Selection Inequality}{%
  $\displaystyle T_{\text{selection}} + T_{\text{train}}(D_{\text{subset}}) \;<\; T_{\text{train}}(D_{\text{total}})$\\[0.6cm]
  {\small\textcolor{white!75}{at matched quality and coverage}}%
}

\begin{frame}{Rescoring every epoch eats a third of the saving}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.1cm,keepaspectratio]{scoreboard_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Selection, Example 9.2 and \S9.7.1. ResNet-50 on one H100 at 25\% of peak, 10\% coreset.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Applying the selection inequality to ResNet-50 for 100 epochs on ImageNet on one H100 at 25 percent of peak exposes what drives $T_{\text{selection}}$. Full-dataset training takes 211 minutes. Scoring the full pool once with ResNet-50 to keep a 10 percent coreset adds 42 seconds and saves 188.8 minutes, or 89.7 percent. Replacing the scorer with a smaller, faster ResNet-18 proxy model trims scoring to 18 seconds, adding only 24 extra seconds of savings for 189.1 minutes saved. By contrast, rescoring the whole pool before each of the 100 epochs pushes scoring to 70 minutes, 3.3 times the 21 minutes of subset training, shrinking net savings to 57 percent and forfeiting 37 percent of the one-pass gain. Both scorer size and scoring schedule govern net savings.

    \notecue{Watch for}{A cheaper scorer saves only 24 seconds under single-pass selection. Schedule frequency dominates when scoring repeats every epoch.}

(2022) found a 61-word sentence starting ``by combining fantastic ideas, interesting arrangements'' 61,036 times in C4 (Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus, filtered from Common Crawl, a public web-crawl archive). Checking every pair across WebText's roughly 8 million pages would take $3.2\times 10^{13}$ comparisons. Broder (1997) invented MinHash to catch duplicate pages for the early web search engine AltaVista without that scan. Splitting text into overlapping three-word shingles turns comparison into set overlap. Applying 128 hash functions to every shingle and keeping each minimum builds a 128-number signature where two pages match at any position with probability equal to their Jaccard similarity (shared fraction of combined shingles). Slicing signatures into bands buckets pages matching across any full band so only colliding candidates are checked, replacing all-pairs comparisons with one 40 GB read (about 6 seconds) and 4.1 GB of signatures in host RAM.

    \notecue{Watch for}{MinHash runs on host CPUs while accelerators sit idle, so schedule deduplication offline rather than inside a GPU training job.}

    \notecue{Next}{See where MinHash sits inside a production web-filtering pipeline.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25cm}
    \item An example is worth the learning it returns per FLOP.
    \item Selection pays only if scoring plus subset training beats full training, at equal quality.
    \item Deduplicate first; rank the rest with a cheap proxy; keep a coverage floor.
    \item Label where the model is unsure, and compare against random labeling at the same budget.
    \item A FLOP saved becomes time saved only if storage and the host keep up.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    Together, these five rules answer the driving question from end to end, linking per-FLOP value, the selection inequality, static pruning under a coverage floor, budget-matched active labeling, and storage and host throughput.
