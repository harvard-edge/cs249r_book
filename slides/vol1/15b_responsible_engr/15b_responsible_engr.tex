\section{Joules to Tonnes}

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{0.5 min}{Core}%
  State today's core equation with units: operational carbon equals IT equipment energy (kWh) times PUE (facility energy over IT energy) times grid intensity (kg CO2e per kWh). Each factor is an independent engineering lever: model and processor set energy, building design sets PUE, and region and hour set grid intensity, while hardware manufacturing sits beside it as a separate embodied term.

  \notecue{Next}{Apply this equation to Google's measured median Gemini Apps text prompt.}
}
\mlsysfocus{Operational carbon}{%
  {\Large Carbon $=$ Energy $\times$ PUE $\times$ Grid intensity}\\[0.5cm]
  {\small\textcolor{white!75}{kWh at the IT equipment \quad facility / IT energy \quad kg CO$_2$e per kWh}}%
}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Build the book's constructed DLRM scenario (\S15.4.2) across four clicks, noting explicitly that it is an illustrative model rather than a measurement. First, 10 million users receiving 20 ranked items daily generate 200 million inferences per day. Second, at 10 ms of dedicated unbatched GPU time per request, daily load equals 2 million GPU-seconds, or 556 GPU-hours per day. First, retraining from scratch at GPT-3 scale re-spends 1,287 MWh and 552 tonnes CO2e per request, tying erasure directly back to Section 1's carbon math. On DLRM, batching requests into the next quarterly retraining costs \$3,200 yet leaves the old model live for up to 90 days (raising a legal timing question). Second and third, introduce SISA machine unlearning (Bourtoule et al., IEEE S\&P 2021): splitting training data into isolated shards lets teams retrain only the affected shard, running 4.63x faster on Purchase, 2.45x on SVHN, and 1.36x on ImageNet at a small accuracy cost.

    \notecue{Check}{Ask why SISA's speedup is smallest on ImageNet (1.36x). Expected answer: harder tasks require more data per shard model to preserve accuracy, forcing fewer, larger shards.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{Verifying that approximate unlearning truly erased a record's influence remains an open research problem (\S15.6).}

    \notecue{Next}{Examine DP-SGD, which bounds single-record influence mathematically during training rather than removing it afterward.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{DP-SGD clips each example's gradient, then adds noise}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=4.1cm,keepaspectratio]{dpsgd_mech_step}{4}\\[0.1cm]
  {\footnotesize Differential privacy: $\Pr[M(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[M(D') \in S] + \delta$ for any datasets $D, D'$ that differ in one record.\\Smaller $\varepsilon$: less any one record can change the output.}
  \mlsyscredit{Responsible Engineering, \S15.5.2, after Abadi et al., ``Deep Learning with Differential Privacy,'' CCS 2016.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Read the differential privacy inequality below the diagram: for any datasets $D, D'$ differing in one record, $\Pr[M(D) \in S] \le e^\varepsilon \Pr[M(D') \in S] + \delta$; smaller $\varepsilon$ yields stronger privacy (at $\varepsilon = 8$, $e^8 \approx 3{,}000\times$, a weak guarantee). Walk through DP-SGD (Abadi et al., CCS 2016) across four clicks: (1) compute per-example gradients before batch averaging; (2) clip each gradient's norm to bound $C$ so no outlier dominates; (3) sum clipped gradients and add Gaussian noise with standard deviation $\sigma C$ before updating weights; (4) track cumulative privacy spent with a privacy accountant and halt when the $\varepsilon$ budget runs out.

    \notecue{Check}{Ask why gradient clipping must happen before adding noise. Expected answer: noise scales with $C$; without bounding one example's gradient norm, no finite noise can conceal an unbounded outlier.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{Differential privacy bounds statistical influence under formal assumptions; it is not encryption.}

    \notecue{Next}{Inspect PyTorch Opacus code and see why per-example gradients create a steep memory bill.}
  }
\end{frame}
