\section{Distance}

\begin{frame}{Light in fiber covers only 200 km per millisecond}
  \centering
  {\normalsize$\displaystyle \text{Round trip}_{\min} = \frac{2 \times \text{distance}}{200{,}000\ \text{km/s}}$}\\[0.2cm]
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{rtt_measured_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{System Paradigms, equation 1. Measured: AWS inter-region median round trips over one week from us-east-1, cloudping.co, retrieved 2026-10-18; great-circle distances.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Even before a packet reaches a server, physics sets a round-trip floor. Light in optical fiber travels near 200,000 km/s, roughly two thirds of its speed in vacuum, and a remote call crosses that path twice. The chart first plots minimum propagation floors. The top bar puts a 1,500 km link at 15 ms, past the green 10 ms arm budget, while the 3,503 km hop to Oregon costs 35.0 ms (near the textbook's 3,600 km, 36 ms coast-to-coast floor). Overlaying measured AWS one-week medians from Virginia (us-east-1) shows extra delay from fiber detours and routers. Ohio takes 13.4 ms against a 4.8 ms floor ($2.8\times$), Oregon 68.1 ms against 35.0 ms ($1.9\times$), Ireland 68.1 ms against 54.6 ms, and Tokyo 148.3 ms against 108.7 ms.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Do not expect 5G cellular links or faster fiber transceivers to cure propagation delay. Higher link speed raises bandwidth while leaving propagation speed untouched, so even nearby Ohio misses a 10 ms sense-and-act control loop.}

    \notecue{Next}{Verify the one-line arithmetic behind that 15 ms top bar against the opening robot-arm prediction.}
  }
\end{frame}

\section{Memory}

\begin{frame}{Compute has outgrown memory bandwidth for decades}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{memory_wall_measured.pdf}\\[0.05cm]
  {\normalsize Over twenty years: peak FLOP/s about 60,000$\times$, DRAM bandwidth about 100$\times$.}
  \mlsyscredit{Data: Gholami et al., ``AI and Memory Wall,'' IEEE Micro 2024, Fig.\ 1 (server chips, normalized to each series' first point), redrawn; Wulf and McKee 1995.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Processors wait for bytes because arithmetic speed has outgrown memory delivery for decades. Plotting published server chips (orange), DRAM generations from graphics memory (GDDR3) to stacked HBM (HBM2E, blue), and chip-to-chip interconnect links (violet), each normalized to its start (Gholami et al., 2024), reveals the divergence. Over twenty years, peak compute grew roughly $3.0\times$ every two years ($60{,}000\times$ total), while DRAM bandwidth grew $1.6\times$ ($100\times$ total) and interconnects grew $1.4\times$ ($30\times$ total). Wulf and McKee (1995) named this widening gap the memory wall, predicting that processor-memory divergence would dominate runtime unless workloads reuse each fetched byte more often.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The textbook's conceptual illustration uses gentler annual rates ($1.6\times$ versus $1.2\times$ per year), whereas the measured server hardware gap shown here is steeper.}

\begin{frame}{Clock speeds stalled when power density hit its limit}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{microprocessor_trends.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
      \normalsize
      $P \propto C\,V^2 f$, and $V$ rises with $f$:\\[0.2cm]
      {\Large$P \propto f^3$}\\[0.4cm]
      \uncover<2->{{\Large\textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{2$\times$ clock $\approx$ 8$\times$ power}}}\\[0.4cm]}
      \uncover<3->{\small Transistors went into more cores and accelerators instead.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
    Why did hardware shift to parallel accelerators instead of faster single clocks? Fifty years of processor measurements (Rupp, 2022) show transistor counts (red) rising roughly $190\times$ after the mid-2000s, while clock frequency (green) flattened near 4.7 GHz and chip power (orange) leveled off. Dynamic switching power obeys $P \propto C V^2 f$, linking capacitance, voltage, and frequency. Because higher clocks demand higher voltage, $P$ grows roughly as $f^3$, so doubling clock speed costs roughly $8\times$ the power and heat. When Dennard scaling (the era when shrinking transistors lowered voltage and kept power density roughly constant) ended in the mid-2000s, that cubic penalty halted free clock speedups and pushed architects to spend extra transistors on parallel cores, GPUs, and TPUs.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The cubic $P \propto f^3$ rule assumes voltage scales linearly with frequency. Exact exponents vary by chip manufacturing generation, though the steep power penalty holds universally.}

    \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip this slide and state $P \propto f^3$ aloud over the phone throttling slide.}

\begin{frame}{Detecting the wake word on the device removes 32 TB/s}
  \centering
  {\small\textcolor{midgray}{1 billion devices $\cdot$ 16 kHz, 16-bit audio $=$ 32 KB/s each $\cdot$ 20 queries a day each}}\\[0.15cm]
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.9\textwidth,height=3.0cm,keepaspectratio]{voice_upload_step}{2}\\[0.25cm]
  \normalsize
  \uncover<3->{Streaming everything to the cloud: about \$500M a year.\\[0.15cm]}
  \uncover<4->{After the split: 1.1M GPU-hours a day, about 20.8 datacenters at peak.}
    \mlsyscredit{System Paradigms, Napkin Math 2.10 (the voice assistant wall). 37 GB/s derived from 32 TB/s and the chapter's 860$\times$.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    To see why running all stages in the cloud fails at scale, price that voice-assistant split across 1 billion devices. Continuous 16 kHz, 16-bit audio at 32 KB/s per microphone generates 32 TB/s nonstop. Moving wake-word detection onto the device lets each unit upload only five seconds per query across 20 queries/day, cutting traffic $860\times$ to roughly 37 GB/s. Cloud detection costs roughly \$0.50 per device per year, or \$500M/year, compared with under one cent locally at 0.1 to 1 mW. Even after the split, 20 billion daily queries at 200 ms of GPU time still need 1.1M GPU-hours/day. At 240,000 GPU-hours/day per 10,000-GPU datacenter, that equals 4.6 average datacenters, or 20.8 at a $4.5\times$ diurnal traffic peak (recalling Lecture 0's diurnal spikes).
