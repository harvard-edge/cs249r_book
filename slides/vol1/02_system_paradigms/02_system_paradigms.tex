\mlsysnote{[1.5 min] The key law for today. The iron law from Lecture 1, with the physical wall that sets each term on each tier. Data movement: the memory wall, compute outgrowing bandwidth. Compute: the power wall, because heat caps how fast a chip can run. Overhead: the light barrier, because a remote request pays the round trip. Below the equation: capacity and energy are checked first; a model that does not fit, or a battery that cannot pay, ends the discussion before any term is evaluated. \begin{frame}{Light in fiber covers only 200 km per millisecond}
  \centering
  {\normalsize$\displaystyle \text{Round trip}_{\min} = \frac{2 \times \text{distance}}{200{,}000\ \text{km/s}}$}\\[0.2cm]
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{rtt_measured_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{System Paradigms, equation 1. Measured: AWS inter-region median round trips over one week from us-east-1, cloudping.co, retrieved 2026-10-18; great-circle distances.}
  \note{[2 min] Light in glass travels at about 200,000 km/s, two thirds of its speed in vacuum. A request goes out and the answer comes back, so the distance counts twice. Step 1, the light bars: the floor for each path. The arm's cloud region 1,500 km away costs 15 ms of pure propagation; the green line is the arm's 10 ms budget. The other rows are real paths from the AWS region in northern Virginia; Oregon, 3,503 km away, is the chapter's coast-to-coast case (about 36 ms for 3,600 km). Step 2, the dark bars: the median round trip AWS actually measured between its regions over one week. Ohio: 13.4 ms against a 4.8 ms floor, 2.8 times. Oregon: 68.1 ms against 35.0, 1.9 times. \section{Memory}

\begin{frame}{Compute has outgrown memory bandwidth for decades}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{memory_wall_measured.pdf}\\[0.05cm]
  {\normalsize Over twenty years: peak FLOP/s about 60,000$\times$, DRAM bandwidth about 100$\times$.}
  \mlsyscredit{Data: Gholami et al., ``AI and Memory Wall,'' IEEE Micro 2024, Fig.\ 1 (server chips, normalized to each series' first point), redrawn; Wulf and McKee 1995.}
  \note{[2 min] Real chips, not assumed rates. Each dot is a published server processor, GPU, or TPU (orange), a DRAM generation (blue: GDDR3 through HBM2E), or an interconnect generation (violet: PCIe and NVLink), each series normalized to its own first point. The lines are least-squares fits on the logarithm, as in the paper. Peak compute grew about 3.0 times every two years, about 60,000 times over twenty years; DRAM bandwidth 1.6 times every two years, about 100 times; interconnect 1.4 times, about 30 times. The gap between orange and blue is the memory wall, which Wulf and McKee named in 1995. The consequence: each hardware generation makes data movement a larger share of time and energy, unless the workload reuses each byte more. The chapter's own illustration uses gentler rates (compute 1.6 times a year, bandwidth 1.2 times); the measured gap is wider. FLEX: [OPTIONAL]; if short, state the two growth rates in one sentence and go to the roofline.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The roofline: bytes or arithmetic caps performance}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{roofline_step}{3}\\[0.1cm]
  \normalsize
  \only<1|handout:0>{Arithmetic intensity $=$ operations $\div$ bytes moved.}%
  \only<2|handout:0>{The chip cannot exceed its peak: 312 TFLOP/s.}%
  \only<3-|handout:1>{Ridge $= 312 \div 2.04 = 153$ FLOP/byte. Left of it, bytes limit; right of it, arithmetic.}
  \mlsyscredit{NVIDIA A100, FP16: System Paradigms, Napkin Math 2.1; roofline model after Williams, Waterman, and Patterson 2009.}
  \note{[2.5 min] This is the roofline, the first time it appears in the book; Lecture 11 develops it fully. Explain the axes first: the horizontal axis is arithmetic intensity, the number of operations a workload performs for each byte it moves from memory; the vertical axis is the achieved rate, both on log scales. Step 1: if every byte arrives at 2.04 TB/s and each byte supports I operations, the rate is I times 2.04 TB/s. That is the blue slope. Step 2: the arithmetic units cannot exceed 312 TFLOP/s, the orange ceiling. Step 3: the two meet at the ridge point, peak divided by bandwidth, about 153 FLOP per byte for the A100. A workload left of the ridge is limited by memory bandwidth (the chapter says bandwidth-bound); right of it, by arithmetic (compute-bound). ENGAGE: ``A workload does 10 operations per byte. Which wall stops it?'' (Bandwidth; it reaches about 20.4 TFLOP/s, 6.5 percent of peak.) WARN: ``memory-bound'' here means bandwidth, not running out of capacity; the book keeps the two words separate. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

      {\Large$P \propto f^3$}\\[0.4cm]
      \uncover<2->{{\Large\textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{2$\times$ clock $\approx$ 8$\times$ power}}}\\[0.4cm]}
      \uncover<3->{\small Transistors went into more cores and accelerators instead.}

  \note{[1.5 min] Real data first. Explain the plot: fifty years of processors on a log scale. Red is transistors, green clock frequency, blue single-thread performance, orange power. In the mid-2000s (the gray band) green flattens near 4.7 GHz and orange flattens too, while red keeps climbing about 190 times. Step 1: the equation. Dynamic power is capacitance times voltage squared times frequency; a faster clock needs a higher voltage, so power grows roughly with the cube of frequency. Step 2: doubling the clock costs about eight times the power, all of it heat. Step 3: until the mid-2000s, Dennard scaling held: shrinking transistors let voltage fall with size, so power density stayed constant and clocks could rise for free.
