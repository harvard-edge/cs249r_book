\section{Distance}

\begin{frame}{Light in fiber covers only 200 km per millisecond}
  \centering
  {\normalsize$\displaystyle \text{Round trip}_{\min} = \frac{2 \times \text{distance}}{200{,}000\ \text{km/s}}$}\\[0.25cm]
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=3.9cm,keepaspectratio]{rtt_distance_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{System Paradigms, equation 1 and \S2.2. Real networks add routing, queuing, and processing on top.}
  \note{[2.5 min] Light in glass travels at about 200,000 km/s, two thirds of its speed in vacuum. A request goes out and the answer comes back, so the distance counts twice. Step 1: a regional datacenter 1,500 km away costs 15 ms of pure propagation. The green line is the arm's 10 ms budget. Step 2: California to Virginia, about 3,600 km, costs about 36 ms. These are floors: real networks add routing, protocol handshakes, and queuing, so measured round trips are larger. WARN: students think 5G or faster links fix this. Bandwidth improves; propagation does not. No engineering makes light faster. FLEX: [CORE].}

\begin{frame}{100 raw camera feeds need 15 times the factory's link}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.2cm,keepaspectratio]{camera_bandwidth_step}{3}\\[0.15cm]

  \mlsyscredit{System Paradigms, Napkin Math 2.4: 100 cameras, 1080p, 30 FPS, 3 bytes per pixel.}
  \note{[2.5 min] Distance is not the only cost of moving data; volume is the other. A factory runs quality control on 100 cameras at 1080p, 30 frames per second. Step 1: one camera produces 1920 times 1080 pixels times 3 bytes times 30 frames, about 186.6 MB/s; 100 cameras produce about 18.7 GB/s. \mlsysnote{[0.5 min] The second wall lives inside every machine: moving bytes from memory to the arithmetic units.}
\mlsysdivider{Why do fast processors sit idle waiting for bytes?}{System Paradigms, \S2.2, \S2.3, and \S2.4}
\section{Memory}

\begin{frame}{Compute has outgrown memory bandwidth for decades}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{memory_wall.pdf}
  \mlsyscredit{System Paradigms, \S2.2, equation 3 (illustrative rates); Wulf and McKee 1995.}
  \note{[2 min] Axes: years on the bottom, growth on a log scale. Orange: peak compute doubling about every 18 months, about 1.6 times a year. Blue: memory bandwidth growing about 20 percent a year. After a decade compute is about 102 times faster and bandwidth about 6 times faster, so compute is about 16 times further ahead. Wulf and McKee named this the memory wall in 1995. The consequence: each hardware generation makes data movement a larger share of time and energy, unless the workload reuses each byte more. WARN: these are the chapter's illustrative rates, not a forecast for any one product line. For students who know architecture: the same wall that motivated caches; ML adds workloads that touch every weight for every token. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The roofline: bytes or arithmetic caps performance}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{roofline_step}{3}\\[0.1cm]
  \normalsize
  \only<1|handout:0>{Arithmetic intensity $=$ operations $\div$ bytes moved.}%
  \only<2|handout:0>{The chip cannot exceed its peak: 312 TFLOP/s.}%
  \only<3-|handout:1>{Ridge $= 312 \div 2.04 = 153$ FLOP/byte. Left of it, bytes limit; right of it, arithmetic.}
  \mlsyscredit{NVIDIA A100, FP16: System Paradigms, Napkin Math 2.1; roofline model after Williams, Waterman, and Patterson 2009.}
  \note{[3 min] This is the roofline, the first time it appears in the book; Lecture 11 develops it fully. Build it slowly. Explain the axes first: the horizontal axis is arithmetic intensity, the number of operations a workload performs for each byte it moves from memory; the vertical axis is the achieved rate, both on log scales. Step 1: if every byte arrives at 2.04 TB/s and each byte supports I operations, the rate is I times 2.04 TB/s. That is the blue slope. Step 2: the arithmetic units cannot exceed 312 TFLOP/s, the orange ceiling. Step 3: the two meet at the ridge point, peak divided by bandwidth, about 153 FLOP per byte for the A100. A workload left of the ridge is limited by memory bandwidth (the chapter says bandwidth-bound); right of it, by arithmetic (compute-bound). ENGAGE: ``A workload does 10 operations per byte. Which wall stops it?'' (Bandwidth; it reaches about 20 TFLOP/s, about 7 percent of peak.) WARN: ``memory-bound'' here means bandwidth, not running out of capacity; the book keeps the two words separate. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

Two are terms of the iron law: compute-bound (ResNet-50 training) and bandwidth-bound (decode). Two are preconditions: capacity-bound (DLRM's embedding tables exceed one accelerator's memory) and power-bound (keyword spotting must run always on, on milliwatts). MobileNetV2 does the same task as ResNet-50 with 13.7 times fewer operations, sized for a phone's 3 to 5 W. WARN: the archetype is a regime, not a property of the model. ResNet-50 is compute-bound in batched training and bandwidth-bound at batch 1, as we just saw. ENGAGE: ``Which row changes if you serve ResNet-50 one image at a time?'' (Row 1 moves to bandwidth.) LINK: Lecture 6 opens up each architecture. FLEX: [OPTIONAL]; if short, name the five bindings aloud over the previous slide.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Clock speeds stalled when power density hit its limit}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{microprocessor_trends.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
      \normalsize
      $P \propto C\,V^2 f$, and $V$ rises with $f$:\\[0.2cm]
      {\Large$P \propto f^3$}\\[0.4cm]
      \uncover<2->{{\Large\textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{2$\times$ clock $\approx$ 8$\times$ power}}}\\[0.4cm]}
      \uncover<3->{\small Transistors went into more cores and accelerators instead.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{System Paradigms, equation 2 and fig.\ 2; data: Karl Rupp, Microprocessor Trend Data, 2022.}
  \note{[2.5 min] Real data first. Explain the plot: fifty years of processors on a log scale. Red is transistors, green clock frequency, blue single-thread performance, orange power. In the mid-2000s (the gray band) green flattens near 4.7 GHz and orange flattens too, while red keeps climbing about 190 times. Step 1: the equation. Dynamic power is capacitance times voltage squared times frequency; a faster clock needs a higher voltage, so power grows roughly with the cube of frequency. Step 2: doubling the clock costs about eight times the power, all of it heat. Step 3: that ended free speedups when Dennard scaling broke down, and the extra transistors went into many cores, GPUs, and TPUs. This is why ML hardware is parallel and specialized. WARN: the cube assumes voltage scales with frequency; real chips vary, but the direction holds. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A phone throttles once heat outruns its chassis}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{thermal_wall_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize A thin, fanless phone can shed only about 3 W, whatever its battery holds.}
  \mlsyscredit{System Paradigms, Napkin Math 2.9 (the thermal wall). Illustrative trace.}
  \note{[2.5 min] The power wall at phone scale. An unoptimized vision model draws 12 W; a fanless phone can remove only about 3 W of heat through its case. Step 1: the model starts at 100 frames per second and the chip warms at about 1 degree Celsius per second. Step 2: within about a minute it reaches its 80 degree trip point, the operating system lowers the clock, and throughput falls to about 30 frames per second, the rate 3 W can sustain. Benchmarks that run for ten seconds report the burst; users live on the sustained line. Suppose a redesign (lighter model, integer arithmetic) cuts power 4 times: 12 W becomes 3 W, exactly at the ceiling with no headroom. WARN: optimization can bring a model down to the ceiling; it cannot raise the ceiling. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.22cm}
    \item Four regimes, nine orders of magnitude in power: each tier binds a different constraint.
    \item Distance sets a floor no GPU can remove: 200 km of fiber per millisecond.
    \item When moving the data misses the deadline, move the computation to the data.
    \item Below the ridge point, bytes limit speed; batch-1 inference lives on the memory slope.
    \item Off the wall socket, energy per inference and a few watts of heat decide what runs.
    \item Screen feasibility first, compare cost last, and split pipelines across tiers.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{[1.5 min] One line per segment; ask students to circle the one they could not yet explain to a friend and reread that section. One more idea from the chapter's close: the same techniques serve every tier, because the walls are the same; moving fewer bytes helps a microcontroller and a datacenter alike. That is why the rest of the course applies everywhere.}
\end{frame}
