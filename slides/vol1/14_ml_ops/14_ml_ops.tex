\begin{frame}{The same model scores 4 to 18.5 points lower on later data}

    Zillow was one company, so the first question is whether frozen models lose accuracy broadly when only time passes. Every benchmark degrades, from FMoW satellite imagery falling 4 points to Yearbook portraits falling 18.5 points over decades. Nothing changed in the weights, code, or serving hardware. Accuracy fell solely because the world moved, echoing Google Flu Trends and the degradation equation from Lecture 1.

        A data source was copied to save resources; the pipeline lacked read permission on the copy and silently stopped learning. No model code changed.
      \end{mlsyscard}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Papasian and Underwood, ``How ML Breaks: A Decade of Outages for One Large ML Pipeline,'' USENIX OpML 2020, slide 21 (values read from bar chart; no table published).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3.5 min}{Core}%
    Lecture 0's technical-debt diagram was only a schematic sketch, so Papasian and Underwood (USENIX OpML 2020) tested it by auditing 96 written outage postmortems, or incident reports, across ten years of a large Google ML pipeline. Each outage was rated from 0 (not ML-specific) to 4 (entirely ML). Of the 96 outages, 61 rated 0 with no ML cause at all, and about 60 percent involved distributed-systems coordination such as joins, permissions, and scheduling. About 24 outages rated 4 as pure ML failures, with few cases in between. A real Google postmortem illustrates how the glue breaks. Copying a data source to save resources broke read permissions on the copy, so data collection silently stopped feeding new training examples while model code stayed unchanged. Reliability therefore requires shared ownership between platform engineers, who prevent the 61, and modelers, who own the 24.

    \notecue{Watch for}{These outage counts were read from the talk's published bar chart, because no numeric table was published.}

\begin{frame}{A corrected forecast cannot undo a purchase}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{cascade_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{After ML Operations, figure 4 and War Story 14.1. Zillow Group Q3 2021 shareholder letter (9,680 bought; 9,790 in inventory; 8,172 under contract; \$304 million).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Beyond broken infrastructure glue, hidden debt compounds through downstream coupling when one system's output becomes another's input. In a software correction cascade (Figure 4), Model B learns to patch Model A's errors and Model C patches Model B's, so updating Model A invalidates Models B, C, and D simultaneously and forces every downstream model to be revalidated. In Zillow Offers (War Story 14.1), that cascade crossed into physical assets. Forecasts generated purchase offers that became 9,790 owned homes and 8,172 contracts dependent on 3-to-6-month price horizons, so revising the model could not erase balance-sheet losses. Safeguards therefore belong between prediction and action, including exposure caps, manual purchase overrides, and uncertainty circuit breakers that pause buying when forecast error widens.

\mlsysdivider{Can we rebuild the serving model and prove what it computes?}{ML Operations, \S14.2.1 and \S14.4}
\section{Rebuild and Trace}

\begin{frame}{A model is a function of four versioned inputs and the rounding}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{repro_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{ML Operations, equation 1 and footnote 4 (DVC), \S14.2.1.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Versioning source code alone cannot rebuild a trained model, because weights depend on everything that ran during training. Equation 1 treats model output as a function of four pinned inputs. Those inputs are code (a Git commit), data (a content hash tracked with Data Version Control, or DVC, recalled from Lecture 4, the input teams most often omit and cannot later recover), configuration including hyperparameters (the training settings chosen before fitting), and the environment (a container image, a packaged snapshot of the runtime and pinned libraries). Dropping any single pin prevents exact reconstruction. Even with all four inputs pinned, parallel GPU floating-point additions are non-associative, meaning different reduction orders round differently and compound over thousands of steps. Deterministic kernels enforce exact order at a speed penalty, and the tolerated run-to-run variation band, called a reproducibility envelope, is left to reading in Benchmarking \S12.8.2.

    \notecue{Next}{Audit a real DVC lock file and devcontainer configuration to find which of the four inputs can still drift.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Exposure grows in stages: shadow, canary, then ramp}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.4cm,keepaspectratio]{release_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{ML Operations, \S14.5.3 and table 11; ranking service illustrative.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Knight Capital exposed live traffic to an unverified change all at once. Staged rollout instead limits blast radius by widening how much traffic a candidate model (v2) meets alongside the incumbent (v1) across three progressive stages. In a shadow deployment, the router mirrors live requests to v2 and logs its outputs without showing them to users, carrying zero user risk while yielding zero behavioral feedback such as click-through. Next, a 5 percent canary serves real users and compares click-through and p99 latency against v1, though small traffic shares remain subject to the sample-size noise from Lecture 12b. Finally, stepped ramping across 5, 25, 50, and 100 percent catches load-dependent problems invisible at 5 percent, such as p99 latency rising at 30 percent traffic, and reverts automatically if any check fails. Every stage requires running a full second copy of v2 alongside v1.

    Holding a hot standby makes switching fast, yet rollback complexity depends on how soon a fault appears and how much state has accumulated. Three tiers (Table 9, Checkpoint 14.2) match trigger to recovery. Immediate rollback switches traffic to a hot standby on crashes, rapid rollback redeploys a prior checkpoint (Lecture 8) from the registry when canary metrics drop, and delayed rollback redeploys and migrates state weeks later. State coupling explains why reverting weights alone fails. Under v2, the model weights and the embedding cache (stored precomputed user and item vectors) share v2's vector space. Reverting only weights to v1 leaves v2 vectors inside the cache alongside personalization profiles and logged features, corrupting predictions. A safe rollback instead restores a compatible bundle of weights, feature transform, and isolated cache namespace (a versioned key prefix per model) together, drains in-flight v2 requests before freeing memory, and quarantines degraded logs from future training.

On switchover, a hardware watchdog timer (a circuit that resets the chip if software hangs) and a self-test guard initial inferences. On any fault, hang, or latency violation, the startup bootloader (the small program choosing which bank runs) automatically reverts to Bank A. That safety costs 200 KB of flash, totaling 400 KB out of 4 MB, just as DLRM pays two H100 GPUs. Across Section 4, staged exposure, per-node verification, and rehearsed state-aware rollback each draw from the device's scarcest resource.

  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35cm}
    \item A registry promotes only artifacts with a signed feature contract and a named owner. Who signs, and what happens when the signer has left the team?
    \item Should a model that triggers irreversible purchases or trades ever act without an exposure cap? Who sets the cap?
    \item A canary doubles the accelerators that hold a large model. Is a shadow on 1 percent of traffic enough evidence? What can it not show?
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
    Even with those technical controls in place, three open tensions in production ML operations have no purely technical answer. They ask how organizations maintain registry contract ownership after signers leave a team, where ML operations ends and business exposure caps begin, and how to weigh shadow deployments carrying zero user feedback against full canary copies.
