\begin{frame}{Every step of the operations loop costs the machine something}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{oploop_step}{3}

\begin{frame}{A corrected forecast cannot undo a purchase}

  \note{[2 min] Step 1: a correction cascade, the book's pattern. Model B is trained to fix Model A's errors, C to fix B's, and so on. \begin{frame}{Automating retraining pays back in about 20 weeks}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.85\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{automation_step}{2}\\[0.2cm]
  \only<3|handout:0>{\mlsysactivity{Estimate}{2 min}\normalsize Your turn: 6 hours a week by hand; a 120-hour pipeline. Break-even? With 2 hours a week of upkeep?}%
  \only<4-|handout:1>{\mlsysactivity{Estimate}{2 min}\normalsize 120 / 6 = \textbf{20 weeks}. With upkeep, 120 / (6 $-$ 2) = \textbf{30 weeks}.}
  \mlsyscredit{ML Operations, Napkin Math 14.1 and its self-check. Upkeep lines derived: break-even = build hours / (manual hours $-$ upkeep hours).}
  \note{[3 min] Step 1: Napkin Math 14.1. Retraining by hand takes 4 engineering hours a week; an automated pipeline takes 80 hours to build. Manual work accumulates; the pipeline is paid once. \begin{frame}{Only a request-keyed replay proves two pipelines agree}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.1cm,keepaspectratio]{parity_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<3->{\small At 1 percent skew, 1 million queries a day, and \$0.10 an error: \$365,000 a year.}
  \mlsyscredit{ML Operations, \S14.3.1 (illustrative ranking-service case) and equation 2.}
  \note{[3 min] Training-serving skew, from Lecture 4: training and serving compute a feature differently. Step 1: the test is a replay. \begin{frame}{Exposure grows in stages: shadow, canary, then ramp}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.4cm,keepaspectratio]{release_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{ML Operations, \S14.4.3 and table 11; ranking service illustrative.}
  \note{[2.5 min] Three ways to put v2 in front of traffic, in order of exposure. Step 1: shadow deployment. The router sends every user to v1 and a copy of every request to v2; v2's answers are logged and compared, never shown. No user is exposed, but nothing is learned about how users respond, because they never see v2. Step 2: canary. Now 5 percent of users get v2's answers, and the team compares click-through and p99 latency against v1 on that slice. Step 3: the ramp. Exposure rises in steps, 5, 25, 50, 100 percent, and each step passes only if its metrics hold; a failed gate routes traffic back to v1. ENGAGE: why not jump from 5 to 100? Some failures appear only at load: the chapter's example is p99 inflation at 30 percent of traffic that was invisible at 5. WARN: a canary needs enough traffic to tell a real drop from noise; Lecture 12's statistics apply. Note the boxes: v2 is a full second copy in every mode, which the next slide prices. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

  \note{[2 min] Step 1: Facebook's 2018 report of how often its production models were retrained. Search hourly, Sigma (classifiers for site integrity and spam) several times a day, News Feed daily, translation and speech weekly, Lumos (image understanding) every few months. WARN: bar heights are read from the paper's figure; the claim rests on its text. ENGAGE: is daily always right? The next slide answers with a formula. FLEX: [OPTIONAL] for the right panel.}
\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{[1 min] The key law of the lecture. Retraining has a fixed cost per run, C. Not retraining has a cost that grows with time: queries per day Q, value per query of a unit of accuracy V, starting accuracy A-zero, and decay rate gamma. Retrain too often and run costs dominate; too rarely and staleness dominates. Minimizing the sum per day gives a square root: the interval grows with the square root of the run cost and shrinks with the square root of traffic, value, and decay. Quadruple any term and the interval changes by a factor of two. The approximation holds when the model loses only a small fraction of its accuracy between runs (Equation 12).}
\mlsysfocus{The optimal retraining interval}{%
  $\displaystyle T^{*} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\,C}{Q \cdot V \cdot A_0 \cdot \gamma}}$\\[0.6cm]
  {\small\textcolor{white!75}{$C$: cost of one run \quad $Q$: queries per day \quad $V$: value per query per unit accuracy}}\\[0.15cm]
  {\small\textcolor{white!75}{$A_0$: accuracy after retraining \quad $\gamma$: accuracy decay rate per day}}%
}
