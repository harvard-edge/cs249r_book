\begin{frame}{Estimate: how many images to test a 1 percent group?}
  \mlsysactivity{Estimate}{3 min}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{mlsyscard}{mlsysaccent}
        \textbf{Given}\\[0.05cm]
        Group: 1\% of users\\
        Error rate within $\pm$1 point, 95\%\\
        Worst case: error rate 0.5
      \end{mlsyscard}
      \vspace{0.15cm}\small
      \uncover<2->{$\text{margin} = 1.96\sqrt{0.25/n}$\\[0.2cm]}
      \uncover<3->{$n = 1.96^2 \times 0.25 / 0.01^2 = \mathbf{9{,}604}$}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      \centering
      \uncover<3->{\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=4.2cm,keepaspectratio]{margin_curve.pdf}\\[0.1cm]}
      \uncover<4->{\small At random: $10{,}000 / 0.01 = \textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{1,000,000}}$ images}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Responsible Engineering, Napkin Math 15.1.}
  \note{[3 min] ENGAGE: one minute with a neighbor before step 2. A Face ID model must be shown to work for a group that is 1 percent of users. Step 2: an error rate measured on n examples is a proportion, and its 95 percent margin is 1.96 times its standard error, the square root of p(1-p)/n. The worst case is p = 0.5, so p(1-p) = 0.25. Step 3: setting the margin to 0.01 gives 9,604, about 10,000 images from that group. The curve shows why: the margin falls with the square root of n, so halving it costs four times the data. Step 4: at random, only 1 image in 100 comes from the group, so the team must collect about 1,000,000 images to expect 10,000 of them. WARN: the common error is to compute the margin on the whole test set; the bound that matters is per group. LINK: Lecture 4's data budget.}
\end{frame}

  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.4cm,keepaspectratio]{gate_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]

  \note{[2.5 min] A release gate is a test that blocks deployment. \begin{frame}{Same false positive rate, a 30-point gap for qualified applicants}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=5.5cm,keepaspectratio]{matrices_step}{4}\\[0.02cm]
  \uncover<4->{\small Against a 5-point gate, this model does not ship.}
  \note{[3.5 min] A constructed example: the chapter built this credit service so every rate can be computed. Say so. Step 1: two confusion matrices, one per group. Rows are what actually happened, columns are the model's decision. Group A has 10,000 applicants, Group B 2,000; in both, half would repay. Step 2: demographic parity compares approval rates: 5,500 of 10,000 is 55 percent, 800 of 2,000 is 40 percent, a 15-point gap. Step 3: equal opportunity compares, among people who would repay, the share approved: 90 against 60 percent, 30 points. Put differently, qualified Group B applicants are rejected 40 percent of the time against 10 percent, four times as often. Step 4: equalized odds also requires equal approval among those who would default: 20 against 20 percent. Equal here, so a team checking only false positives would call the model fair. ENGAGE: which gap harms whom? WARN: the matrices show a disparity, not its cause.}
\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{[0.5 min] The chapter's principle 13. Read it: the disparity after k retraining cycles is the launch disparity times a feedback factor raised to the k. If the factor is above one, a small gap grows; below one, it shrinks. A gate passed at launch is a statement about k = 0 only.}
\mlsysfocus{The Bias Feedback Model}{%
  $\displaystyle \Delta(k) \approx \Delta(0)\,\alpha^{k}$\\[0.5cm]
  {\small\textcolor{white!75}{$\Delta$: gap between groups \quad $k$: retraining cycles \quad $\alpha$: feedback factor}}%
}

\begin{frame}{Denied applicants never produce a label}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.70\textwidth,height=5.8cm,keepaspectratio]{censored_loop.pdf}
  \note{[1 min] Why the factor can exceed one. The model decides; approved applicants either repay or default, and that outcome becomes next quarter's training data. Denied applicants are never observed, so the model never learns whether they would have repaid. Each retraining learns only from the people the previous model chose. If the previous model under-approved Group B, the next one sees even less evidence about Group B. LINK: this is Lecture 14's retraining loop, seen as a fairness loop. The DLRM ranking service has the same problem at scale: of 200 million impressions a day, each user's feedback covers 0.002 percent of a 1 million item catalog, all chosen by the previous model.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A 3-point gap passes at launch and fails in six quarters}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.90\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{feedback_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small $\ln(5/3)\,/\,\ln 1.10 = 5.4$, so the gate fails in quarter 6.}

  \note{[2 min] Step 1: the credit service ships with a 3.0-point gap, inside the 5-point gate. An independent audit sample next quarter measures 3.3 points, so the fitted factor is 3.3 / 3.0 = 1.10. The gap passes 5 when 3 times 1.1 to the k exceeds 5, at k greater than 5.4: quarter 6, at 5.3 points. Without the earlier mitigation, the original 30-point gap would reach 43.9 points within a year. Step 2: change the loop. Approve a small randomized sample of marginal applicants and observe their repayment; the loop again sees outcomes it was censoring. If the refitted factor falls to 0.90, the same gap decays to 1.6 points. For the DLRM service, reserving 1 percent of slots for exploration gives 2 million unselected impressions a day, served at lower expected engagement. WARN: two audits do not validate a growth law; refit the factor every audit. Land the segment: criteria conflict, the chosen one has a price, and under feedback a passed gate drifts unless the loop changes.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Five things to carry out of this lecture}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25cm}
    \item A model can predict its target well and still harm people when the target is a proxy.
