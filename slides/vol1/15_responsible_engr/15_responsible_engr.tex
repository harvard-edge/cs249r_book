\begin{frame}{Past the knee, clicks rise while satisfaction falls}

\begin{frame}{Estimate: how many images to test a 1 percent group?}
  \mlsysactivity{Estimate}{3 min}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{mlsyscard}{mlsysaccent}
        \textbf{Given}\\[0.05cm]
        Group: 1\% of users\\
        Error rate within $\pm$1 point, 95\%\\
        Worst case: error rate 0.5
      \end{mlsyscard}
      \vspace{0.15cm}\small
      \uncover<2->{$\text{margin} = 1.96\sqrt{0.25/n}$\\[0.2cm]}
      \uncover<3->{$n = 1.96^2 \times 0.25 / 0.01^2 = \mathbf{9{,}604}$}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      \centering
      \uncover<3->{\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=4.2cm,keepaspectratio]{margin_curve.pdf}\\[0.1cm]}
      \uncover<4->{\small At random: $10{,}000 / 0.01 = \textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{1,000,000}}$ images}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Responsible Engineering, Napkin Math 15.1.}
  \note{[3 min] ENGAGE: one minute with a neighbor before step 2. A Face ID model must be shown to work for a group that is 1 percent of users. Step 2: an error rate measured on n examples is a proportion, and its 95 percent margin is 1.96 times its standard error, the square root of p(1-p)/n. The worst case is p = 0.5, so p(1-p) = 0.25. Step 3: setting the margin to 0.01 gives 9,604, about 10,000 images from that group. The curve shows why: the margin falls with the square root of n, so halving it costs four times the data. Step 4: at random, only 1 image in 100 comes from the group, so the team must collect about 1,000,000 images to expect 10,000 of them. WARN: the common error is to compute the margin on the whole test set; the bound that matters is per group. LINK: Lecture 4's data budget. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A fairness gate returns pass, fail, or not enough data}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{gate_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<4->{\small\textbf{Invariance test:} swap ``John'' for ``Jamal'', all else equal; the approval must not change.}
  \mlsyscredit{Illustrative slices. Gate logic: Responsible Engineering, \S15.3.2; invariance testing after Ribeiro et al., CheckList, ACL 2020.}
  \note{[3 min] A release gate is a test that blocks deployment. Here it checks the gap in true positive rate between groups against an application-specific bound of 5 points; each slice shows its measured gap as a dot and its 95 percent confidence interval as a bar. Values are illustrative. Step 1: slice A sits well inside the bound, pass; slice B sits outside, fail. Step 2: slice C has few labeled examples, so its interval crosses the line. It is neither a pass nor a fail; the gate must report insufficient evidence, which triggers more data collection or a narrower launch. Step 3: slice D passes. Only a pass permits release. ENGAGE: why not treat C as a pass? Expected: absence of evidence is not evidence of parity. Step 4: a second kind of gate needs no group labels at all. An invariance test changes one input that should not matter, here the applicant's name from John to Jamal with every other field fixed, and requires the same decision; a suite of such pairs runs on every release (Ribeiro et al. 2020). WARN: passing the invariance test does not prove fairness, because proxies such as ZIP code are not swapped. LINK: the four-fifths rule (1978 Uniform Guidelines) is a selection-rate diagnostic in US employment, not a verdict. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Same false positive rate, a 30-point gap for qualified applicants}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=5.5cm,keepaspectratio]{matrices_step}{4}\\[0.02cm]
  \uncover<4->{\small Against a 5-point gate, this model does not ship.}
  \note{[3.5 min] A constructed example: the chapter built this credit service so every rate can be computed. Say so. Step 1: two confusion matrices, one per group. Rows are what actually happened, columns are the model's decision. Group A has 10,000 applicants, Group B 2,000; in both, half would repay. Step 2: demographic parity compares approval rates: 5,500 of 10,000 is 55 percent, 800 of 2,000 is 40 percent, a 15-point gap. Step 3: equal opportunity compares, among people who would repay, the share approved: 90 against 60 percent, 30 points. Step 4: equalized odds also requires equal approval among those who would default: 20 against 20 percent. Equal here, so a team checking only false positives would call the model fair. ENGAGE: which gap harms whom? WARN: the matrices show a disparity, not its cause. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Discuss: unequal errors, similar precision}
  \mlsysactivity{Discuss}{2 min}
  \centering
  {\small COMPAS scores defendants' risk of reoffending for US bail and sentencing decisions.}\\[0.1cm]
  \only<1|handout:0>{\safeimg[width=0.94\textwidth,height=4.0cm,keepaspectratio]{compas_step1.pdf}}%
  \only<2-|handout:1>{\safeimg[width=0.94\textwidth,height=4.0cm,keepaspectratio]{compas_step2.pdf}}\\[0.1cm]
  \only<2|handout:0>{\normalsize Was the tool fair? Which number should a court use?}%
  \only<3-|handout:1>{\normalsize With unequal base rates, equal precision and equal error rates cannot both hold.}
  \mlsyscredit{ProPublica, ``How We Analyzed the COMPAS Recidivism Algorithm,'' 2016 (7,214 defendants, Broward County, Florida); base rates derived from its tables. Chouldechova 2017; Kleinberg et al. 2017.}
  \note{[4 min] COMPAS scores defendants' risk of reoffending for bail and sentencing decisions. Step 1: among defendants who did not reoffend, 45 percent of Black and 23 percent of White defendants were labeled high risk; among those who did reoffend, 28 and 48 percent were labeled low risk. Step 2: precision, the share of high-risk labels that were correct, was similar, 63 and 59 percent; the maker argued the score was equally predictive. Both sides read the same data correctly. ENGAGE: two minutes with a neighbor; take one answer per side. Step 3: the base rates differ, 51 and 39 percent (derived from ProPublica's tables). When base rates differ and prediction is imperfect, a score that is equally precise for both groups must make unequal errors (Chouldechova; Kleinberg et al.). WARN: no amount of engineering removes the conflict. Choosing the criterion is a policy decision someone must own and state. The credit example had equal base rates, so there it does not bind. The next slide computes why. FLEX: [CORE]; this discussion is where the lecture's slack goes, up to four minutes.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The conflict, computed from three published numbers}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \begin{mlsyscard}{mlsysaccent}
        \textbf{Chouldechova's identity}\\[0.15cm]
        \small$\text{FPR} = \dfrac{p}{1-p}\cdot\dfrac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot(1-\text{FNR})$\\[0.15cm]
        \footnotesize $p$: base rate; PPV: precision
      \end{mlsyscard}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.51\textwidth}
      \small
      \uncover<2->{\textbf{Black:} $\dfrac{0.514}{0.486}\cdot\dfrac{0.37}{0.63}\cdot 0.720 = \mathbf{0.447}$\\
        \smalltag{published 44.85\%}\\[0.2cm]}
      \uncover<3->{\textbf{White:} $\dfrac{0.394}{0.606}\cdot\dfrac{0.41}{0.59}\cdot 0.523 = \mathbf{0.236}$\\
        \smalltag{published 23.45\%}\\[0.25cm]}
      \uncover<4->{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{\textbf{Similar PPV, unequal $p$: the error rates must differ.}}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Chouldechova, ``Fair Prediction with Disparate Impact,'' Big Data 2017. ProPublica 2016: PPV 0.63 and 0.59, FNR 27.99\% and 47.72\%; base rates 1,901/3,696 and 966/2,454.}
  \note{[3 min] Worked example: the impossibility as arithmetic. Chouldechova showed that the false positive rate is fixed by three other numbers: the base rate p, the precision PPV, and the false negative rate. The identity follows from the confusion matrix; derive it on the board only if the class asks. Step 2: Black defendants. The base rate is 1,901 of 3,696, 0.514; precision 0.63; false negative rate 0.280. 0.514 over 0.486 is 1.058; 0.37 over 0.63 is 0.587; times 0.720 gives 0.447, the published 44.85 percent. Step 3: White defendants. 966 of 2,454, 0.394; precision 0.59; false negative rate 0.477. 0.650 times 0.695 times 0.523 is 0.236, the published 23.45 percent. Step 4: the first factor differs because the base rates differ. If precision is held equal, the only way to equalize false positive rates is to make the false negative rates unequal, and the reverse. ENGAGE: what would make both conditions hold? Expected: equal base rates, or a perfect predictor; neither exists here. WARN: this does not say which side was right; it says the choice cannot be avoided. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{[0.5 min] The chapter's principle 13. Read it: the disparity after k retraining cycles is the launch disparity times a feedback factor raised to the k. If the factor is above one, a small gap grows; below one, it shrinks. A gate passed at launch is a statement about k = 0 only. FLEX: [CORE].}

  $\displaystyle \Delta(k) \approx \Delta(0)\,\alpha^{k}$\\[0.5cm]
  {\small\textcolor{white!75}{$\Delta$: gap between groups \quad $k$: retraining cycles \quad $\alpha$: feedback factor}}%

\begin{frame}{Denied applicants never produce a label}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.70\textwidth,height=5.8cm,keepaspectratio]{censored_loop.pdf}
  \note{[1.5 min] Why the factor can exceed one. The model decides; approved applicants either repay or default, and that outcome becomes next quarter's training data. Denied applicants are never observed, so the model never learns whether they would have repaid. Each retraining learns only from the people the previous model chose. If the previous model under-approved Group B, the next one sees even less evidence about Group B. LINK: this is Lecture 14's retraining loop, seen as a fairness loop. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A 3-point gap passes at launch and fails in six quarters}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.90\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{feedback_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small $\ln(5/3)\,/\,\ln 1.10 = 5.4$, so the gate fails in quarter 6.}
  \mlsyscredit{Responsible Engineering, Napkin Math 15.3 (illustrative).}
  \note{[2.5 min] Step 1: the credit service ships with a 3.0-point gap, inside the 5-point gate. An independent audit sample next quarter measures 3.3 points, so the fitted factor is 3.3 / 3.0 = 1.10. The gap passes 5 when 3 times 1.1 to the k exceeds 5, at k greater than 5.4: quarter 6, at 5.3 points. Without the earlier mitigation, the original 30-point gap would reach 43.9 points within a year. Step 2: change the loop. Approve a small randomized sample of marginal applicants and observe their repayment; the loop again sees outcomes it was censoring. If the refitted factor falls to 0.90, the same gap decays to 1.6 points. WARN: two audits do not validate a growth law; refit the factor every audit. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

  \note{[2.5 min] The exposure form of the same loop. Step 1: the book's DLRM service shows 10 million users 20 ranked items a day, 200 million impressions. \begin{frame}{Five things to carry out of this lecture}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25cm}
    \item A model can predict its target well and still harm people when the target is a proxy.
    \item A group becomes testable when it is named: 10,000 examples for a 1\% group, or 1,000,000 at random.
    \item Fairness criteria conflict under unequal base rates; the chosen one has a price stated as a number.
    \item Under feedback, a gate passed at launch drifts unless the loop observes what it censored.
    \item A ranking service that shows only what it chose never learns what it hid; exploration is the price of keeping the feedback factor below one.
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}
