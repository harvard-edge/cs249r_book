  \note{%

\begin{frame}{The training label decides who is selected}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{label_choice_step}{3}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<3->{\normalsize Same data, same predictors, same method. Only the label changed.}
  \mlsyscredit{Obermeyer et al., Science 2019, Table 2: models retrained on each label; share of Black patients at or above the 97th percentile of predicted risk.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    To isolate how much the specification alone drives disparity, Obermeyer et al. (Table 2) held the hospital data, race-blind predictors, and training algorithm fixed and swapped only the training label. Training on total cost puts 14.1 percent Black patients in the top 3 percent of predicted risk. Switching the label to avoidable cost raises that share to 21.0 percent. Training on active chronic illnesses raises it further to 26.7 percent, which is 1.9 times the cost-label share. Working with the manufacturer on a combined health-and-cost label later reduced one bias metric by 84 percent.

    \notecue{Check}{Which label should a hospital choose? Choose the label closest to the clinical goal, health need, and document the justification explicitly because every label carries a trade-off. Picking cost simply because billing data is clean repeats the original failure.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{Do not confuse 14.1 percent from the authors' retrained total-cost model with 17.7 percent on Slide 2 from the deployed commercial algorithm.}

    \notecue{Next}{See how proxy misalignment also appears in ranking systems when engagement diverges from user satisfaction.}

\begin{frame}{One error rate hid a 43-fold gap}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
      \centering
      \safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{ppb_grid.png}\\[0.15cm]
      {\footnotesize\raggedright The test set they built: 1,270 members of six parliaments, chosen to balance skin type and gender.\par}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      \centering
      \mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{gender_shades_step}{2}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Buolamwini and Gebru, ``Gender Shades,'' FAT* 2018: Fig. 1 (Pilot Parliaments Benchmark examples and average faces), Tables 1 and 4 (Face++); chapter table 3.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3.5 min}{Core}%
    Why do aggregate scores miss harm? Because face datasets skewed toward lighter skin, Joy Buolamwini (MIT Media Lab) and Timnit Gebru (Microsoft Research) audited commercial gender classifiers from Microsoft, IBM, and Face++ in Gender Shades (FAT* 2018). They built the Pilot Parliaments Benchmark from 1,270 photos across six parliaments (Rwanda, Senegal, South Africa, Iceland, Finland, Sweden), balanced by gender and skin type. Face++ posted 10.0 percent overall error, with 0.8 percent on lighter men and 0.7 percent on darker men. Error rose to 9.8 percent on lighter women and 34.5 percent on darker women, 43 times the lighter-men rate. Like p99 tail latency in Lectures 12 and 13, averages hide worst-case slices.

    \notecue{Check}{Does 10 percent overall error sound acceptable? An acceptable aggregate can mask 34.5 percent error on one subgroup.}

In-processing adversarial debiasing adds a group-prediction head whose reversed gradient steers the encoder (shared feature layers) away from group cues, adding tuning instability.     \notecue{Check}{Which intervention leaves weights untouched? Post-processing, whereas pre-processing and in-processing both alter trained weights.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{All three interventions require post-launch re-validation once deployment populations shift.}

    \notecue{Teach}{In a 50-minute class, skip this comparison, note that fairness can be enforced before, during, or after training, and move to feedback loops.}

    \notecue{Next}{Examine Lum and Isaac's Oakland predictive-policing study to see a feedback loop unfold on real records.}
  }
\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{0.5 min}{Core}%
  When a model retrains on data shaped by its own decisions, launch-day disparity need not stay put. Principle 13 captures that carry-over as the Bias Feedback Model, written $\Delta(k) \approx \Delta(0)\,\alpha^k$, where $\Delta$ is the inter-group gap, $k$ counts retraining cycles, and $\alpha$ is the feedback factor. When $\alpha > 1$, a small gap compounds. When $\alpha < 1$, it decays. A fairness check passed at launch certifies only $k = 0$.

  \notecue{Next}{Trace the censored-label mechanism in lending that pushes $\alpha$ above one.}

\begin{frame}{Denied applicants never produce a label}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.70\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{censored_loop_step}{3}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    What pushes $\alpha$ above 1 in lending? In the operations retraining loop from Lectures 14 and 14b, the credit model splits applicants into approved and denied branches. Only approved applicants repay or default and generate ground-truth labels, meaning observed repayment outcomes. Denied applicants never produce a repayment outcome. Retraining then updates the model solely on the approved branch, starving future versions of evidence on any group the prior model under-approved.

    \notecue{Check}{Which branch disappears from the next training split? Every denied applicant is censored, meaning omitted from future training data because the denial prevented an outcome from being observed.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{Accuracy measured only on logged approved outcomes can look completely stable while censored disparity widens.}

\begin{frame}{Predict: when does a 3-point gap fail?}

  \centering

  \mlsyscredit{Responsible Engineering, Napkin Math 15.3 (illustrative).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    How fast does censored-label compounding break a passing release check? In Napkin Math 15.3 (illustrative), the credit service launches with a 3.0-point gap inside its 5-point limit. One quarter later, an independent audit measures 3.3 points, fitting $\alpha = 3.3 / 3.0 = 1.10$. Before reading on, predict which quarter the gap breaches the 5-point limit.

    \notecue{Check}{The gap fails in quarter 6 at 5.3 points, because $3.0 \times 1.10^k > 5$ solves to $k > \ln(5/3)/\ln(1.10) = 5.4$. A tempting answer is quarter 7, from linear extrapolation ($+0.3$ per quarter) that ignores compounding. At the same rate, an unmitigated 30-point gap reaches 43.9 points in one year. Randomly approving a small sample of marginal applicants restores uncensored repayment labels, dropping the refitted factor to $\alpha = 0.90$ and shrinking the gap to 1.6 points.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{Two audit points never validate an exponential law. Teams must refit $\alpha$ after every audit cycle.}

\begin{frame}{Five things to carry out of this lecture}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25cm}
    \item A model can predict its target well and still harm people when the target is a proxy.
    \item A group becomes testable when it is named: 10,000 examples for a 1\% group, or 1,000,000 at random.
    \item Fairness criteria conflict under unequal base rates; the chosen one has a price stated as a number.
    \item Under feedback, a check passed at launch drifts unless the loop observes what it censored.
    \item A ranking service that shows only what it chose never learns what it hid; exploration is the price of keeping the feedback factor below one.
