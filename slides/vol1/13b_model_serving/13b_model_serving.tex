\begin{frame}{A chat answer has two deadlines, the first token and each one after it}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.8cm,keepaspectratio]{ttft_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{Patel et al., ``Splitwise,'' ISCA 2024, Table IV (Llama 2 70B, P50, no batching, conversation trace). Targets: Model Serving, Systems Perspective 13.5 (illustrative).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    One end-to-end latency hides whether a chat starts quickly or streams smoothly. In 2024, Patel et al. published Splitwise (ISCA 2024), measuring prompt and token phases separately across real conversation traces to guide phase-aware serving (Table IV, median unbatched Llama 2 70B). On an 8-GPU DGX-H100 server, prefill processes the entire prompt in parallel and emits token 1 at 84 ms, setting TTFT, what a user notices first. Decode then generates one token every 28 ms, setting TPOT and whether reading feels fluid. Completing about 120 tokens takes 3,387 ms in total, so decode accounts for 98 percent of the duration (derived from the two medians). On an earlier-generation DGX-A100 server, the same request takes 155 ms for TTFT and 40 ms for TPOT, finishing in 4,957 ms. The chapter's illustrative user targets sit at 500 ms for TTFT and 50 ms for TPOT, or 20 tokens/s, faster than human reading.

    \notecue{Watch for}{A fast first token does not guarantee a fast reply, because prefill and decode hit different hardware ceilings.}

    \notecue{Next}{Map prefill onto the H100's compute ceiling and decode onto its HBM ceiling.}
  }
\end{frame}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Optional}%
    Because batch-1 decode leaves arithmetic idle, speculative decoding (Leviathan et al. 2023, 2 to 3x on T5-XXL; Equation 15) drafts tokens with a small model and verifies them in one parallel pass, keeping the exact output distribution. GPT-2 Small (248 MB) drafts $k = 4$ tokens in 0.296 ms; GPT-2 XL checks all 5 positions (four drafts plus one bonus slot) in one 0.896 ms read (1.192 ms/round). In the example round, two match and the third fails, replaced by one correction while discarding token 4 ($2 + 1 = 3$). Compute expected tokens per round and ms/token for $\alpha = 0.7, k = 4$.

    \notecue{Check}{Reaching position $j$ has probability $\alpha^j$, summing $1 + \alpha + \dots + \alpha^k = (1 - \alpha^{k+1}) / (1 - \alpha) = (1 - 0.7^5)/0.3 = 2.8$ tokens/round, or $1.192 / 2.8 = 0.43$ ms/token (2.1x faster). A tempting formula, $0.7\times 4 + 1 = 3.8$, forgets that the first rejection discards every later draft token.}

\begin{frame}{Splitting costs one cache transfer and frees the hardware choice}

        Capping a GPU from 700 to 350 W barely slows decode.\\[0.05cm]

      \end{mlsyscard}}
    \end{column}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Optional}%
    Separating the phases adds one KV-cache transfer yet frees hardware choice. A 512-token OPT-66B language-model prompt creates 1.13 GB of cache, so 10 QPS needs 11.3 GB/s (about 90 gigabits per second, Gbps), small beside 600 GB/s intra-node NVLink bandwidth. DistServe measured under 0.1 percent latency overhead on OPT-175B, while Splitwise overlapped layer-by-layer transfers at 0.8 percent (about 5 ms on H100). Splitting also lets each pool match its silicon. Splitwise found capping decode GPUs from 700 to 350 W barely slowed memory-bound decode, which rarely needs peak power; pairing specialized prefill and decode pools delivered 1.4x cluster throughput at 20 percent lower cost. Inside an 8-GPU node, chunking avoids transfers while disaggregation uses NVLink. For models over 80 GB, tensor parallelism, splitting each layer's weights and cache across one node's GPUs so all compute every token together, is left to reading (\S13.10.5). Section 4 thus trades a small transfer for interference-free, phase-matched pools.

    Returning to the hook answers our driving question. By Part 1's Little's law ($N=\lambda\times W$), decoding streams are $\le\text{tokens/s}\times\text{TPOT}$, giving $30{,}904\times0.2=6{,}181$ at 200 ms versus $12{,}979\times0.04=519$ at 40 ms (derived).
