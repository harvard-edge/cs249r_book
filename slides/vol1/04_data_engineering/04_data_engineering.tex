  \note{[3 min] ENGAGE: vote with fingers, one to four, then thirty seconds with a neighbor and vote again. Step 2 reveals C. \begin{frame}{Real detectors trade misses for false alarms}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{pv_missrate.pdf}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{Lower the threshold and misses fall, but false alarms rise.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Data: Picovoice wake-word benchmark, github.com/Picovoice/wake-word-benchmark, README ``Accuracy'' (commit 8491919), redrawn; run by the maker of Porcupine.}
  \note{[1.5 min] NARRATE: measured numbers for the trade-off the napkin math implies. Picovoice's open benchmark plays more than 300 recordings of six wake words from more than 50 speakers, mixed at 10 dB SNR with noise recorded in 18 environments such as kitchens, offices, and traffic (the DEMAND dataset), with LibriSpeech read speech as the background that must not trigger a wake. Each engine's threshold is set so that it raises 1 false alarm per 10 hours, and the bars are the share of wake words it then misses: 52.0 percent for PocketSphinx, 31.9 percent for Snowboy, 2.7 percent for Porcupine. Note the axis the industry uses: false alarms per hour of streaming audio, not a per-window rate, because real detectors score overlapping windows every few tens of milliseconds. One per 10 hours is 72 false alarms in a 720-hour month, 72 times our budget. Step 2: a stricter false-alarm target means a higher threshold and more misses; only better data and models move the whole curve. WARN: Picovoice makes Porcupine, so treat the ranking as the vendor's; the size of the gap between operating points is the lesson. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Proving one false wake a month takes 2,160 hours of audio}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=3.2cm,keepaspectratio]{listen_hours.pdf}\\[0.35cm]
  {\normalsize Zero false wakes in $T$ hours: the 95\% upper bound on the rate is about $3/T$.}\\[0.2cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{The target sets a data requirement that no training set replaces.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Engineering, \S4.11; the rule of three (Hanley and Lippman-Hand 1983).}
  \note{[2.5 min] NARRATE: suppose we play a month of ordinary household audio, 720 hours, and the detector never wakes. Have we shown one false wake a month? No. With zero observed events in $T$ hours, the 95 percent upper confidence bound on the true rate is about three events divided by $T$, the rule of three. To push that bound down to one per 720 hours we need $3 \times 720 = 2{,}160$ hours. At 16 kHz and 16 bits that is about 249 GB of recordings (the chapter rounds it down to 248), a storage line and a collection line the \$150K allocation never priced; the team must add an evaluation-collection line. Step 2: the requirement. ENGAGE: why must these recordings stay out of training and threshold tuning? Expected: once the model or its threshold has seen them, they no longer estimate the deployed false-wake rate. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Label errors come from bad inputs or missing expertise}
  \mlsysactivity{Vote}{1.5 min}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.96\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{fig-hard-labels.png}\\[0.35cm]
  {\normalsize For each image: was the label wrong because of the input, or the annotator?}\\[0.2cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Quality: filter the input before labeling.\quad Expertise: a rubric and a trained reviewer.}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Engineering, \S4.6.3 (figure 12); examples from Northcutt, Athalye, and Mueller, NeurIPS 2021.}
  \note{[1.5 min] NARRATE: six test-set errors from the same study, each with the label it was given and the corrected one. ENGAGE: thumbs up for ``bad input,'' thumbs down for ``missing expertise,'' one image at a time. \begin{frame}{True or false? Three beliefs that break data pipelines}
  \mlsysactivity{Vote}{2 min}
  \normalsize
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Claim} & \textbf{Verdict} \\
    \midrule
    More data always improves the model. & \uncover<2->{\textbf{\textcolor{RedLine}{False}}: 10$\times$ labels, +5 to 8\%} \\
    Preprocessing can be finished once. & \uncover<3->{\textbf{\textcolor{RedLine}{False}}: fitted parameters go stale} \\
    Shared code keeps training and serving consistent. & \uncover<4->{\textbf{\textcolor{RedLine}{False}}: share fitted state too} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Engineering, \S4.13 (fallacies and pitfalls) and \S4.3.1 (design space, illustrative).}
  \note{[2 min] ENGAGE: thumbs up or down on each, then reveal one at a time. Row 1: false. Test loss falls as a power law in dataset size, so each improvement takes multiplicatively more data; in the KWS design space, ten times the examples bought an illustrative 5 to 8 points of detection at ten times the labeling cost, and repeated examples consume bandwidth and compute without new evidence. Row 2: false. Preprocessing applies parameters fitted on historical data, such as vocabularies, normalization constants, and clipping bounds; when the inputs drift, those go stale, and a fixed normalization pushes features outside the range the model trained on. Row 3: false. Identical code with per-environment statistics produces skew. Another fallacy, already answered today: synthetic data cannot replace real recordings. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25cm}
    \item Data is the program: version it, test it, review it.
    \item A product target is a data budget: 1 false wake a month means 2,160 h of audio.
    \item Labels are the specification and usually the largest data cost.
    \item Leakage and skew come from inside the pipeline; contracts fitted on the training split stop them.
    \item A release records how the dataset was built.
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}
