\begin{frame}{Predict: how many false wakes a month at 99.9\%?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{3 min}
  \centering
  {\large The detector checks one 1-second window, every second, all month.}\\[0.15cm]
  {\large It correctly rejects 99.9\% of windows with no wake word.}\\[0.45cm]
  \begin{minipage}{0.45\textwidth}
    \Large
    \choice{A}{about 3}\\[0.15cm]
    \choice{B}{about 26}\\[0.15cm]
    \alt<2->{\pick{C}{about 2,600}}{\choice{C}{about 2,600}}\\[0.15cm]
    \choice{D}{about 26,000}
  \end{minipage}
  \note{[3 min] ENGAGE: vote with fingers, one to four, then thirty seconds with a neighbor and vote again. Step 2 reveals C. The arithmetic: 60 seconds, 60 minutes, 24 hours, 30 days gives 2,592,000 windows a month; 0.1 percent of them is 2,592 false wakes, about 86 a day. If many chose A, they heard ``99.9 percent'' as nearly perfect; the denominator is what makes it large. If many chose D, they used 1 percent instead of 0.1 percent. WARN: this is why overall accuracy is the wrong target. Almost every window holds no wake word, so a detector that never fires scores nearly 100 percent accuracy and is useless. The chapter's target is a detection rate measured at a threshold that holds the false-wake budget. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Proving one false wake a month takes 2,160 hours of audio}

  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=3.2cm,keepaspectratio]{listen_hours.pdf}\\[0.35cm]

\begin{frame}{Agreement beyond chance measures label quality}
  \centering
  \vspace{0.2cm}
  {\Large $\kappa = \dfrac{\alert<2>{p_o} - \alert<3>{p_e}}{1 - p_e}$}\\[0.5cm]
  \begin{minipage}{0.80\textwidth}
    \normalsize
    \uncover<2->{$p_o$: how often two annotators agree.\\[0.15cm]}
    \uncover<3->{$p_e$: how often they would agree by chance. If both say ``not spam'' 90\% of the time, $p_e = 0.82$.\\[0.3cm]}
    \uncover<4->{Wake-word campaign: $\kappa$ fell from \textbf{0.85} to \textbf{0.72} in six months while every audio feature stayed flat.}
  \end{minipage}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Engineering, \S4.6.3 and \S4.6.4 (Cohen 1960). The campaign is the chapter's illustrative scenario.}
  \note{[2 min] NARRATE: we rarely know the truth, but we can measure whether annotators agree. Step 2: $p_o$ is observed agreement. Step 3: $p_e$ is the agreement expected by chance from each annotator's label mix: with two independent annotators who each say ``not spam'' 90 percent of the time, chance agreement is $0.9\times0.9 + 0.1\times0.1 = 0.82$, so a raw 85 percent agreement is barely better than chance. Cohen's kappa removes that. Step 4: label quality drift. New annotators joined without the original rubric training and began accepting near-homophones; kappa fell from 0.85 to 0.72 while every input-feature monitor stayed flat, because the inputs did not change. WARN: high agreement is not correctness; annotators can agree on the same systematic error, so gold-standard items and expert audits are still needed. FLEX: [OPTIONAL]; cut if behind and say the last line aloud.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Only DRAM and local NVMe answer within 1 ms}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{latency_ladder_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Engineering, \S4.10 (margin figure).}
  \note{[1 min] Define the tiers first: DRAM is the server's main memory; a local NVMe SSD is flash storage attached directly to the server's PCIe bus; a remote SSD is flash reached over the network; an object store such as S3 keeps whole files behind a web interface. NARRATE: one bar per tier, log scale; the dashed line is 1 millisecond, a typical budget for one feature lookup inside a serving request. Steps 1 and 2: DRAM at about 100 nanoseconds and a local NVMe SSD at about 100 microseconds fit. Steps 3 and 4: a remote SSD at 2 ms and an object store such as S3 at 20 ms do not. Each tier is about ten to a hundred times slower than the one above it. ENGAGE: a feature store keeps its online copy in which tier? Expected: memory, for example Redis. \section{Skew and Drift}

\begin{frame}{One speaker's clips must stay in one split}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{leak_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Engineering, \S4.1 and \S4.8 (table 5).}
  \note{[2 min] NARRATE: the training, validation, and test splits are trust boundaries. Training may shape the weights, validation may shape our choices, and the test set only estimates how the model does on data it has never seen. Leakage is information crossing those boundaries. Step 1: split audio files at random, and every speaker's clips land on both sides; the test set now measures whether the model recognizes familiar voices. Step 2: split by a hash of the speaker key, and each speaker lives in exactly one split, in every release, even after relabeling. ENGAGE: name another way information leaks. Expected: duplicates or augmented copies of one recording on both sides; features computed with future data; normalization statistics fitted on the whole dataset before splitting. WARN: deduplication must run before the split, not after. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Same code, different statistics: training-serving skew}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{skew_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Engineering, \S4.7.1.}
  \note{[2 min] NARRATE: Step 1: training normalizes each feature with the training set's mean and standard deviation. A serving service runs the same function but recomputes the mean and standard deviation on each incoming batch. The code is identical; the numbers the model receives are not. Nothing raises an error: the model gets a valid vector and returns a confident prediction. That is training-serving skew. Step 2: the fix is to fit the statistics once, on the training split, persist them with the model, and load them at serving. The KWS version: normalizing each stored clip against its own peak uses information a streaming device never has, so training may use only gain handling the device can reproduce. WARN: students believe sharing code guarantees consistency; consistency is a state-synchronization problem. LINK: skew is internal to our pipeline; drift, next, comes from the world. Feature stores exist largely to prevent skew for features computed from user history (§4.7.5). FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Drift is a change in $p(x)$, $p(y)$, or $p(y \mid x)$}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{drift_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Engineering, \S4.12.1.}
  \note{[2 min] NARRATE: drift is the world moving away from the training data. Read the notation aloud: $p(x)$ is how often each input occurs, $p(y)$ how often each answer occurs, and $p(y \mid x)$ the chance of each answer given an input, the rule the model learned. Step 1: covariate shift: the inputs change but the input-to-label relationship does not. The KWS case: a new microphone changes the audio's frequency response; people say the same words. Detect it by comparing input feature distributions. Step 2: label shift: the mix of outcomes changes, such as more positive flu tests in winter, while each class looks the same. Detect it from the model's prediction mix, before labels arrive. Step 3: concept drift: the mapping itself changes, as when fraudsters adapt. Only fresh labels reveal it, and labels arrive late. ENGAGE: which is hardest to detect, and why? Expected: concept drift, because the inputs can look unchanged. NARRATE: the population stability index (PSI) compares binned distributions; practitioners treat below 0.1 as stable, 0.1 to 0.25 as moderate, above 0.25 as a prompt to investigate. Those are conventions to calibrate, not physical limits. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The K-S test flags drift when the CDFs separate}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.60\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{ks_cdf_step}{3}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.38\textwidth}
      \normalsize
      \uncover<3->{$D = \max_x \lvert F_{\text{train}}(x) - F_{\text{recent}}(x)\rvert$\\[0.35cm]}
      \uncover<4->{$n_0 = n_t = 1{,}000$:\\[0.1cm]
        $D_{\text{crit}} \approx 1.36\sqrt{\tfrac{n_0+n_t}{n_0 n_t}} = 0.061$\\[0.35cm]}
      \uncover<5->{$0.08 > 0.061$: \textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{flag and investigate}}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Data Engineering, Napkin Math 4.5. The curves are illustrative; the numbers are the chapter's.}
  \note{[3 min] NARRATE: the scenario: monitor the loudness of incoming wake-word clips against the training baseline. Step 1: the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) of training loudness; at each loudness, the share of clips at most that loud. Loudness is in dBFS, decibels relative to the loudest value the recorder can store, so values nearer zero are louder. Step 2: the CDF of 1,000 recent clips, slightly quieter, as if one phone app update had lowered microphone gain. Step 3: the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic $D$ is the largest vertical gap between the curves, here 0.08. Step 4: for two samples of 1,000, the critical value at the 5 percent significance level is about $1.36\sqrt{2000/10^6} = 0.061$. Step 5: 0.08 exceeds it, so the test rejects ``same distribution'' and flags the feature. ENGAGE: with 100,000 clips in each sample, what happens to the critical value? Expected: it shrinks tenfold, to about 0.006, so tiny harmless shifts get flagged; large monitors need effect-size thresholds, not just significance. WARN: a flag starts an investigation and an outcome check; it does not prove accuracy fell, and it should not trigger retraining by itself. The K-S test is for continuous features; categorical features need frequency comparisons. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}
