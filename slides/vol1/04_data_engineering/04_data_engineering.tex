  \mlsyscredit{Data Engineering, \S4.11; the rule of three (Hanley and Lippman-Hand 1983). Photo: Raimond Spekking, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Optional}%
    Observing zero false wakes across one 720-hour month does not prove a rate below one per month. By the rule of three (Hanley and Lippman-Hand, 1983), zero events in $T$ hours yields a 95\% upper confidence bound near $3/T$, so bounding the rate at $1/720\text{ h}$ requires $3 \times 720 = 2{,}160$ hours of continuous audio. At 16 kHz and 16 bits, that evaluation corpus spans roughly 248 GB, a collection and storage line the initial \$150K plan never budgeted. Step 2 highlights that statistical targets create data requirements no training set replaces.

    \notecue{Check}{Ask why these 2,160 hours must never enter training or threshold tuning. Once a model or threshold adapts to them, they cease to estimate live false-wake rates.}

\begin{frame}{Agreement beyond chance measures label quality}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25cm}
    \item Data is the program, so version it, test it, and review it.
    \item A product target sets a data budget; 1 false wake a month means 2,160 h of audio.
    \item Labels are the specification and usually the largest data cost.
    \item Leakage and skew come from inside the pipeline; contracts fitted on the training split stop them.
    \item A release records how the dataset was built.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    Review one takeaway per segment: treat data as code, derive data and 2,160-hour listening budgets from product targets, price and audit labels, seal leakage and skew with training-fitted boundary contracts, and publish immutable traceable releases.
