  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    CPUs spend hundreds of picojoules (trillionths of a joule) of control overhead to issue a few-picojoule operation (\S11.2.2). Our reference accelerator from earlier lectures, the NVIDIA H100 (about 80 billion transistors), devotes far more silicon to parallel arithmetic than to CPU control logic (branch prediction and out-of-order scheduling) and large caches (Definition 11.1). On TPU v1's floor plan (Jouppi et al. 2017, Figure 2), the 65,536 MAC 8-bit matrix unit takes 24 percent of the die and the unified activation buffer 29 percent. By function, buffers take 37 percent, compute 30 percent, I/O 10 percent, and control 2 percent, so buffers outweigh arithmetic. For dense FP16 math, an H100 delivers 989 TFLOP/s versus 1 to 2 TFLOP/s on the chapter's reference CPU (490 to 990 times higher).

    \notecue{Watch for}{NVIDIA publishes no H100 floor plan, so do not assume its die is ``mostly arithmetic.'' The area breakdown is TPU v1, while the throughput contrast compares H100 against the CPU.}

\begin{frame}{Accelerator anatomy: arrays, local memory, HBM, and a host link}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{\mlsyskit/machine_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{After Hardware Acceleration, figure 1 (anatomy of an accelerator).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Every ML accelerator is built around the same four universal blocks, expanding the machine schematic from Lecture 0. First come thousands of compute units. On an H100, these include 16,896 CUDA cores for general math and 528 Tensor Cores for matrix tiles (\S11.7.1). Second is small, fast on-chip SRAM right beside arithmetic. Third is HBM, stacked DRAM inside the package that holds weights and activations. Fourth is the host CPU and its DRAM connected over an external link. GPUs, TPUs, and mobile NPUs combine these four blocks in different ratios. What ML changes is how many times each fetched byte is reused before returning to memory. Chapter 11 Figure 1 shows finer detail.

    \notecue{Next}{Compare the energy cost of crossing each tier from arithmetic out to DRAM.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Each primitive matches an execution unit; matrix units gave 6$\times$}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{units_step}{2}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \centering
      \uncover<3->{\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=2.8cm,keepaspectratio]{tensorcore.pdf}\\[0.2cm]
        {\footnotesize One Tensor Core instruction: 2,048 MACs, replacing 64 scalar FMAs per thread.}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Hardware Acceleration, \S11.3 and \S11.4.2; footnote 13 (NVIDIA 2017).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Because fetching operands costs so much energy, accelerators match each primitive to a silicon unit built for its reuse pattern. Vector units handle elementwise work like ReLU and residual additions; matrix units run GEMMs behind convolutions and linear projections; special-function units approximate exponentials, the Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) activation, and reciprocal square roots. ResNet-50 calls special functions only at its final 1,000-way softmax, whereas GPT-2 XL calls them every block for softmax and GELU. NVIDIA's Tensor Cores (Lectures 7 and 8b) debuted on the 2017 Volta V100, lifting peak FP16 roughly 6 times over the earlier Pascal P100's vector arithmetic (21.2 to 125 TFLOP/s). A GPU executes one Tensor Core instruction cooperatively across thirty-two execution threads (parallel lanes), completing 2,048 MACs at once instead of 64 scalar fused multiply-adds (FMAs) per thread.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Every peak rate belongs to a named numerical precision. INT8 doubles throughput again, as Section 3 quantifies.}

    \notecue{Teach}{Skip the Tensor Core comparison if short on time; leave warps and SIMT to the reading.}

\begin{frame}{A systolic array reuses each operand across a whole row}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{systolic_step}{3}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \normalsize
      \only<1|handout:0>{\textbf{Weights} stay in the cells.}%
      \only<2|handout:0>{\textbf{Activations} enter at the edge and move right, one cell per cycle.}%
      \only<3-|handout:1>{\textbf{Weights} stay in the cells.\\[0.2cm]
        \textbf{\textcolor{BlueLine}{Activations}} move right.\\[0.2cm]
        \textbf{\textcolor{GreenLine}{Partial sums}} move down.\\[0.4cm]
        {\small $128{\times}128$ cells: 16,384 MACs per cycle (TPU v4)}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{After Hardware Acceleration, figure 6 and \S11.4.4; Kung 1982; Jouppi et al. 2023.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    While GPU Tensor Cores reuse small tiles inside one instruction, Google's TPUs wire their matrix units into a systolic array, a grid formalized for parallel matrix math by Kung and Leiserson (Kung 1982) where data pulses between neighboring cells in lockstep with the clock. In this weight-stationary array, each cell holds one weight and performs one MAC per cycle. Activations enter along the left edge and move rightward one cell per cycle, so a single operand loaded into an $N\times N$ array is reused $N$ times across its row. Partial sums flow downward one cell per cycle, accumulating products and exiting the bottom as finished outputs. Short neighbor-to-neighbor wires replace register-file and memory reads. TPU v4's 128 by 128 array completes 16,384 MACs per cycle, while TPU v1 used 256 by 256.

    \notecue{Watch for}{This schematic omits temporal skew. In hardware, each row enters one cycle after the row above, creating fill and drain phases that Lecture 11b traces cycle by cycle.}

    \notecue{Next}{Compute how much energy systolic reuse saves per multiply-accumulate compared with naive DRAM fetches.}
  }
\end{frame}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    To bridge the memory wall, an H100 stages operands across four progressively slower tiers (Lecture 7 covered host transfers). Registers respond in a few cycles at about 0.1 pJ per access. Each streaming multiprocessor (SM) holds 228 KB of program-managed shared memory (about 17 nanoseconds, ns), backed by 50 MB of L2 cache (about 150 ns). HBM holds 80 GB at 3.35 TB/s, yet a dependent access (each read awaiting the prior address) takes about 273 ns (546 cycles at 2 GHz, above the chapter's ``100+'' note) and about 160 pJ per byte. \mlsysdivider{Which ceiling sets the limit: arithmetic or bytes?}{Hardware Acceleration, \S11.6}
\section{Roofline}

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Recall from Lecture 2 that the roofline model of Williams et al. (2009) bounds attainable performance by $\min(R_{\text{peak}}, \text{BW} \times I)$, with its bandwidth slope and compute ceiling meeting at the ridge point $I_{\text{ridge}} = R_{\text{peak}} / \text{BW}$ (153 FLOP/byte on an A100 at 312 TFLOP/s over 2.04 TB/s). Here we quantify the H100 and extend that single-curve preview across number formats. Because each precision runs on its own datapath with a distinct $R_{\text{peak}}$, one chip carries a separate ridge per format. Below a path's ridge, kernels are bandwidth bound and gain only from fewer bytes or higher reuse; above it, they are compute bound and gain only from faster arithmetic.

  \notecue{Watch for}{The roofline sets an upper bound. A kernel sitting well below both lines is stalled by launch overhead, latency, or poor parallelism, which profiling diagnoses (Lecture 12).}

  \notecue{Next}{Plot all three H100 precision roofs and ridges on one log-log chart.}
}
\mlsysfocus{The Roofline}{%
  $\displaystyle \text{Attainable} = \min\left(\Rpeak,\ \text{BW} \times I\right)$\\[0.6cm]
  $\displaystyle I_{\text{ridge}} = \frac{\Rpeak}{\text{BW}}$\\[0.5cm]
  {\small\textcolor{white!75}{One ridge per number format: each path has its own $\Rpeak$.}}%
}

    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    To reach that 3,958 TOPS sparse ceiling on Lecture 10's 2:4 structured sparsity without sparse-index slowdowns, the Tensor Core routes operands in fixed-width silicon. Start with an 8-element weight vector holding two 4-element groups, each with two nonzeros, paired against dense activations $x = [1..8]$. Dense execution runs 8 MACs to produce 73. The compressed 2:4 encoding instead packs the weights into 4 values $[2, 3, 4, 5]$ plus 2-bit within-group indices $[00, 11, 01, 10]$. Those indices drive a compact 4-to-2 hardware multiplexer (MUX) that selects the matching activations $[1, 4, 6, 7]$ without scattered memory reads. The Tensor Core then executes 4 MACs instead of 8 for the exact sum 73. Unstructured 50 percent sparsity misses this 2 times speedup because varying nonzero counts per group break fixed-width multiplexer routing and leave lanes idle.

    \notecue{Teach}{In a 50-minute class, skip the sparse MUX walkthrough and proceed directly to the INT8 ridge diagnosis.}

    \notecue{Next}{Return to dense INT8 and diagnose why quantizing ResNet-50 can drop a compute-bound convolution below the ridge.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Heterogeneous SoCs share memory under a cubic power wall}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=2.3cm,keepaspectratio]{iphone-xr-logic-board.jpg}\\[0.04cm]

        \footnotesize\textbf{Mobile envelope:} 3--5 W sustained (fanless); 10--15 W burst only. All engines share one LPDDR memory bus.
      \end{mlsyscard}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      \normalsize
      $P_{\text{dyn}} = \alpha\,C\,V^2 f \;\propto\; f^3$ \quad{\footnotesize(when $V \propto f$)}\\[0.25cm]
      \uncover<2->{\small\textbf{Why wide, moderate-clock arrays win:} doubling $f$ costs $\approx 8\times$ dynamic power; TPU v1 clocked 65,536 MACs at 700 MHz.\\[0.22cm]}
      \uncover<3->{\small\textbf{DVFS and shared LPDDR:} thermal governors cut $V$ and $f$ under sustained load; unsupported ops fall back to CPU with cache flushes and layout transposes.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Hardware Acceleration, \S11.10 (Heterogeneous SoC Design; CMOS dynamic power scaling). Photo: Sonic8400 (CC BY-SA 4.0), Wikimedia Commons.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Unlike a 700 W server accelerator, mobile silicon like Lecture 10's Apple A12 system-on-chip (SoC) packs a CPU, GPU, NPU, and image signal processor (ISP) on one die sharing one low-power memory (LPDDR) bus inside a fanless 3 to 5 W sustained envelope (10 to 15 W burst only). Recall from Lecture 2's power wall that dynamic complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) power follows $P_{\text{dyn}} = \alpha C V^2 f \propto f^3$ when voltage $V$ scales proportionally with clock $f$, with switching activity $\alpha$ (fraction of gates switching per cycle) and capacitance $C$. Doubling $f$ costs about 8 times the power, and cutting $f$ 20 percent saves far more than 20 percent power, explaining wide arrays at moderate clocks like TPU v1 at 700 MHz. Under sustained load, thermal governors apply dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS) to cut $V$ and $f$ (measured in Lecture 12), while CPU fallbacks force cache flushes (writing cached data back to DRAM), layout transposes (reordering tensor elements in memory), and shared-bus contention.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Short burst benchmarks hide DVFS throttling; sustained power defines mobile throughput.}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35cm}
    \item The ridge rose from 139 to 295 FLOP/byte in five years. Should model designers chase intensity, or chip designers spend area on bandwidth?
    \item DLRM's tables exceed one H100's memory and keyword spotting needs none of its arithmetic. Should one design serve all five lighthouses, or each regime get its own chip?
    \item Much of the field runs on one vendor's software stack. Is that lock-in a cost, or a reason the field moved fast?
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{0.5 min}{Core}%
    Those four principles leave three open architectural questions with no single answer. First, should designers chase higher arithmetic intensity or spend silicon on bandwidth, a trade-off Lecture 12 revisits? Second, across the five lighthouses, should one general accelerator serve all regimes, or should each regime get a specialized chip? Third, how should engineers weigh single-vendor software speed against ecosystem lock-in?
