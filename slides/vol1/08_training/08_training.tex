\mlsysnote{[0.5 min] First question: how long does the run take, and what sets that time?}
\mlsysdivider{How long does a training run take, and what does it cost?}{Model Training, \S8.2 and \S8.5}
\section{Time}

\begin{frame}{A training token costs $6P$ FLOPs; an inference token, $2P$}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{flops_per_token_step}{2}\\[0.35cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize Backward: one product for the activation gradient, one for the weight gradient.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Training, Lighthouse 8.1.}
  \note{[2 min] $P$ is the number of parameters. Step 1: in the forward pass every weight takes part in one multiply-add per token, two FLOPs, so a token costs $2P$. That is all inference pays. Step 2: the backward pass computes two matrix products per weight: one carries the error signal to the previous layer (the activation gradient), and one produces the gradient the optimizer needs (the weight gradient). That is $4P$, and a training token costs $6P$. Over a dataset of $D$ tokens, the work is $O \approx 6PD$. For GPT-2 XL that is $1.5\times10^{21}$ FLOPs. LINK: Lecture 5 derived both backward products for one layer. WARN: the $6P$ rule leaves out the attention-score products, which grow with sequence length squared; for GPT-2 XL at 1,024 tokens they add about 10 percent (320.8 against 295 TFLOP per step in the chapter's per-layer count).}
\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{[2 min] The training-time law, the iron law of Lecture 1 specialized to training. The numerator is the work, $6PD$: data times algorithm. The denominator is what the machine delivers: the peak rate at the chosen number format times the utilization, the fraction of that peak spent on useful model arithmetic. Every stall, data wait, synchronization, and slow kernel (a kernel is one operation's program on the accelerator) lowers the utilization. Three levers: do less work, buy a higher peak, or waste less of it. Then point to the last line: memory appears nowhere in the law. A step that does not fit in memory runs at no speed, so memory is a gate, checked before the law applies. LINK: Lecture 1's iron law has the same three factors.}
\mlsysfocus{The Training-Time Law}{%
  $\displaystyle T_{\text{train}} = \frac{\overbrace{6PD}^{\text{work, } O}}{\underbrace{R_{\text{peak}}}_{\text{peak rate}} \times \underbrace{\eta_{\text{hw}}}_{\text{utilization}}}$\\[0.6cm]
  {\small\textcolor{white!75}{Memory is not in the law. It decides whether the step runs at all.}}%
}

\mlsysnote{[0.5 min] The law said nothing about memory. Now the gate: what must the step hold?}
\mlsysdivider{What does a training step have to hold in memory?}{Model Training, \S8.3.2 and \S8.3.3}
\section{Memory}

\begin{frame}{Training holds four kinds of state; inference holds one}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{train_state_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Training, \S8.3.2 (simplified static view: parameters, gradients, and Adam's two moments, 1:1:2).}
  \note{[2.5 min] Build the memory bar one term at a time. Step 1: the weights. Inference keeps these (plus a small working set) and nothing else. Step 2: gradients, one per weight, the same size again. Step 3: the optimizer's state. Adam, the default optimizer for language models, keeps two running averages per weight: the first moment $m$ (a smoothed gradient, momentum) and the second moment $v$ (a smoothed squared gradient, which scales each weight's step size). Weights, gradients, and moments sit in a 1:1:2 ratio. Plain SGD keeps no extra state, momentum keeps one value per weight, Adam two. Step 4: the activations, the forward values autodiff saves for the backward pass (Lecture 7). They are the only term that grows with batch size and sequence length. WARN: the width of the activation block is drawn for illustration; the next slides put numbers on every term.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mixed-precision Adam holds 16 bytes per parameter}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{static_bytes.pdf}\\[0.3cm]
  {\normalsize Eight times the 16-bit weights an inference server stores.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Training, \S8.3.2.}
  \note{[1.5 min] The common recipe, mixed precision (16-bit arithmetic with 32-bit bookkeeping, detailed later today): 2 bytes for the 16-bit weights, 2 for the 16-bit gradients, 4 for a 32-bit master copy of the weights that the optimizer updates, and 8 for Adam's two 32-bit moments. Sixteen bytes per parameter, against the 2 bytes an inference server holds: eight times. WARN: this is before a single activation is stored. A 7B model that serves from 14 GB needs 112 GB to train this way.}
\end{frame}

\section{Feeding}

\begin{frame}{From NVMe to HBM, bandwidth rises about 480 times}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.8cm,keepaspectratio]{bw_ladder_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Training, \S8.4.2.}
  \note{[1.5 min] Training data crosses three tiers on its way to the arithmetic units. Step 1: a local NVMe solid-state drive, about 7 GB/s for large sequential reads; shuffled, random reads can drop that by ten times. Step 2: the host's main memory (DRAM), about 50 GB/s. Step 3: the H100's HBM, 3,350 GB/s, about 480 times the drive. Data that moves freely inside the accelerator becomes a bottleneck when it must come from disk. GPT-2's 40 GB WebText corpus fits in host memory, so once cached it never touches the drive; image datasets often do not fit. LINK: Lecture 4 covered storage tiers and file formats.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The slowest stage sets the pace of every step}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{pipeline_stages.pdf}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Training, \S8.4.2, equation 6.}
  \note{[2 min] Every batch passes three stages: the host prepares it (reads, decodes, augments or tokenizes), the PCIe link (the bus between host and accelerator) transfers it, and the accelerator computes the forward and backward passes. Convert each to batches per second; the pipeline runs at the minimum. Example from the chapter: GPT-2 XL with tokenization on one CPU core takes about 655 ms to prepare a batch, against a 596 ms step on the device, while the PCIe copy of 262 KB takes 0.004 ms. Run one after the other, the accelerator is busy only about 48 percent of the time. ENGAGE: which stage is the bottleneck? (Expected: preparation, by a wide margin; the link is irrelevant.)}
\end{frame}

(Expected: more host cores per accelerator, decoding on the accelerator, or caching decoded images. Not a faster accelerator.)}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Overlap hides the faster stage, not the slowest one}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.8cm,keepaspectratio]{prefetch_overlap.pdf}
  \mlsyscredit{Schematic, after Model Training, \S8.6.2.}
  \note{[1.5 min] Top: serial. The host prepares a batch, then the accelerator computes, then the host prepares the next; four batches take 140 units. Bottom: overlapped. While the accelerator computes batch $i$, the host prepares batch $i+1$ (prefetching); four batches take 95. The overlapped schedule is set by its slowest stage, here preparation at 20 units against compute at 15, so the accelerator still waits 5 units per batch. Overlap removes the sum; only more workers or faster preparation raise the minimum. In PyTorch the knobs are num\_workers, prefetch\_factor, and pin\_memory (page-locked host memory so copies run asynchronously); their price is host memory and CPU. LINK: Lecture 7 introduced these DataLoader settings.}
\end{frame}

  \mlsysactivity{Diagnose}{3 min}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.95\textwidth,height=3.3cm,keepaspectratio]{fig-tf-bottleneck-trace.png}\\[0.2cm]
  {\normalsize Python threads prepare each batch in 250 ms; the GPU computes it in 12 ms.}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize Busy $= 12 / (250 + 12) \approx$ \textbf{4.6\%}. The data path binds.\\[0.1cm]}
  \uncover<3->{\normalsize Fix: process workers, prefetching, or decoding on the accelerator.}
  \mlsyscredit{Trace: Model Training, \S8.5 (data-bound profiler trace). Numbers: Example 8.2.}
  \note{[3 min] Explain the trace first: time runs left to right; the upper rows are GPU streams, the bottom rows the host's Python thread. The GPU works in short bursts separated by wide gaps while the host is busy. ENGAGE: the class names the binding resource before any reveal. Step 2: preparation 250 ms, compute 12 ms, so the accelerator is busy about 4.6 percent of the time. A data problem in D·A·M terms; no kernel optimization helps. Step 3: the cause is Python's global interpreter lock (GIL), which lets one thread run Python code at a time, so the preparation threads take turns. Separate worker processes (num\_workers greater than zero) avoid it; libraries such as NVIDIA DALI decode on the accelerator. WARN: a twice-as-fast GPU changes this step from 262 ms to 256 ms. Land the segment: throughput is the minimum of preparation, transfer, and compute; overlap hides the faster stages but cannot speed up the slowest.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{16-bit arithmetic cuts GPT-2 XL's run from 518 days to 35}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{precision_days_step}{3}\\[0.2cm]
  \uncover<3->{\normalsize Peak ratios bound the gain; elementwise work and input waits do not speed up.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Training, Napkin Math 8.7 (H100 peaks; 50\% utilization; \$4/hour).}
  \note{[2 min] Mixed precision first, for speed. The H100 runs 32-bit math on its ordinary cores at 67 TFLOP/s, a 32-bit-like format (TF32) on its matrix units (Tensor Cores) at 494, and 16-bit BF16 on the same units at 989, 15 times the FP32 rate. Step 1: GPT-2 XL in FP32, about 518 days on one H100. Step 2: TF32, about 70 days. Step 3: BF16, about 35 days, the number from the time segment. WARN: these hold utilization fixed at 50 percent. \begin{frame}{FP16 needs loss scaling; BF16 usually does not}
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \node[font=\scriptsize\bfseries, text=midgray] at (1.4,1.85) {sign, \textcolor{BlueLine}{exponent bits}, \textcolor{OrangeLine}{mantissa bits}};
    \node[anchor=west, font=\scriptsize\bfseries, text=midgray] at (9.2,1.85) {smallest normal};
    \fprow{1.2}{FP32}{8}{23}{$\approx 10^{-38}$}
    \fprow{0.3}{FP16}{5}{10}{$6.1\times10^{-5}$}
    \fprow{-0.6}{BF16}{8}{7}{$\approx 10^{-38}$}
  \end{tikzpicture}\\[0.4cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize Smaller gradients round to zero in FP16. Fix: multiply the loss by $S$,\\divide the gradients by $S$ before the update.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Training, \S8.6.3 and table 9; Micikevicius et al. 2018.}
  \note{[2 min] One block per bit, to scale. The exponent sets the range a format can hold; the mantissa sets its precision. FP16 has 5 exponent bits, so its smallest normal value is about $6.1\times10^{-5}$. BF16 keeps FP32's 8 exponent bits and gives up mantissa, so its range matches FP32. Step 2: deep in the backward pass many gradients fall below $6\times10^{-5}$ and round to zero in FP16, and training stalls. Loss scaling multiplies the loss by a large factor $S$, such as $2^{10}$; by the chain rule every gradient grows by $S$. Dividing by $S$ before the update restores the true values. Frameworks adjust $S$ automatically, halving it and skipping the step on overflow. BF16 usually needs none of this and is the default on A100 and H100. WARN: the FP32 master weights solve a different problem: an update more than about 2,048 times smaller than its weight vanishes when added in FP16.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{FlashAttention swaps a 67 MB matrix for a 65.5 KB tile}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.7cm,keepaspectratio]{flash_tile_step}{2}\\[0.2cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize Same arithmetic, exact result: scores are rebuilt tile by tile, never stored.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Training, \S8.6.4 (4,096 tokens, FP32 scores, one head); Dao et al. 2022.}
  \note{[3 min] Step 1: standard attention computes the full score matrix, sequence length by sequence length, for every head, writes it to HBM, reads it back for the softmax, and keeps it for backward. At 4,096 tokens one head's FP32 scores are 67.1 MB. Step 2: FlashAttention splits queries, keys, and values into tiles that fit the on-chip SRAM, the small fast memory beside the arithmetic units. A $128\times128$ tile is 65.5 KB, 1,024 times smaller. The softmax divides by a sum over the whole row; the online softmax keeps a running maximum and sum per row and rescales partial results as tiles arrive, so the answer is exact. Backward recomputes tiles instead of reading a stored matrix. Per head, the stored footprint drops from 138.4 MB to 4.2 MB; the paper reports up to 3 times faster training on its workloads. WARN: the arithmetic is unchanged; only bytes moved and stored fall. LINK: Lecture 11 puts tiling on the roofline.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Checkpointing keeps segment inputs and rebuilds one segment}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{ckpt_segments_step}{2}\\[0.3cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize Activations: 85.6 GB to 11.5 GB, for one extra forward pass.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Training, Napkin Math 8.8 (48 layers in 8 segments of 6) and \S8.6.5.2.}
  \note{[2 min] Activation checkpointing (not the training checkpoints of the PaLM story; the name is shared). Step 1: split GPT-2 XL's 48 layers into 8 segments of 6. During the forward pass, keep only each segment's input, 32 by 1,024 by 1,600 values in 16-bit: 0.84 GB for all eight. Drop everything inside the segments. Step 2: during the backward pass, rerun the forward pass of one segment from its saved input, use its activations, free them, and move to the previous segment: 10.7 GB live at a time. Activations fall from 85.6 GB to 11.5 GB, and the whole step to 35.5 GB. The price is one extra forward pass of every layer: a step that cost three forward passes now costs four, about 33 percent more arithmetic. The general rule: checkpoints every $\sqrt{L}$ layers cut activation memory from order $L$ to order $\sqrt{L}$; for 48 layers, about 7 checkpoints save 71 percent.}
\end{frame}

WARN, two misconceptions: accumulation does not multiply memory by the number of micro-batches, since only one micro-batch's activations are alive at a time; and it reproduces the large-batch gradient only if each micro-batch's loss is divided by the number of micro-batches. \begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25cm}
    \item Run time is $6PD$ over peak times utilization; utilization moves time and energy together.
    \item Mixed-precision Adam holds 16 bytes per parameter before any activation.
    \item Activations grow with batch and sequence length; at batch 32 they decide what fits.
    \item Throughput is the slowest of preparation, transfer, and compute. Profile first.
    \item Fit, then feed, then speed up. Each technique relieves one term and costs another.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{[1 min] One line per segment, plus the order of work. Ask students to star the one they would have gotten wrong before today.}
\end{frame}
