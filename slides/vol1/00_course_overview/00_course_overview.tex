\begin{frame}{Moving a byte costs far more energy than computing with it}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{energy_per_op_step}{3}\\[0.1cm]
  \uncover<3->{\normalsize Reading a byte from DRAM costs over 100 times an FP16 multiply.}
  \mlsyscredit{Introduction, \S1.7, after Horowitz 2014 (45 nm process, DDR3 DRAM; per byte read and per operation).}
  \note{[3 min] [CORE] FP16 and INT8 are number formats: 16-bit floating point and 8-bit integers; smaller formats cost less energy per operation (Lecture 10). A picojoule (pJ) is a trillionth of a joule. Step 1: reading one byte from off-chip DRAM, about 160 pJ. Step 2: one FP16 multiply, about 1.1 pJ. Step 3: one INT8 multiply, about 0.2 pJ. The book's ratio is about 145 to 1 per byte against one multiply. WARN: these are Horowitz's 45 nm figures; modern HBM costs less per byte, and an FP16 multiply reads more than one byte of operands, so say "over a hundred times" rather than defending the exact ratio. Whether movement dominates a whole workload depends on how often each byte is reused. LINK: Lecture 11.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Generating text is limited by memory, not arithmetic}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{roofline_train_decode_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Arithmetic intensity: FLOPs done per byte moved from memory.}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small One user at a time: about 1 FLOP per byte, far below the ridge.}
  \mlsyscredit{H100 ridge point (dense FP16): Hardware Acceleration, \S11.6. Decode: Model Serving, \S13.9. Training point's position illustrative.}
  \note{[2.5 min] [OPTIONAL] This plot is the roofline; Lecture 11 builds it properly, with rooflines for real accelerators. Define the horizontal axis first: arithmetic intensity is the number of FLOPs a workload does for each byte it moves from memory. The dashed line is the H100's ridge point, about 295 FLOP per byte: its peak arithmetic rate, 989 TFLOP/s, divided by its memory bandwidth, 3.35 TB/s (Hardware Acceleration). Right of the ridge, performance is limited by arithmetic; left of it, by how fast memory delivers bytes. Step 1: generating text for one user. Each new token reads every weight of the model once and does about two operations per weight, so with 16-bit weights it performs about one operation per byte moved. It sits low on the memory slope, and most of the arithmetic units wait. Step 2: training with a large batch reuses each weight across many examples, so it sits on the compute ceiling; its exact position is illustrative. The same chip is limited by memory in the first workload and by arithmetic in the second. This is why serving systems batch users together (Lecture 13) and why accelerator designers care about memory bandwidth. FLEX: if cut, say on the next slide that generating text for one user is limited by memory reads, not arithmetic.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A poorly used accelerator spends six times the energy per token}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=3.8cm,keepaspectratio]{energy_utilization_step}{2}\\[0.3cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize At 10\% of the batched rate, the GPU still draws 300 W and the host 300 W.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Serving, Napkin Math 13.5.}

\end{frame}

      \only<4|handout:0>{\textbf{Machine}: where it runs.}%
      \only<5-|handout:1>{\textbf{Data}: what it learns from.\\[0.2cm]\textbf{Algorithm}: what it computes.\\[0.2cm]\textbf{Machine}: where it runs.\\[0.4cm]{\small Fixing one axis often moves the bottleneck to another.}}%
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{[2.5 min] [CORE] D·A·M, applied to the chatbot from the opening. Step 1: three axes joined by edges, because relieving one bottleneck often exposes another. Step 2, data: what the model was trained on, and whether today's users still ask the kinds of questions it saw. Step 3, algorithm: the FLOPs each token needs, set by the model's size. Step 4, machine: the bytes the accelerator must read for each token, and how fast it can read them. Step 5: the three together; every lecture says which axis it works on. WARN: students treat the axes as separate. Fixing one moves the bottleneck to another: batching raises the machine's efficiency and also raises each user's wait.}
\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{[3 min] [CORE] The iron law as the book writes it (Introduction, equation 2): time is samples per task, times FLOPs per sample, times seconds per FLOP, one factor per D·A·M axis. Samples are the units of work: images, examples, or tokens. The units cancel to seconds. Point to the line beneath the equation: the GPT-3 estimate from earlier was this law with its factors grouped. The first two factors multiply to the total work, 3.14 times 10 to the 23 FLOPs. Seconds per FLOP is one over the rate the cluster achieves, 1,024 GPUs times 312 TFLOP/s times 0.45, about 7 times 10 to the minus 18 seconds. The product is about 2.2 million seconds, about 25 days. Seconds per FLOP is the realized time per operation, not the datasheet peak, so it already includes memory stalls and idle time; that is why the decode example earlier still fits. Lecture 1 unpacks the machine factor into memory, compute, and overhead terms. For students who know computer architecture: it has the same form as the CPU iron law, instructions per program times cycles per instruction times seconds per cycle.}
\mlsysfocus{The Iron Law of ML Systems}{%
  $\displaystyle T = \underbrace{\frac{\text{Samples}}{\text{Task}}}_{\text{Data}} \times
   \underbrace{\frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{Sample}}}_{\text{Algorithm}} \times
   \underbrace{\frac{\text{Seconds}}{\text{FLOP}}}_{\text{Machine}}$\\[0.6cm]
  {\small\textcolor{white!75}{GPT-3 on 1,024 GPUs, from the earlier estimate:}}\\[0.25cm]
  {\small\textcolor{white!75}{$\underbrace{3.14\times10^{23}\ \text{FLOPs}}_{\text{Samples} \times \text{FLOPs/Sample}} \times \underbrace{7\times10^{-18}\ \text{s/FLOP}}_{1/(1{,}024 \times 312\times10^{12} \times 0.45)} \approx 2.2\times10^{6}\ \text{s} \approx 25\ \text{days}$}}%
}
