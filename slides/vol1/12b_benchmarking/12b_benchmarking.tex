\begin{frame}{Identical runs of one server differ by up to 1 percent}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.8cm,keepaspectratio]{strip_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  \uncover<3->{\small Sources: \textbf{machine} (clocks, OS jitter) \quad \textbf{algorithm} (seed, data order) \quad \textbf{sample} (finite test set)}
  \mlsyscredit{MLPerf Training v4.0 closed, ResNet-50, four submissions, 20 runs (result\_0 to result\_4). CV over converged runs, derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    Plotting all 20 MLPerf runs against each submission's median exposes run-to-run spread across all four systems on one axis. Eos's five runs span 0.99 percent, while the other three submissions cluster within about 0.2 percent. Two non-converging runs, on Dell and 64-GPU Eos, sit off scale at 6.0 and 5.4 percent slower. The coefficient of variation (CV), standard deviation (SD) divided by the mean over converged runs, reaches 0.48 percent on Eos against 0.06 percent on Dell, 0.07 percent on H200, and 0.04 percent on 64 GPUs. That 7.5x noisiness spread has no cause recorded in the logs. Noise comes from three sources, machine (clocks, OS scheduling jitter, heat), algorithm (random seed, data order, parallel summation or reduction order), and sample (a finite test set). For scale, Tummalapalli et al. (2026) measured 2.2 percent CV on laptop LLM decoding.

    \notecue{Watch for}{There is no universal cutoff such as 5 percent CV (Perspective 12.4). Judge CV relative to the effect size needed.}

    \notecue{Next}{Those runs varied in time, so ask next how much the same random seeds move final accuracy.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Within one recipe, seeds move accuracy by a third of a point}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.65cm,keepaspectratio]{seedacc_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{MLPerf Training v4.0 logs (last eval\_accuracy, 20 runs, one seed each). Binomial 95\% half-width at 76\% on 50,000 images, derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Optional}%
    Random seeds move accuracy as well as runtime. Across the same 20 runs, code, data, and training settings (hyperparameters) stay fixed within each submission while only the seed, data order, and reduction order change. Final \texttt{eval\_accuracy} still spans 75.88 to 76.21 percent, with two runs stopping just short of the 75.9 percent target. Seed spread alone reaches 0.32 points overall and 0.31 points within Eos. Finite-sample binomial error on 50,000 test images adds roughly $\pm 0.37$ points at 95 percent confidence, meaning such an interval contains the true value in 95 of 100 repetitions. A single-run 0.2-point gain therefore sits inside seed noise alone. Outside vision classification, Henderson et al. (2018) showed reported gains in trial-and-error learning evaporating into seed noise, and Lucic et al. (2018) showed rankings of image generators reversing across seeds.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{MLPerf drops the fastest and slowest run, then averages}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=3.5cm,keepaspectratio]{olympic_step}{3}\\[0.02cm]
  {\footnotesize\textit{``Each benchmark result is computed by dropping the fastest and slowest runs, then taking the mean of the remaining times. For this purpose, a single non-converging run may be treated as the slowest run and dropped.''}}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<3->{\small Published score: $13.163 \times 1.0042 = 13.22$ min.}
  \mlsyscredit{Quoted: mlcommons/training\_policies, training\_rules.adoc, Scoring (commit 9f32f97, April 2024). Dell runs: MLPerf Training v4.0.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Optional}%
    Because identical runs fluctuate and can even miss the target, MLPerf's verbatim scoring rule fixes how five runs combine. Image classification requires at least 5 runs, and a second non-converging run invalidates the submission. Dell's fifth run took 13.94 min and aborted without reaching 75.9 percent. Under Olympic scoring, which drops the fastest and slowest runs, treating one failed run as the slowest ensures convergence risk counts against time to quality, while trimming both ends resists both one lucky run and one stalled outlier. Averaging Dell's middle three runs gives 13.163 min, and multiplying by the round's committee convergence factor 1.0042 yields the published 13.22 min score.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Cherry-picking the best set of five runs is forbidden. Rules allow only a pre-declared sliding window of consecutive runs, reporting the median window.}

    \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip Olympic scoring and move straight to the sample-size calculation.}

    \notecue{Next}{Repeated runs absorb machine and seed noise, so turn next to the third source and estimate how many samples accuracy and latency tails need.}
  }
\end{frame}

  \mlsysactivity{Estimate}{2.5 min}
  \centering
  {\large $n = \dfrac{z^2\, p\,(1-p)}{m^2}$}\quad
  {\footnotesize $p$: fraction that passes;\; $m$: margin;\; $z$: 1.96 at 95\%, 2.576 at 99\%}\\[0.15cm]
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccccr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Claim} & $p$ & $m$ & \textbf{Confidence} & $n$ & \textbf{MLPerf} \\

    p90: share under bound $\pm$ 0.5 points & 0.90 & 0.005 & 99\% & \uncover<3->{\textbf{23,886}} & \uncover<3->{24,576} \\
    p99: share under bound $\pm$ 0.05 points & 0.99 & 0.0005 & 99\% & \uncover<3->{\textbf{262,742}} & \uncover<3->{270,336} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<4->{\safeimg[width=0.85\textwidth,height=1.95cm,keepaspectratio]{sizing.pdf}}
  \mlsyscredit{Benchmarking, Napkin Math 12.5 (1,825 images). MLPerf Inference rules, minimum query counts (mlcommons/inference\_policies). $n$ derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    Accuracy and latency percentiles both estimate a pass fraction $p$, so both size sample noise via $n = z^2 p(1-p)/m^2$ with rank margin $m$ and multiplier $z$ (1.96 at 95 percent, 2.576 at 99 percent). Compute $n$ for 95 percent accuracy within $\pm 1$ point at 95 percent confidence.

    \notecue{Check}{Row 1 gives $1.96^2 \times 0.95 \times 0.05 / 0.01^2 = 1{,}825$ images (Napkin Math 12.5). A tempting error is treating $m$ as milliseconds, not rank. At 99 percent confidence in MLPerf Inference, p90 within $\pm 0.5$ points needs 23,886 queries (rounded to 24,576) and p99 within $\pm 0.05$ points needs 262,742 (270,336, with early stopping allowing fewer well inside the bound). Deep tails need over 100x more samples than accuracy (Lecture 13 covers queues).}

    \notecue{Watch for}{A set sized for aggregate accuracy is too small for a 5 percent slice (Section 4).}

    \notecue{Teach}{Give one minute in pairs on row 1 before showing the answers.}

    \notecue{Next}{Having sized one-system noise across runs and samples, Section 2 tests whether a gap is real, attributable, and useful.}

  \note{%

    Shrinking an interval takes many more runs than intuition suggests, as the eight-line script images/runs\_demo.py shows assuming Eos's five-run variance stays stable. At MLPerf's minimum of 5 runs, the 95 percent half-width (the plus-or-minus margin around Eos's 13.26 min mean) is $\pm 0.59$ percent. Scaling to 20 runs cuts it to $\pm 0.22$ percent under the $1/\sqrt{n}$ law, because quadrupling runs halves the interval. By the two-sample power formula $2(1.96 + 0.84)^2 s^2 / \delta^2$, an 80 percent chance of detecting a 0.5 percent gap needs about 15 runs per system, whereas a 1 percent gap needs about 4.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Fix run counts from a statistical power target before collecting data. Stopping early once a gap looks significant invalidates the interval.}

    \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip this script and move straight to paired model comparison with McNemar's test.}

      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.8cm,keepaspectratio]{ece_step}{3}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \small
      \textbf{Quality beyond accuracy:} is the confidence right?\\[0.3cm]
      $\text{ECE} = \displaystyle\sum_{i} \frac{|\text{bin}_i|}{n}\,\bigl|\text{acc}_i - \text{conf}_i\bigr|$\\[0.3cm]
      \calcline{2}{gaps: 0.05, 0.05, 0.13}
      \calcline{3}{weights: 20, 30, 50 of 100 predictions}
      \calcline{3}{$0.2 \times 0.05 + 0.3 \times 0.05 + 0.5 \times 0.13 = 0.09$}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Benchmarking, \S12.9.1, equation 4 (Pakdaman Naeini et al. 2015). Bins constructed for this slide.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    Clearing a hardware frontier checks accuracy and speed, not whether confidence scores match reality. Answering Lecture 10b's open question, expected calibration error (ECE) measures the average gap between predicted confidence and observed accuracy. Here a reliability diagram groups 100 predictions into three confidence bins against the diagonal of perfect calibration. In the lowest bin, 20 predictions near 0.65 confidence reach 60 percent accuracy, and 30 near 0.85 reach 80 percent. In the highest bin, 50 predictions near 0.97 reach only 84 percent, exposing overconfidence. The three absolute gaps are 0.05, 0.05, and 0.13. Weighting each gap by its share gives $\text{ECE} = 0.2(0.05) + 0.3(0.05) + 0.5(0.13) = 0.09$, matching the INT8 camera-trap value ahead. On a camera trap alerting above 85 percent confidence, that overconfidence triggers false alerts.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Confidence calibration differs from Lecture 10b's quantization range calibration (fitting integer clipping ranges) despite sharing the word. Also fix the bin count and estimator before comparing models.}

    \notecue{Next}{Ask whether deeper, more accurate networks calibrate better on real benchmarks, and how temperature scaling repairs confidence.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The more accurate network was three times worse calibrated}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.8cm,keepaspectratio]{guo_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Temperature scaling divides the logits by one fitted constant, so the top class never changes.}
  \mlsyscredit{Measured: Guo, Pleiss, Sun, Weinberger, ``On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks,'' ICML 2017, Figure 1 and Table 1 (15 bins).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Higher accuracy does not guarantee better calibration. In the study that formalized modern neural network calibration, Guo et al. (2017) used 15 bins to show that on CIFAR-100, the deeper ResNet-110 beats LeNet-5 in error rate (30.6 versus 44.9 percent) yet records over 3x worse ECE (16.53 versus 4.85 percent). They also showed that temperature scaling, a post-hoc correction that divides every logit (raw class score, from Lecture 5) by one positive constant fitted on held-out validation data, cuts ResNet-152 ImageNet ECE from 5.48 to 1.86 percent. Because dividing every logit by one positive constant preserves the highest-scoring class, temperature scaling repairs confidence without changing top-1 accuracy or fixing misclassified nighttime species.

    \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip Guo et al. and move straight to unseen camera-trap shifts in WILDS.}

    \notecue{Next}{Even calibrated models can still fail on rare groups in new environments, so measure what aggregate accuracy hides on unseen camera traps.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]{Verification gap: a test set samples almost no inputs}
  \mlsysactivity{Estimate}{1.5 min}
  \vspace{-0.15cm}
  \centering
  {\normalsize How many different 224 $\times$ 224 color images with 8-bit pixels exist?}\\[0.18cm]
  \raggedright\hspace{1.2cm}\begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\footnotesize
  \calcline{2}{Values per image: $224 \times 224 \times 3 = 150{,}528$, each one of 256 \quad$\Rightarrow$\quad $256^{150{,}528} \approx 10^{362{,}508}$ images}
  \calcline{3}{ImageNet validation set: $50{,}000 \approx 10^{4.7}$ \quad$\Rightarrow$\quad coverage $\approx 10^{-362{,}503}$}
  \end{minipage}\\[0.12cm]
  \centering
  \uncover<3->{\safeimg[width=0.88\textwidth,height=2.25cm,keepaspectratio]{verif_gap.pdf}\\[0.08cm]}
  \uncover<4->{\small A passed test certifies the population it sampled, within the margin its size allows. Nothing else.}
  \mlsyscredit{Benchmarking, \S12.9 (the verification gap: a test set covers an effectively zero fraction of the input space). Counts derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Both the unresolved edge slice and the 16-point unseen-trap drop follow from Lecture 1's verification gap, the limit on what any finite test set can cover. Before reading on, estimate the order of magnitude of all possible 224x224 RGB color images with 8-bit channels.

    \notecue{Check}{With $224\times 224\times 3 = 150{,}528$ 8-bit channels, there are $256^{150{,}528} \approx 10^{362{,}508}$ possible images. A tempting guess treats the count like a large dataset, overlooking how multiplying 256 choices per channel explodes exponentially. ImageNet's 50,000 validation images ($10^{4.7}$) therefore cover $10^{-362{,}503}$ of input space, effectively zero. Because a passed test certifies only the sampled population within its margin, teams defend by sampling deployment strata (subgroups) and monitoring post-release drift (Lecture 14b).}

    \notecue{Teach}{Give one minute for an order-of-magnitude guess before revealing the exponent.}

    \notecue{Next}{Section 4 showed that shipping requires frontier, calibration, and slice evidence under this sampling limit. Return to the opening Dell-versus-Eos comparison to deliver three verdicts and unite all six conditions.}
  }
\end{frame}
