\begin{frame}{ECE is the average gap between confidence and accuracy}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.8cm,keepaspectratio]{ece_step}{3}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \small
      $\text{ECE} = \displaystyle\sum_{i} \frac{|\text{bin}_i|}{n}\,\bigl|\text{acc}_i - \text{conf}_i\bigr|$\\[0.3cm]
      \calcline{2}{gaps: 0.05, 0.05, 0.13}
      \calcline{3}{weights: 20, 30, 50 of 100 predictions}
      \calcline{3}{$0.2 \times 0.05 + 0.3 \times 0.05 + 0.5 \times 0.13 = 0.09$}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Benchmarking, \S12.9.1, equation 4 (Pakdaman Naeini et al. 2015). Bins constructed for this slide.}
  \note{[3 min] A well-calibrated model that says 80 percent is right about 80 percent of the time. Step 1: a reliability diagram for 100 predictions, grouped by confidence into three bins. The 20 predictions near 0.65 confidence were right 60 percent of the time; the 30 near 0.85, 80 percent; the 50 near 0.97, only 84 percent. The dashed line is perfect calibration. Step 2: the gap in each bin, shaded: 0.05, 0.05, 0.13. The model is overconfident, most in its most confident bin. Step 3: expected calibration error (ECE) weights each gap by the bin's share of predictions: 0.2 times 0.05 plus 0.3 times 0.05 plus 0.5 times 0.13, about 0.09. That is the size of the INT8 model's ECE in the chapter's camera-trap table. ENGAGE: why does this matter for the camera trap? Expected: if it only alerts above 85 percent confidence, an overconfident model sends wrong alerts it should have held back. WARN: the calibration of quantization ranges in Lecture 10b is a different procedure with the same name. ECE also depends on the number of bins, so a protocol must fix the estimator before comparing models. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The more accurate network was three times worse calibrated}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.8cm,keepaspectratio]{guo_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Temperature scaling divides the logits by one fitted constant: the top class never changes.}
  \mlsyscredit{Measured: Guo, Pleiss, Sun, Weinberger, ``On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks,'' ICML 2017, Figure 1 and Table 1 (15 bins).}
  \note{[2 min] Measured calibration. Step 1: Guo and colleagues compared a 1998 network, LeNet 5, with a 2016 ResNet-110 on CIFAR-100. Step 2: the fix they found most effective on vision tasks, temperature scaling. Divide the logits (the scores before the softmax) by one constant fitted on held-out data. On ImageNet, ResNet-152's ECE fell from 5.48 to 1.86 percent. Dividing every score by the same positive number cannot change which class is largest, so accuracy is unchanged. ENGAGE: could temperature scaling fix a model that misses rare species at night? Expected: no; it changes confidence, not which class wins. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}
