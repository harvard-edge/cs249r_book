\begin{frame}{Each monitoring layer sees a different failure at a different delay}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{layers_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{After ML Operations, figure 11 (the uptime iceberg) and \S14.6.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Part 1 showed that green serving gauges hide data and model failures beneath the surface. The uptime iceberg on the left puts service uptime above the waterline and data and model health below, and the four monitoring layers on the right match those failures from fastest to slowest. At the top, infrastructure telemetry checks uptime, latency, and whether processors or memory are saturated within seconds. Beneath it, input checks inspect every batch at ingest for missing columns, wrong types, nulls, and out-of-range values, which would have caught Unity's bad customer batch immediately. At the base, outcome monitoring computes accuracy against ground-truth labels only after labels arrive, which is how Unity's revenue drop surfaced.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The deepest layer is not ``best'' on its own. Each deeper layer is slower, so fast upper checks exist specifically to prevent business outcomes from becoming the first alarm.}

\begin{frame}{A missing label is pending until its window closes}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{join_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{ML Operations, \S14.6.2, joining predictions to outcomes.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Input checks catch malformed batches at ingest, but confirming live accuracy requires joining predictions with outcomes that arrive later (\S14.6.2). First, the pipeline joins the prediction log (request ID, timestamp, score, and served model version) to the outcome log on request ID, crediting each outcome to the version that served it even after newer models deploy. Under a fixed conversion window, each request sits in one of three states, an observed purchase inside the window (positive), a closed window with no purchase (negative), or an open window (pending and still unknown). For example, a morning request under a 7-day window stays pending until next week. Every reported metric and slice must therefore display label coverage, the share of eligible predictions with resolved outcomes.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Counting pending requests as negatives systematically depresses the apparent performance of every newly deployed candidate model.}

    \notecue{Next}{Test this label-coverage rule on a 2-day-old candidate whose conversion rate looks 3 points worse.}
  }

\begin{frame}{Per-feature monitors miss a reversed relationship}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.88\textwidth,height=5.5cm,keepaspectratio]{joint_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{ML Operations, \S14.6.2 (feature distribution monitoring).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
    Even with clean input checks and mature outcome windows, single-feature drift monitors have a blind spot. Suppose two binary sensors each stay 50/50 in training and serving, so Lecture 4b's single-feature Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) and Population Stability Index (PSI) checks report zero drift. In training the flags always matched, both off or both on, whereas in serving they flipped to disagreeing, A on and B off or the reverse. \section{How Fast}

\begin{frame}{Half the drop needs about four times the evidence}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.78\textwidth,height=4.85cm,keepaspectratio]{evidence_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small 95\% confidence, 80\% power, from 95\% accuracy (derived).}
  \mlsyscredit{Counts derived with the binomial sizing of Lecture 12b. Budget: ML Operations, Napkin Math 14.2.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Starting from a 95 percent accuracy baseline, extend Lecture 12b's binomial sample-sizing formula to require both 95 percent confidence ($z = 1.96$) and 80 percent statistical power (adding $z_\beta \approx 0.84$, as in Part 1's A/B formula, which explains why a 1-point drop takes more than twice Lecture 12b's 1,825 confidence-only count). Confirming a 5-point drop takes 185 labeled outcomes, a 2-point drop takes 1,031, and a 1-point drop takes 3,932. Required evidence grows as $1/\Delta^2$, so halving the drop quadruples the sample count, pushing a 0.5-point drop to 15,329 derived outcomes, roughly 4x 3,932. Above that floor, the dashed line marks the chapter's 1,000-outcome operational budget for a 5-point drop (Napkin Math 14.2), leaving margin for noisy labels, correlated events, and per-slice checks.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The 1,000-outcome budget is an operational safety policy set above the 185-outcome statistical floor, not a mathematical constant.}

    \notecue{Next}{Convert that 1,000-outcome budget into wall-clock confirmation time across three real domains.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{At a million samples, almost any shift is significant}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.78\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{ks_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Critical value $1.36\sqrt{2/n}$, 95\% (Lecture 4b, Napkin Math 4.5); the value at 1,000,000 derived. Threshold: ML Operations, listing 2.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Why does a significance test fail as an alert rule at production scale? Recall from Lecture 4b that the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test's 95 percent critical gap ($1.36\sqrt{2/n}$) already shrank from 0.061 at $n=1{,}000$ to 0.006 at $n=100{,}000$, forcing monitors to pair p-values with an effect-size cutoff. \begin{frame}{Each cycle without retraining loses a triangle of value}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.78\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{saw_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<4->{\small Cost per day $= \dfrac{C}{T} + \dfrac{1}{2}\,QVA_0\gamma\, T$\quad lowest where \textbf{triangle = C}}
  \mlsyscredit{ML Operations, equations 8 to 12 and table 17 (illustrative ranking service: $QVA_0\gamma$ = \$1,107 per day per day).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    To choose a retraining interval $T$ from numbers rather than habit, balance run cost against accuracy decay (Equations 8 to 12, Table 17). When accuracy decays as $A_0 e^{-\gamma t}$ with small $\gamma t$, $1 - e^{-\gamma t} \approx \gamma t$, so daily lost value grows linearly at $Q V A_0 \gamma$. On the ranking service, $Q = 10^6$/day, value per query per unit accuracy $V = \$0.50$, fresh accuracy $A_0 = 0.94$, and decay rate $\gamma \approx 0.00236$/day yield $Q V A_0 \gamma \approx \$1{,}107$ per day per day before resetting. Over a cycle of length $T$, cumulative loss equals the shaded triangle $\frac{1}{2}(1{,}107)T^2$. Adding run cost $C = \$5{,}000$ and dividing cycle cost by $T$ gives daily cost $C/T + \frac{1}{2} Q V A_0 \gamma T$. Shrinking $T$ raises run cost, stretching $T$ raises staleness cost, and daily cost is minimized where the staleness triangle equals $C$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{This sawtooth assumes steady, gradual decay. Sudden drops belong to incident response rather than routine retraining.}

    \notecue{Next}{Solve that equality for the closed-form square-root law governing the optimal retraining interval.}

\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{%

The optimal interval grows as $\sqrt{C}$ and shrinks as $1/\sqrt{Q V A_0 \gamma}$, so quadrupling any input changes $T^*$ by a factor of two. This approximation holds while fractional accuracy loss per cycle remains small (Equation 12).

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3cm}
    \item A fraud model's labels arrive within 120 days. Should a drift alert alone ever roll it back? Who pays for an unneeded rollback?
    \item One false page a week costs an engineer's sleep; one missed failure costs \$184,932. Who should set the threshold?
    \item Continuous retraining minimizes staleness and maximizes exposure to bad data. For a model trained on customers' uploads, is any automatic retraining safe?
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Optional}%
    Formulas price delay and cadence, yet three operational trade-offs still resist purely technical answers because they allocate risk and human cost. First, consider whether a label-free drift alert should ever trigger rollback when fraud labels lag by 120 days. Second, consider who should set alert thresholds that balance engineer sleep against \$184,932 incidents. Third, consider whether automatic continuous retraining is ever safe on customer-uploaded data, as in Unity's incident.
