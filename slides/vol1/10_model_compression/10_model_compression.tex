\begin{frame}{Most of ResNet-50's weights can go for about one point}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{prune_step}{3}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<3->{\normalsize ResNet-50: about 80\% of weights removed for about one point of top-1 accuracy.}
  \mlsyscredit{Gale, Elsen, Hooker 2019 (magnitude pruning with retraining), via Model Compression, \S10.2. Matrix illustrative.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Pruning starts from the observation that trained networks carry surplus parameters. In a 2019 empirical study, Gale, Elsen, and Hooker showed that magnitude pruning with retraining removes roughly 80 percent of ResNet-50's weights on ImageNet for about one point of top-1 accuracy. Formally, pruning minimizes loss while keeping at most $k$ nonzero weights, using weight magnitude (darker cells) as a tractable proxy. On this dense 6 by 6 matrix, unstructured pruning zeroes the 18 smallest entries while keeping the tensor shape unchanged, so ordinary dense kernels still fetch and multiply every zero. Structured pruning instead deletes entire columns, removing whole output feature-map channels to leave a smaller dense matrix that runs faster on standard phone CPU kernels without special support.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Dropping weights on paper is not the same as running faster on silicon. The upcoming sparsity slides quantify the storage and runtime costs of sparsity.}

    \notecue{Next}{Next, work through magnitude thresholding by hand on a 3 by 3 weight matrix.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Block-sparse GEMM skips empty tiles using dense inner kernels}

  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{blocksparse_step}{3}\\[0.08cm]

    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    To avoid paying an index and a scattered load for every weight, block-sparse general matrix-matrix multiply (GEMM, Lecture 6) sits between unstructured zeros and full channel deletion, grouping nonzeros into dense tiles so matrix units stay busy. \begin{frame}{Folding batch norm cuts a layer's traffic by 45 percent}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.88\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{fusion_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Compression, \S10.4.1.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Even after structural pruning settles a model's layers, unfused execution still wastes memory traffic. On a 256-channel, 28 by 28 ResNet-50 layer moving through high-bandwidth memory (HBM), running convolution, BatchNorm, and ReLU as three separate kernels requires six activation transfers plus the weight read. Convolution reads 802.8 KB of input and 2.4 MB of weights and writes 802.8 KB, totaling 4 MB. BatchNorm reads 802.8 KB and writes 802.8 KB (1.6 MB total), and ReLU repeats that 1.6 MB round-trip, totaling 7.2 MB. Once pruning fixes channel counts and inference freezes BatchNorm statistics, BatchNorm's scale and shift fold algebraically into the convolution weights and bias while ReLU runs in registers before writing. One fused kernel makes two activation transfers plus the weight read, totaling 4 MB, cutting traffic by 44.8 percent via Lecture 7's fusion rule that removes intermediate memory round-trips. At the H100 GPU's 3,350 GB/s bandwidth, ideal traffic-only lower bounds on time fall from 2.1 to 1.2 microseconds, a 1.81 times ratio for the traffic component alone (derived, ignoring compute).

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Even in the right order, combined compression levers shrink stored bytes multiplicatively without multiplying device speed. Removing half the weights promises 2 times speedup and quantizing to INT8 promises 4 times, multiplying to 8 times on paper, yet the chapter's illustrative pipeline delivers only 1.5 times, or 18.8 percent of that paper figure. As a real anchor, Han et al.'s 2016 Deep Compression study shrank AlexNet 35 times yet reached 3 to 4 times speedup only with custom sparse kernels. Three losses explain the gap. Dense kernels still stream zero weights unless structured channel pruning removes them, unfused format conversions add memory traffic between layers, and small layers are dominated by kernel launch overhead.

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.28cm}\normalsize
    \item Find the limiting term first: arithmetic for the camera frame, weight bytes for a decode token, capacity for a microcontroller.
    \item Move a dominated model to the accuracy-efficiency frontier before trading along it.
    \item Remove structure the hardware can drop, or train a smaller dense student.
    \item Sparsity saves bytes and time only past a break-even set by the format and the kernel.
    \item Order the levers and measure after each; the rest of the pipeline caps the gain.
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
    These five takeaways trace the lecture's arc from budget diagnosis through structure and execution to system measurement. Anchor each point in the slide or measurement that proved it, paying particular attention to the first and last, diagnosing the limiting budget term before acting and measuring after each ordered lever under Amdahl's cap.
