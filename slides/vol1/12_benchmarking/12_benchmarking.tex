\begin{frame}{One server's p99 is 10 ms; the whole request's is 140 ms}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.9cm,keepaspectratio]{tail_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Measured: Dean and Barroso, ``The Tail at Scale,'' CACM 2013, Table 1 (a Google service). Lower panel: Benchmarking, \S12.10 (illustrative).}
  \note{[3.5 min] Define the terms first. The median (p50) is the time half of requests beat; p99 is the time 99 percent of requests meet or beat. Step 1: one leaf server in a large Google service: median 1 ms, p99 10 ms. Step 2: the root sends each request to many leaves and waits for all of them. The median is now 40 ms and the p99 is 140 ms, 14 times the single-leaf p99. Each leaf is slow only 1 percent of the time, but a request that touches 100 of them meets at least one slow leaf about 63 percent of the time. Step 3: in the chapter's illustrative case, a model-only test reports a 15 ms mean on evenly spaced inputs; under production traffic the p99 is 150 to 200 ms. The camera trap's 33 ms frame deadline sits between them, so the mean says nothing about how many frames miss it. A service-level objective (SLO) is a target on a named indicator such as p99; an agreement with penalties is an SLA. WARN: students assume p99 is a rare-case curiosity. At a million requests, it is 10,000 users. LINK: generative services split this tail into time to first token and time per output token, and Lecture 13 explains how queues build it. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]{Estimate: how many requests hit a slow shard?}
  \mlsysactivity{Estimate}{2 min}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
      \begin{mlsyscard}{mlsysaccent}
        \small Each of 100 shards is slow 1\% of the time, independently. A request waits for all 100.\\[0.1cm]
        What fraction of requests waits on a slow one?
      \end{mlsyscard}\vspace{0.15cm}
      \uncover<2->{\small $P = 1 - 0.99^{100}$\\[0.05cm] $\phantom{P} = 1 - 0.366 = 63\%$}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=4.95cm,keepaspectratio]{fanout_step}{2}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Benchmarking, footnote 20 (Dean and Barroso, ``The Tail at Scale,'' CACM 2013); curve $1 - 0.99^n$ computed.}
  \note{[2.5 min] A worked estimate that explains the previous slide's 140 ms. A shard is one leaf server holding part of the data, so one request touches many shards. ENGAGE: one minute with a neighbor. Each shard is fast 99 percent of the time. Step 2: work it slowly on the board. The chance one shard is fast is 0.99. The chance all 100 are fast is 0.99 multiplied by itself 100 times, about 0.366. The chance at least one is slow is the rest: 1 minus 0.366, about 63 percent. Point to the curve: at 10 shards it is already 9.6 percent, the chapter's self-check answer. Students who answered 1 percent assumed the request is as slow as a typical shard; it is as slow as its slowest one. So a leaf's p99 becomes something close to the whole request's median, which is what the measured table showed: 40 ms median against one leaf's 1 ms. WARN: the formula assumes shards are slow independently; correlated slowdowns, such as a shared network switch, behave differently. LINK: Lecture 13 uses the complement, 36.6 percent of requests meeting the target, to motivate hedged requests. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A closed-loop client erases the stall it should measure}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{loop_step}{2}
  \note{[2 min] The client that sends requests is part of the measurement. Step 1: a closed-loop client sends one request, waits for the reply, then sends the next. The server stalls for 500 ms, for example in garbage collection. The client waits too, sends nothing, and records one slow request. Step 2: an open-loop client sends on its own schedule, the way independent users do; MLPerf's Server scenario uses random (Poisson) arrivals. Every request that arrives during the stall waits behind it, and the log shows 14 slow requests. The closed-loop client hid the tail it was built to find; this error is called coordinated omission. ENGAGE: which client does a load-testing script that loops ``send, wait, send'' implement? Expected: closed loop. LINK: Lecture 13 measures queues under load. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{A host timer measures the launch and misses the kernel}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.59\textwidth}
      {\footnotesize\textbf{timer\_demo.py}, after its imports}
\vspace{-0.25cm}
\begin{semiverbatim}\scriptsize
x = torch.randn(8192, 8192, device="mps")
for _ in range(5): y = x @ x          # warm-up
torch.mps.synchronize()

t0 = time.perf_counter(); y = x @ x
t1 = time.perf_counter()              # no barrier
print(f"host timer:       \{1e3*(t1-t0):.3f\} ms")
torch.mps.synchronize()

t0 = time.perf_counter(); y = x @ x
torch.mps.synchronize()               # barrier
t1 = time.perf_counter()
print(f"with synchronize: \{1e3*(t1-t0):.3f\} ms")
\end{semiverbatim}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.39\textwidth}
      \begin{uncoverenv}<2->
      {\footnotesize\textbf{Printed}}
\vspace{-0.25cm}
\begin{semiverbatim}\scriptsize
host timer:       0.175 ms
with synchronize: 76.525 ms
\end{semiverbatim}
      \vspace{-0.1cm}
      \small The multiply ran \textbf{437$\times$} longer than the host timer reported.
      \end{uncoverenv}\\[0.2cm]
      \begin{mlsyscard}{RedLine}\footnotesize\textbf{Barrier rule:} synchronize before the clock stops, or time with device events.\\[0.05cm]
      \textbf{Warm-up rule:} discard cold iterations.\end{mlsyscard}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Run here: python3 images/timer\_demo.py (torch 2.13.0, MPS backend on an Apple M5 Max GPU). Rules: Benchmarking, Systems Perspective 12.4, rules 1 and 5; \S12.2.4.}
  \note{[2 min] Real code and its real output. The script times one 8,192 by 8,192 matrix multiply on a laptop GPU through PyTorch's Metal backend (MPS); on an NVIDIA GPU the calls are torch.cuda.synchronize() and torch.cuda.Event, and the behavior is the same. Step 1: read the code. The warm-up loop runs the multiply five times so allocation and kernel selection are paid before timing. The first timed block stops the clock right after the multiply is called. ENGAGE: predict the first print against the second. Step 2: 0.175 ms against 76.525 ms, 437 times apart. A GPU launch returns to Python as soon as the work is queued, so the first timer measured the enqueue and missed the kernel. The barrier waits for the GPU to finish before the clock stops. The two cards are the chapter's micro-benchmarking rules 5 and 1. WARN: a result that looks impossibly fast usually timed the launch. Students who time PyTorch with time.time() and no synchronize make exactly this error. FLEX: [CORE]; if time allows, rerun the script live (it takes a few seconds).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4cm}
    \item MLPerf lets vendors choose which systems and workloads to submit. Is a voluntary benchmark evidence, or advertising with rules?
    \item Sixty-four H100s trained ResNet-50 5.3 times faster than eight, at 66 percent efficiency. When is the faster, less efficient system the right purchase?
    \item A vendor leads MLPerf Offline but did not submit Server. What would you ask before buying it for a public API?
  \end{itemize}
  \note{[2 min] No single answer. Take one response to each if time allows. Useful prompts: for the first, MLPerf results are audited and reproducible, but absence from a category is also information. For the second, compare the cost of the extra accelerators with the value of a model that is ready eight hours sooner; the answer differs for a research team and a production retraining job. For the third, students should ask for the Server result or a latency distribution under random arrivals, since Offline batching hides the tail (the RTX 4090 and H100 slides). FLEX: [OPTIONAL].}
\end{frame}
