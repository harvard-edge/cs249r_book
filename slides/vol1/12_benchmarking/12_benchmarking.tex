\begin{frame}{One server's p99 is 10 ms; the whole request's is 140 ms}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.9cm,keepaspectratio]{tail_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Measured: Dean and Barroso, ``The Tail at Scale,'' CACM 2013, Table 1 (a Google service). Lower panel: Benchmarking, \S12.10 (illustrative).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3.5 min}{Core}%
    Averages hide slow requests, so benchmarks report percentiles, with median (p50) met or beaten by half of requests and p99 by 99 percent. In ``The Tail at Scale'' (Dean and Barroso, 2013, Table 1), a Google root server fans each request out to many leaf servers and waits for every reply. One leaf answers in 1 ms median and 10 ms p99, whereas waiting for all leaves pushes the root median to 40 ms and root p99 to 140 ms, 14x higher. Below, the chapter's illustrative strip contrasts a 15 ms model-only mean against 150 to 200 ms production p99, hiding misses past a 33 ms camera deadline. A service-level objective (SLO, Lecture 4b) sets a target on a metric such as p99, while a service-level agreement (SLA) adds contractual penalties. Generative services split tails into time to first token (TTFT) and time per output token (TPOT), modeled with queues in Lecture 13.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Do not dismiss p99 as rare. Across one million requests, p99 affects 10,000 users.}

    \notecue{Next}{Derive by hand why fanning across 100 backend servers makes slow tails common.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A closed-loop client erases the stall it should measure}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.15cm,keepaspectratio]{loop_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Benchmarking, \S12.2.4 (interactive Explore figure: Coordinated Omission).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Stating every operating condition still fails when the test client pauses alongside a stalled server, a trap called coordinated omission. A closed-loop client follows a naive ``send, wait, send'' loop, issuing the next request only after receiving a reply. When the server freezes for 500 ms, such as during a garbage-collection pause (when a runtime halts to reclaim unused memory), the client stops sending and logs only one slow request. An open-loop client instead sends on an independent schedule like real users, as in the Poisson arrivals (random independent arrivals at a constant average rate) that Section 3 revisits in MLPerf's Server scenario. Requests then queue behind the stall and record 14 slow responses, showing that the closed-loop harness hid 13 of 14 tail events. Lecture 13 models the resulting queues.

    \notecue{Next}{Ask what goes wrong when a host clock times an accelerator running asynchronously behind a command queue.}
  }
\end{frame}

    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Optional}%
    Even without thermal throttling, clock and voltage move energy per inference on identical silicon. Recalling MLPerf Tiny v1.2 and the dedicated Syntiant NDP9120 neural chip from Lecture 2, these closed entries run the visual wake words vision benchmark (a compact MobileNetV1 0.25x reference) at 84.8 to 85.2 percent accuracy.     \notecue{Check}{Average power is 52.9 mW, 94 percent idle. Duty cycle (active fraction) is $d = 8.3\text{ ms}/1\text{ s} = 0.0083$, so $P_{\text{avg}} = d P_{\text{active}} + (1 - d) P_{\text{idle}} = 0.0083\times 400 + 0.9917\times 50 = 3.3 + 49.6 = 52.9$ mW. A tempting error is cutting per-inference millijoules without checking duty cycle, targeting 6 percent of drain; halving inference energy saves 1.67 mW (about 3 percent). Active energy matches idle only above $d \approx 50/450 = 11$ percent, whereas continuous video ($d \approx 1$) flips the balance.}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4cm}
    \item MLPerf lets vendors choose which systems and workloads to submit. Is a voluntary benchmark evidence, or advertising with rules?
    \item Sixty-four H100s trained ResNet-50 5.3 times faster than eight, at 66 percent efficiency. When is the faster, less efficient system the right purchase?
    \item If a vendor leads MLPerf Offline without submitting Server, what would you ask before buying it for a public API?
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    Applying those rules to real procurement raises three open design questions. First, voluntary MLPerf submissions are audited yet selective, so what a vendor chooses not to submit is information too. Second, 64 H100s at 66 percent efficiency pay off on that ResNet-50 run only when saving 10.8 minutes of wall-clock time beats the added GPU cost (or, on longer jobs, when faster turnaround justifies paying more total GPU-hours). Third, an Offline-only vendor should supply Server tail distributions under random arrivals before you buy its hardware for a public API.
