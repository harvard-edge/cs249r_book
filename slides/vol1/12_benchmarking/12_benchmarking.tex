\begin{frame}{One server's p99 is 10 ms; the whole request's is 140 ms}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.9cm,keepaspectratio]{tail_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Measured: Dean and Barroso, ``The Tail at Scale,'' CACM 2013, Table 1 (a Google service). Lower panel: Benchmarking, \S12.10 (illustrative).}
  \note{[4 min] Define the terms first. The median (p50) is the time half of requests beat; p99 is the time 99 percent of requests meet or beat. Step 1: one leaf server in a large Google service: median 1 ms, p99 10 ms. Step 2: the root sends each request to many leaves and waits for all of them. The median is now 40 ms and the p99 is 140 ms, 14 times the single-leaf p99. Each leaf is slow only 1 percent of the time, but a request that touches 100 of them meets at least one slow leaf about 63 percent of the time. Step 3: the chapter's illustrative case: a model-only test reports a 15 ms mean on evenly spaced inputs; under production traffic the p99 is 150 to 200 ms. The camera trap's 33 ms frame deadline sits between them, so the mean says nothing about how many frames miss it. A service-level objective (SLO) is a target on a named indicator such as p99; an agreement with penalties is an SLA. WARN: students assume p99 is a rare-case curiosity. At a million requests, it is 10,000 users. LINK: Lecture 13 explains how queues build this tail.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A closed-loop client erases the stall it should measure}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.4cm,keepaspectratio]{loop_step}{3}
  \note{[2.5 min] The client that sends requests is part of the measurement. Step 1: a closed-loop client sends one request, waits for the reply, then sends the next. The server stalls for 500 ms, for example in garbage collection. The client waits too, sends nothing, and records one slow request. Step 2: an open-loop client sends on its own schedule, the way independent users do; MLPerf's Server scenario uses random (Poisson) arrivals. Every request that arrives during the stall waits behind it, and the log shows many slow requests. The closed-loop client hid the tail it was built to find; this error is called coordinated omission. Step 3: the same mistake inside one machine. A GPU launch returns as soon as the work is queued, so a timer on the CPU measures microseconds of queueing, not the kernel (the program the GPU runs). A synchronize barrier or device events time the kernel itself. WARN: students time PyTorch code with time.time() and no synchronize; ask how many have. FLEX: [OPTIONAL] step 3 if short. LINK: Lecture 13 measures queues under load.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Vote: is a one-point accuracy drop a regression?}
  \mlsysactivity{Vote}{3 min}
  {\normalsize Baseline 95\%, compressed 94\%, on the \textbf{same} 1,000 test images.}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small\choice{A}{Yes, one point is one point}\quad \choice{B}{No, it is inside the noise}\quad \choicehl{2}{C}{It depends on data not given}}\\[0.15cm]
  \centering
  \only<1|handout:0>{\vspace{4.4cm}}%
  \only<2|handout:0>{\safeimg[width=0.9\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{conf_step1.pdf}}%
  \only<3-|handout:1>{\safeimg[width=0.9\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{conf_step2.pdf}}
  \mlsyscredit{Benchmarking, Napkin Math 12.5 (McNemar's test, 95\% level, no continuity correction).}
  \note{[3.5 min] ENGAGE: vote, one minute with a neighbor, vote again. Answer C. Step 2: 95 and 94 percent are 50 and 60 errors. If the two scores came from independent test sets, the difference would have a 95 percent interval of about minus 1 to plus 3 points, which includes zero. Step 3: but both models saw the same images, so compare them image by image. Images both get right or both get wrong say nothing about which is better. The score gap fixes the difference between the two kinds of disagreement at 10; McNemar's test calls the drop a regression only if the models disagree on at most 26 images. If they disagree on 50, the same one-point gap is unresolved. Students who chose B have the right instinct with the wrong reason. WARN: ``not significant'' does not mean ``equivalent''; it means the test could not tell. Keep per-image outcomes, not only the two accuracies. Sizing: estimating one accuracy to plus or minus 1 point needs about 1,825 images.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A benchmark that becomes the goal stops measuring}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.2cm,keepaspectratio]{goodhart_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  \only<3->{\mlsysactivity{Discuss}{2 min}}\uncover<3->{\normalsize Your bonus depends on a leaderboard rank. Name one change that raises the rank and hurts users, and one rule that stops it.}
  \mlsyscredit{Left: Benchmarking, Example 12.1 (illustrative). Right: Recht et al., ``Do ImageNet Classifiers Generalize to ImageNet?'' ICML 2019.}
  \note{[3.5 min] Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Step 1: the chapter's illustrative case. A translation team widens beam search from 1 to 10 candidates to gain 0.5 BLEU (a translation-quality score); latency rises from 50 to 200 ms against a 100 ms objective. The score improved and the product broke. Step 2: measured. Recht et al. rebuilt the CIFAR-10 and ImageNet test sets by the original process. Accuracy fell 3 to 15 points on CIFAR-10 and 11 to 14 on ImageNet; the authors attribute the drop to harder images rather than to tuning on the old test set, and rankings were largely kept. A fixed test set is an instrument with limits. Step 3: ENGAGE: two minutes with a neighbor. Expected: a larger batch or beam that breaks the latency objective; kernels tuned to the benchmark's fixed shapes; quantizing until calibration fails. Rules: closed-division run rules, a latency bound inside the score, refreshed held-out tests, audits. MLPerf forbids undisclosed precision cuts, skipping work on known test inputs, and detecting the benchmark to run special code. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}
