\begin{frame}{One server's p99 is 10 ms; the whole request's is 140 ms}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.9cm,keepaspectratio]{tail_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Measured: Dean and Barroso, ``The Tail at Scale,'' CACM 2013, Table 1 (a Google service). Lower panel: Benchmarking, \S12.10 (illustrative).}
  \note{[4 min] Define the terms first. The median (p50) is the time half of requests beat; p99 is the time 99 percent of requests meet or beat. Step 1: one leaf server in a large Google service: median 1 ms, p99 10 ms. Step 2: the root sends each request to many leaves and waits for all of them. The median is now 40 ms and the p99 is 140 ms, 14 times the single-leaf p99. Each leaf is slow only 1 percent of the time, but a request that touches 100 of them meets at least one slow leaf about 63 percent of the time. Step 3: the chapter's illustrative case: a model-only test reports a 15 ms mean on evenly spaced inputs; under production traffic the p99 is 150 to 200 ms. The camera trap's 33 ms frame deadline sits between them, so the mean says nothing about how many frames miss it. A service-level objective (SLO) is a target on a named indicator such as p99; an agreement with penalties is an SLA. WARN: students assume p99 is a rare-case curiosity. At a million requests, it is 10,000 users. The next slide shows why fan-out multiplies the tail. LINK: Lecture 13 explains how queues build this tail. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]{Estimate: how many requests hit a slow shard?}
  \mlsysactivity{Estimate}{2 min}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
      \begin{mlsyscard}{mlsysaccent}
        \small Each of 100 shards is slow 1\% of the time, independently. A request waits for all 100.\\[0.1cm]
        What fraction of requests waits on a slow one?
      \end{mlsyscard}\vspace{0.15cm}
      \uncover<2->{\small $P = 1 - 0.99^{100}$\\[0.05cm] $\phantom{P} = 1 - 0.366 = 63\%$}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=4.95cm,keepaspectratio]{fanout_step}{2}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Benchmarking, footnote 20 (Dean and Barroso, ``The Tail at Scale,'' CACM 2013); curve $1 - 0.99^n$ computed.}
  \note{[3 min] A worked estimate that explains the previous slide's 140 ms. A shard is one leaf server holding part of the data, so one request touches many shards. ENGAGE: one minute with a neighbor. Each shard is fast 99 percent of the time. Step 2: work it slowly on the board. The chance one shard is fast is 0.99. The chance all 100 are fast is 0.99 multiplied by itself 100 times, about 0.366. The chance at least one is slow is the rest: 1 minus 0.366, about 63 percent. Point to the curve: at 10 shards it is already 9.6 percent, the chapter's self-check answer. Students who answered 1 percent assumed the request is as slow as a typical shard; it is as slow as its slowest one. So a leaf's p99 becomes something close to the whole request's median, which is what the measured table showed: 40 ms median against one leaf's 1 ms. WARN: the formula assumes shards are slow independently; correlated slowdowns, such as a shared network switch, behave differently. LINK: Lecture 13 uses the complement, 36.6 percent of requests meeting the target, to motivate hedged requests. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A closed-loop client erases the stall it should measure}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{loop_step}{2}
  \note{[2 min] The client that sends requests is part of the measurement. Step 1: a closed-loop client sends one request, waits for the reply, then sends the next. The server stalls for 500 ms, for example in garbage collection. The client waits too, sends nothing, and records one slow request. Step 2: an open-loop client sends on its own schedule, the way independent users do; MLPerf's Server scenario uses random (Poisson) arrivals. Every request that arrives during the stall waits behind it, and the log shows 14 slow requests. The closed-loop client hid the tail it was built to find; this error is called coordinated omission. ENGAGE: which client does a load-testing script that loops ``send, wait, send'' implement? Expected: closed loop. LINK: Lecture 13 measures queues under load. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A host timer measures the launch, not the kernel}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.9\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{timer_step}{2}\\[0.2cm]
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \begin{mlsyscard}{RedLine}\footnotesize\textbf{Barrier rule:} synchronize before and after the timed region, or use device events.\end{mlsyscard}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \begin{mlsyscard}{BlueLine}\footnotesize\textbf{Warm-up rule:} discard cold iterations; caches, kernels, and clocks need time to settle.\end{mlsyscard}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Benchmarking, Systems Perspective 12.4, rules 1 and 5; \S12.2.4.}
  \note{[2 min] The same mistake inside one machine. Step 1: a GPU kernel launch returns to the host as soon as the work is queued. A timer on the CPU stops there and measures the enqueue, not the kernel, the program the GPU runs. Step 2: a synchronize barrier, such as torch.cuda.synchronize(), or GPU-side events stop the clock when the kernel finishes. The two cards are the chapter's micro-benchmarking rules 5 and 1. The first runs of a model also pay for memory allocation, just-in-time compilation, and weight transfers, so the harness discards warm-up iterations before timing steady state. ENGAGE: how many of you have timed PyTorch code with time.time() and no synchronize? WARN: a result that looks impossibly fast usually timed the launch. FLEX: [OPTIONAL]: a two-minute live demo timing one model with and without synchronize and warm-up.}
\end{frame}
