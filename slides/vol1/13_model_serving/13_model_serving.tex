\begin{frame}{Overlapping host and GPU work raises GPU use to 78 percent}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.8cm,keepaspectratio]{pipeline_step}{2}

\begin{frame}{Requests in the server equal arrival rate times time inside}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering
      {\Huge $N = \lambda \times W$}\\[0.35cm]
      \uncover<2->{\begin{tabular}{@{}rl@{}}
        $N$ & requests inside, on average \\[0.1cm]
        $\lambda$ & arrivals per second \\[0.1cm]
        $W$ & seconds each one stays \\
      \end{tabular}}\\[0.45cm]
      \uncover<4->{\normalsize $1{,}000/\text{s} \times 0.05\ \text{s} = \mathbf{50}$ requests inside}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \centering
      \uncover<3->{%
      \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\footnotesize]
        \foreach \i in {0,...,5} {\draw[fill=BlueL, draw=BlueLine] (\i*0.42,0) rectangle ++(0.34,0.6);}
        \node[below=0.05cm, text=BlueLine] at (1.15,0) {waiting in host memory};
        \draw[-{Latex}, thick, midgray] (2.6,0.3) -- (3.1,0.3);
        \draw[fill=OrangeL, draw=OrangeLine, line width=1.2pt, rounded corners=2pt] (3.2,-0.4) rectangle (5.4,1.0);
        \node[align=center, text=OrangeLine] at (4.3,0.3) {\textbf{GPU}\\one batch\\of up to 32};
        \draw[-{Latex}, thick, midgray] (5.5,0.3) -- (6.0,0.3);
      \end{tikzpicture}}\\[0.4cm]
      \uncover<4->{\small 32 can run at once; the rest wait.\\ A memory requirement, not a GPU limit.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Little 1961; Model Serving, \S13.4.1 and Napkin Math 13.2.}
  \note{[3 min] Step 1: Little's law. It holds for any stable system, whatever the arrival pattern or scheduling order. Step 2: the terms. N is the average number of requests inside the server, queued or running; lambda (the Greek letter) is arrivals per second; W is the average time each spends inside. Step 3: the picture. Some requests wait in host memory, up to 32 run on the GPU as one batch. Step 4: a service at 1,000 requests per second (QPS, queries per second) with 50 ms mean time inside holds 50 requests on average. The GPU runs at most 32 at once and that is fine: the other 18 wait in host memory, so the server needs buffers and worker threads for 50. ENGAGE: a service holds 50 requests at 10 ms each; what rate does it sustain? Expected: 5,000 per second. WARN: Little's law gives the average; it says nothing about whether the slowest request meets its deadline. That is the next slide. FLEX: [OPTIONAL]; fold the 50-request example into the capacity estimate if short.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Waiting time explodes as utilization nears 100 percent}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.72\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=6.0cm,keepaspectratio]{knee_step}{3}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.26\textwidth}
      \uncover<3->{\normalsize Mean\\[0.1cm] $W = \dfrac{S}{1-\rho}$\\[0.4cm] p99\\[0.1cm] $W_{99} \approx \dfrac{4.6\,S}{1-\rho}$\\[0.4cm]
      {\footnotesize $S$: service time\\ $\rho$: utilization}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{[4 min] Utilization, rho, is the fraction of time the GPU is busy: arrival rate divided by the rate it can serve. S is the service time, 3.5 ms. M/M/1 means random arrivals, variable service times, one server. Step 1: the mean time in system. At 50 percent busy, 7.1 ms, twice the service time; at 80 percent, 17.6 ms; at 90 percent, 35.3 ms; at 95 percent, 70.6 ms. Each new arrival is more likely to find work already waiting. Step 2: the 99th percentile, p99, the time that 99 of every 100 requests beat. It sits 4.6 times above the mean at every load, because in this model time in system is exponentially distributed (most requests are quick, a few are very slow) and the natural log of 100 is 4.6. Step 3: the two laws. At 70 percent busy, the mean is 11.8 ms but p99 is 54.1 ms. ENGAGE: a mean of 12 ms against a 50 ms deadline: safe? Expected: no, p99 is above 50. WARN: the shaded zone above 70 percent is a planning policy, not a law. Compiled models have steadier service times than M/M/1 assumes, so treat it as a first estimate for a load test to check. LINK: Lecture 12's tail at scale starts from this per-server tail. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{At batch 1, 99 percent of each pass is fixed cost}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.90\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{fixed_cost_step}{2}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Energy per image (modeled): 39.2 mJ at batch 1, 24.9 at 8, 23.4 at 32.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Serving, Napkin Math 13.4, tables 8 and 9 (modeled reference profile).}
  \note{[2.5 min] Step 1: one image at batch 1 takes 3.53 ms, and almost all of it does not depend on the image: 174 kernel launches at about 15 microseconds of host time each (2.6 ms), a minimum device time per kernel, and one read of the 102 MB of weights. That is why throughput rises from 280 to 7,040 images per second, 25 times, while the batch takes only 4.55 ms instead of 3.53. \begin{frame}{Waiting for a batch to form costs half the window}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{window_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  \only<1-2|handout:0>{\small Mean wait = window / 2. A 20 ms window spends 10 ms of a 50 ms budget.}%
  \only<3-|handout:1>{\small Dispatch at the cap or at the timeout. At 500 QPS: mean 8.7 ms; p99 bound 13.9 ms.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Serving, \S13.5.4, equations 8, 9, and 12; window arrivals illustrative, counts Poisson.}
  \note{[3 min] One time axis, one change per step. Step 1: a 10 ms window. Requests land at random; the batch leaves at the end of the window. The first arrival waits almost 10 ms, the last almost none, so on average a request waits half the window. A 20 ms window adds 10 ms on average, 20 percent of a 50 ms budget. Step 2: how many requests a 10 ms window collects. At 50 QPS the mean is 0.5 and 61 percent of windows are empty; at 200 QPS the count swings widely around 2; at 1,000 QPS it clusters near 10. Low traffic gives small, ragged batches. Step 3: real servers use a dual trigger: dispatch when the batch reaches its cap or when the oldest request has waited the timeout, whichever comes first (the third window). At 500 QPS with a 10 ms window, the batch a request sees averages 6 and reaches 12 at the 99th percentile; the mean time is 5 + 3.7 = 8.7 ms, and a conservative bound is the full window plus the p99 batch, 10 + 3.9 = 13.9 ms. WARN: the tail of the batch distribution, not its mean, must fit the deadline. FLEX: [CORE]. Assign Example 13.4, which traces one batch through a busy worker.}
\end{frame}

  \note{[2 min] Vote, discuss, vote again. Step 2: B. At 500 requests per second, a batch that waits for 16 requests waits on average (16 minus 1) / (2 times 500) seconds, 15 ms; at 32, 31 ms. The capacity gain is real, 3,980 to 7,040 images per second, but the service already has 3,980 against 500 offered, so the extra capacity buys nothing while every request waits twice as long.   \note{[2.5 min] A cold start is everything a new replica does before its first useful answer. Step 1: read the weights (0.5 s from a local SSD, 3 to 5 s from cloud storage such as S3), create the CUDA context (the driver's per-process state on the GPU, 0.3 to 0.5 s), then warm up: run a few dummy inferences so lazy initialization happens before a user arrives. Step 2: the first deployment also builds a TensorRT engine, compiling the model for this exact GPU, 15 to 30 s. WARN: the fallacy is that cold start matters only for the first request. It strikes during spikes, deployments, and recovery, exactly when capacity is short. \mlsysdivider{What changes when the answer is a stream of tokens?}{Model Serving, \S13.9}
\section{Tokens}

\begin{frame}{A chat answer has two deadlines: first token, then each next}
  \centering
  \only<1|handout:0>{\safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.3cm,keepaspectratio]{ttft_step1.pdf}}%
  \only<2-|handout:1>{\safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.3cm,keepaspectratio]{ttft_step2.pdf}}\\[0.2cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small GPT-2 XL on an H100: decode reads 3 GB per token, 0.9 ms at 3.35 TB/s.}\\[0.1cm]
  \uncover<3->{\small MLPerf Llama 2 70B targets: TTFT/TPOT 2,000/200 ms, or 450/40 ms interactive.}
  \mlsyscredit{Patel et al., ``Splitwise,'' ISCA 2024, Table IV (Llama 2 70B, one DGX-H100, no batching, median). Model Serving, \S13.9.2. MLPerf Inference v5.0 rules.}
  \note{[2.5 min] Step 1: one measured chat request, Llama 2 70B on one eight-GPU H100 server, no batching, median of a conversation trace. Prefill reads the whole prompt in parallel and produces the first token after 84 ms: time to first token, TTFT. Decode then produces one token every 28 ms: time per output token, TPOT. The answer ends at 3,387 ms. Step 2: decode is about 97 percent of the time (our arithmetic from the two medians; the paper says most of the time). LINK: Lectures 0 and 11 placed decode at about 1 FLOP per byte on the roofline, far below the H100's ridge; here that becomes milliseconds. Each decode step reads every weight once, so for GPT-2 XL (3 GB in FP16) the step can be no faster than 3 GB divided by 3.35 TB/s, 0.9 ms. Prefill for a 512-token prompt is about 1.5 TFLOP, about 3.9 ms. Issued kernel by kernel from Python, a decode step would spend 8.6 ms just launching 576 kernels, which is why servers capture the step as one CUDA graph (Lecture 7). Step 3: MLPerf sets both targets: 2,000 ms to first token and 200 ms per token for conversation, 450 and 40 ms for interactive use. WARN: a fast first token does not mean a fast answer. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Batching decode shares the weights but not the KV cache}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.7cm,keepaspectratio]{decode_bytes_step}{2}\\[0.2cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Measured (Splitwise): batching decode to 64 only doubles the time between tokens.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Serving, \S13.9.2 (GPT-2 XL, 307.2 KB of cache per token). Patel et al., ``Splitwise,'' ISCA 2024, \S III-C (DGX-H100).}
  \note{[2.5 min] The KV cache holds the attention keys and values of every earlier token, so each new token does not recompute them (Lecture 6). GPT-2 XL stores 307.2 KB per token, 0.31 GB for a full 1,024-token context. Step 1: batch 1. Each step reads the 3 GB of weights plus one sequence's cache: 3.3 GB in 1.0 ms, about 1,010 tokens per second. Step 2: batch 32. The weights are still read once, but every sequence reads its own cache: 13.1 GB, 77 percent of it cache, in 3.9 ms. Aggregate throughput rises to 8,200 tokens per second, while each user's time per token stretches 3.9 times. The measured line: Splitwise forced batches of decode on H100 servers and found that a batch of 64 only doubled the time between tokens. ENGAGE: which grows faster with batch size, weight bytes or cache bytes? Expected: cache bytes; weight bytes do not grow at all. FLEX: [CORE]. Speculative decoding is a second lever: a small draft model proposes four tokens and the large model checks them in one pass; at 70 percent acceptance GPT-2 XL goes from 0.90 to 0.43 ms per token (\S13.9.4).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Continuous batching refills a slot the step it frees}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.8cm,keepaspectratio]{continuous_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Serving, \S13.9.4; Yu et al., ``Orca,'' OSDI 2022.}
  \note{[2 min] Answers have unknown lengths. Step 1: request-level batching keeps the batch fixed until its longest member finishes. A sequence that ends after 10 tokens leaves its slot idle for the other 90 steps of a 100-step batch: 90 percent of that slot wasted. Step 2: iteration-level, or continuous, batching (Orca; vLLM and TensorRT-LLM use it) reconsiders the batch at every decode step. When a sequence emits its end token, a waiting request takes the slot at the next step. ENGAGE: what new problem appears when a long prompt joins a batch of active decodes? Expected: its prefill takes tens of milliseconds and stalls everyone's next token; chunked prefill (Sarathi-Serve) splits long prompts into pieces of about 512 tokens. FLEX: [OPTIONAL].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Paging the KV cache lets vLLM batch 2.2 times more requests}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{vllm_step}{2}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35cm}
    \item A provider could offer a slower, cheaper tier that waits to batch. Who should decide how much latency a request is worth?
    \item Reasoning models choose to spend more tokens per question. Each token costs decode time, cache memory, and joules. Who should cap that spending?
    \item Energy per prompt fell 33$\times$ in a year, and API prices fell by orders of magnitude. Will total serving energy fall or rise?
  \end{itemize}
