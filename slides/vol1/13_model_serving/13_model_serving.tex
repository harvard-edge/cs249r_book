\begin{frame}{Overlapping host and GPU work raises GPU use to 78 percent}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.8cm,keepaspectratio]{pipeline_step}{2}
  \note{[1.5 min] Step 1: serial service. Each request runs its full 8.6 ms before the next begins, so the GPU is busy 3.5 ms of every 8.6 ms, 41 percent. Step 2: pipelining. \begin{frame}{Requests in the server equal arrival rate times time inside}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering
      {\Huge $N = \lambda \times W$}\\[0.35cm]
      \uncover<2->{\begin{tabular}{@{}rl@{}}

        $\lambda$ & arrivals per second \\[0.1cm]
        $W$ & seconds each one stays \\
      \end{tabular}}\\[0.45cm]
      \uncover<4->{\normalsize $1{,}000/\text{s} \times 0.05\ \text{s} = \mathbf{50}$ requests inside}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \centering
      \uncover<3->{%
      \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\footnotesize]
        \foreach \i in {0,...,5} {\draw[fill=BlueL, draw=BlueLine] (\i*0.42,0) rectangle ++(0.34,0.6);}
        \node[below=0.05cm, text=BlueLine] at (1.15,0) {waiting in host memory};
        \draw[-{Latex}, thick, midgray] (2.6,0.3) -- (3.1,0.3);
        \draw[fill=OrangeL, draw=OrangeLine, line width=1.2pt, rounded corners=2pt] (3.2,-0.4) rectangle (5.4,1.0);
        \node[align=center, text=OrangeLine] at (4.3,0.3) {\textbf{GPU}\\one batch\\of up to 32};
        \draw[-{Latex}, thick, midgray] (5.5,0.3) -- (6.0,0.3);
      \end{tikzpicture}}\\[0.4cm]
      \uncover<4->{\small 32 can run at once; the rest wait.\\ A memory requirement, not a GPU limit.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Little 1961; Model Serving, \S13.4.1 and Napkin Math 13.2.}
  \note{[3 min] Step 1: Little's law. It holds for any stable system, whatever the arrival pattern or scheduling order. Step 2: the terms. N is the average number of requests inside the server, queued or running; lambda (the Greek letter) is arrivals per second; W is the average time each spends inside. Step 3: the picture. Some requests wait in host memory, up to 32 run on the GPU as one batch. Step 4: a service at 1,000 requests per second (QPS, queries per second) with 50 ms mean time inside holds 50 requests on average. The GPU runs at most 32 at once and that is fine: the other 18 wait in host memory, so the server needs buffers and worker threads for 50. ENGAGE: a service holds 50 requests at 10 ms each; what rate does it sustain? Expected: 5,000 per second. WARN: Little's law gives the average; it says nothing about whether the slowest request meets its deadline. That is the next slide. FLEX: [OPTIONAL]; fold the 50-request example into the capacity estimate if short.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Waiting time explodes as utilization nears 100 percent}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.72\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=6.0cm,keepaspectratio]{knee_step}{5}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.26\textwidth}
      \uncover<5->{\normalsize Mean\\[0.1cm] $W = \dfrac{S}{1-\rho}$\\[0.4cm] p99\\[0.1cm] $W_{99} \approx \dfrac{4.6\,S}{1-\rho}$\\[0.4cm]
      {\footnotesize $S$: service time\\ $\rho$: utilization}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{[4.5 min] [CORE] Utilization, rho, is the fraction of time the GPU is busy: arrival rate divided by the rate it can serve. S is the service time, 3.5 ms. M/M/1 means random arrivals, variable service times, one server. Step 1: at 50 percent busy, the mean time in system is 7.1 ms, twice the service time. Step 2: at 80 percent, 17.6 ms. Step 3: pause. ENGAGE: write down the mean at 90 percent before the reveal. Students who extrapolate linearly write about 25 ms. Step 4: 35.3 ms at 90 percent and 70.6 ms at 95 percent: each new arrival is more likely to find work already waiting. Step 5: the 99th percentile, p99, the time that 99 of every 100 requests beat. It sits 4.6 times above the mean at every load, because in this model time in system is exponentially distributed and the natural log of 100 is 4.6. The two laws: at 70 percent busy, the mean is 11.8 ms but p99 is 54.1 ms. ENGAGE: a mean of 12 ms against a 50 ms deadline: safe? Expected: no, p99 is above 50. WARN: the shaded zone above 70 percent is a planning policy, not a law. Compiled models have steadier service times than M/M/1 assumes; a load test must check the estimate.}
\end{frame}

\section{Batching}

\begin{frame}{At batch 1, 99 percent of each pass is fixed cost}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.90\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{fixed_cost_step}{2}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Energy per image (modeled): 39.2 mJ at batch 1, 24.9 at 8, 23.4 at 32.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Serving, Napkin Math 13.4, tables 8 and 9 (modeled reference profile).}
  \note{[2.5 min] Step 1: one image at batch 1 takes 3.53 ms, and almost all of it does not depend on the image: 174 kernel launches at about 15 microseconds of host time each (2.6 ms), a minimum device time per kernel, and one read of the 102 MB of weights. Only 0.033 ms is work on this image. Step 2: batch 8 shares the same fixed cost among eight images, 0.47 ms each; batch 32, 0.14 ms each. That is why throughput rises from 280 to 7,040 images per second, 25 times, while the batch takes only 4.55 ms instead of 3.53. The energy line says the same in joules: data movement is 52 percent of an image's energy at batch 1 and 19 percent at batch 32. LINK: Lecture 11 put batch-1 inference low on the roofline; this is the same fact as a cost per image. Lecture 7 owns the launch overhead. WARN: the fixed-cost share is not utilization. A GPU serving batch 1 back to back is 100 percent busy in queueing terms while 99 percent of its time is fixed cost. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Removing dispatch, not precision, wins at batch 1}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{runtime_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Serving, Example 13.6, table 14 (the reference profile, modeled, one term changed per row).}

\begin{frame}{Waiting for a batch to form costs half the window}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{window_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Mean wait = window / 2. A 20 ms window spends 10 ms of a 50 ms budget.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Serving, \S13.5.4, equations 8, 9, and 12; window arrivals illustrative, counts Poisson.}
  \note{[3 min] One time axis, one change per step. Step 1: a 10 ms window. Requests land at random; the batch leaves at the end of the window. The first arrival waits almost 10 ms, the last almost none, so on average a request waits half the window. A 20 ms window adds 10 ms on average, 20 percent of a 50 ms budget. Step 2: how many requests a 10 ms window collects. At 50 QPS the mean is 0.5 and 61 percent of windows are empty; at 200 QPS the count swings widely around 2; at 1,000 QPS it clusters near 10. Low traffic gives small, ragged batches. At 500 QPS with a 10 ms window, the batch a request sees averages 6 and reaches 12 at the 99th percentile. WARN: the tail of the batch distribution, not its mean, must fit the deadline. ENGAGE: at 50 QPS, is a 10 ms window worth having? (Expected: rarely; most windows hold zero or one request.) FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

  {\small A batch leaves at its cap or its 12 ms timeout, then waits for a free worker.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Serving, Example 13.4 (hypothetical trace; batch time from the chapter's linear service model).}
  \note{[3 min] [CORE] Real servers use a dual trigger: a batch leaves when it reaches its size cap or when its oldest request has waited the timeout, whichever comes first. Step 1: four requests arrive at 0, 2, 5, and 9 ms, and no more come. The batch never reaches its cap, so the timer makes it eligible at 12 ms. ENGAGE: two minutes; the worker is busy until 18 ms, a batch of four takes 3.6 ms, and the response path takes 4 ms. Does the oldest request finish within 50 ms? The oldest request finishes at 25.6 ms with 24.4 ms of slack; the later arrivals have more. WARN: the timeout decides when a batch may leave; the worker decides when it does.   \note{[3 min] [CORE] A cold start is everything a new replica does before its first useful answer. Step 1: read the weights (0.5 s from a local SSD, 3 to 5 s from cloud storage such as S3), create the CUDA context (the driver's per-process state on the GPU, 0.3 to 0.5 s), then warm up: run a few dummy inferences so lazy initialization happens before a user arrives. Step 2: the first deployment also builds a TensorRT engine, compiling the model for this exact GPU, 15 to 30 s. Totals: about 1.5 s with a precompiled engine on a warm container, about 35 s from scratch. Step 3: measured loading of large language models with PyTorch: OPT-30B onto 4 GPUs in 34 s, Llama 2 70B onto 8 GPUs in 84 s; downloading a 130 GB checkpoint at 5 GB/s takes at least 26 s before loading starts. WARN: the fallacy is that cold start matters only for the first request. It strikes during spikes, deployments, and recovery, exactly when capacity is short. Step 4: the file format matters too. A 5 GB checkpoint takes 15 s through Python's pickle, which rebuilds every object on the CPU, and 1.5 s through memory-mapped safetensors, which maps the weights straight from the file (Example 13.10). FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
