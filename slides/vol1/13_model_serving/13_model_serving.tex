  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    That Server-versus-Offline split explains why live serving reverses every priority of training. Training (Lecture 8, analogous to MLPerf Offline) maximizes whole-run throughput with large batches and keeps utilization near 100 percent; if one step runs slow, the job finishes slightly later. The slide contrasts that large-batch regime using ResNet-50's modeled 7,040 img/s batch-32 forward-pass rate on an H100, noting that full training steps also run backward and parameter-update passes. \begin{frame}{Requests in the server equal arrival rate times time inside}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering
      {\Huge $N = \lambda \times W$}\\[0.35cm]
      \uncover<2->{\begin{tabular}{@{}rl@{}}

        $W$ & seconds each one stays \\
      \end{tabular}}\\[0.45cm]
      \uncover<4->{\normalsize $1{,}000/\text{s} \times 0.05\ \text{s} = \mathbf{50}$ requests inside}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}

      \end{tikzpicture}}\\[0.4cm]
      \uncover<4->{\small 32 can run at once; the rest wait.\\ The other 18 need buffers in host memory.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Little 1961; Model Serving, \S13.5.1 and Napkin Math 13.2.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Optional}%
    Before sizing GPUs for concurrent traffic, an engineer needs to know how many requests sit inside the server at once so host buffers do not overflow. Formulated by John D. C. Little in 1961, Little's law gives an exact balance for any stable queue, regardless of arrival pattern or scheduling order. In $N = \lambda \times W$, $N$ is the average number of requests inside (waiting or running), $\lambda$ is the arrival rate in QPS (Lecture 12), and $W$ is the average time inside. At 1,000 QPS and 0.05 s inside, 50 requests occupy the server beside a batch-32 GPU, with 32 running and 18 waiting in host memory buffers. Reading backward, 50 requests in flight at 10 ms each sustain 5,000 QPS.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Little's law gives averages only and says nothing about whether the 99th-percentile request meets its deadline.}

    \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip this slide and mention the 50-request host-buffer example briefly during the fleet estimate.}

\begin{frame}{Waiting time explodes as utilization nears 100 percent}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.72\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=6.0cm,keepaspectratio]{knee_step}{5}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.26\textwidth}
      \uncover<5->{\normalsize Mean\\[0.1cm] $W = \dfrac{S}{1-\rho}$\\[0.4cm] p99\\[0.1cm] $W_{99} \approx \dfrac{4.6\,S}{1-\rho}$\\[0.4cm]
      {\footnotesize $S$: service time\\ $\rho$: utilization}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{4 min}{Core}%
    Averages cannot show how waiting climbs near capacity. Model an M/M/1 queue (Poisson arrivals at rate $\lambda$, exponentially varying service times with mean $S = 3.5$ ms, unrounded 3.528 ms, and one server with utilization $\rho = \lambda S$). Mean time in system $W$ doubles to 7.1 ms at 50 percent busy and reaches 17.6 ms at 80 percent. Predict $W$ at 90 percent before reading on.

    \notecue{Check}{At 90 percent, $W$ hits 35.3 ms, then 70.6 ms at 95 percent, by $W = S/(1 - \rho)$. Linear extrapolation tempts a 25 ms guess, missing that steep curve. Because M/M/1 total time is exponential, p99 is $\ln 100 \approx 4.6$ times the mean ($W_{99} \approx 4.6\,S/(1 - \rho)$), so at 70 percent an 11.8 ms mean hides a 54.1 ms p99, breaking a 50 ms deadline.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{The 70 percent knee is a planning heuristic, not a law. Compiled models vary less than M/M/1 assumes, so validate with a realistic load test.}

    \notecue{Teach}{At 80 percent, pause for written predictions of $W$ at 90 percent before revealing.}

\begin{frame}{Waiting for a batch to form costs half the window}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{window_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Mean wait = window / 2. A 20 ms window spends 10 ms of a 50 ms budget.}
  \mlsyscredit{Model Serving, \S13.6.4, equations 8, 9, and 12; window arrivals illustrative, counts Poisson.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Those curves count GPU execution time alone, omitting the wait for a batch to form. Under Poisson arrivals in a 10 ms batch-formation window, the first arrival waits nearly 10 ms and the last waits zero, so mean wait equals window/2. A 20 ms window thus spends 10 ms waiting, or 20 percent of a 50 ms budget. Batch counts in a 10 ms window also fluctuate with rate. At 50 QPS, the mean count is 0.5, leaving 61 percent of windows empty, making batching marginal. At 200 QPS, counts swing widely around 2, whereas at 1,000 QPS they cluster near 10. At 500 QPS, a random window averages 5 arrivals, so an arriving request (itself plus 5 others on average) sees a mean batch of 6 and a p99 batch of 12. Pairing a timeout with a maximum batch size forms a dual-trigger policy that dispatches when either limit fires.

    Before serving, a new replica loads weights (0.5 s from SSD, 3 to 5 s from S3, Lecture 4b), builds a CUDA context (0.3 to 0.5 s), and spends 0.6 s on runtime setup and warm-up passes for lazy initialization (deferred setup), totaling about 1.5 s precompiled in a warm container (an already running software environment). First deploy adds 15 to 30 s to build a TensorRT engine, reaching about 35 s cold. ServerlessLLM (Fu et al., OSDI 2024), a study of serverless LLM loading, measured PyTorch loads of 34 s for OPT-30B on 4 GPUs and 84 s for Llama 2 70B on 8 GPUs, with a 130 GB download taking 26 s or more at 5 GB/s before loading starts. Format matters too (Example 13.10). A 5 GB checkpoint takes 15 s in Python pickle (CPU object parsing) versus 1.5 s in safetensors, memory-mapped into virtual address space, bypassing CPU parsing.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Cold starts strike during spikes, rollouts, and crash recovery, when capacity is scarce, not only on the first request.}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35cm}
    \item A provider could offer a slower, cheaper tier that waits to batch. Who should decide how much latency a request is worth?
    \item When a spike exceeds capacity, should a service reject what it cannot finish in time, serve a smaller model, or serve late? Who decides?
    \item Below 3,000 images/s, T4s beat one H100 per image. As traffic grows, when should a team switch, given a 35 s first deploy?
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
    Three open engineering trade-offs remain without a single technical answer. First, when a slower batched tier is cheaper, should the provider or the user price that latency? Second, during a spike, teams must choose among 503 rejection (which hurts availability), fallback models (which hurt quality), and late replies (which break the SLO), and must report whichever failure occurs. Third, switching a growing service from T4s to H100s requires planning around 35 s cold starts.
