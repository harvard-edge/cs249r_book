    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Walk through the comparison table column by column. First, training (Lectures 8 and 8b, analogous to MLPerf Offline) maximizes whole-run throughput with large batches (7,040 img/s at batch 32 on an H100) and pushes utilization near 100 percent; a slow step merely finishes the job slightly later. \begin{frame}{Waiting time explodes as utilization nears 100 percent}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.72\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=6.0cm,keepaspectratio]{knee_step}{5}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.26\textwidth}
      \uncover<5->{\normalsize Mean\\[0.1cm] $W = \dfrac{S}{1-\rho}$\\[0.4cm] p99\\[0.1cm] $W_{99} \approx \dfrac{4.6\,S}{1-\rho}$\\[0.4cm]
      {\footnotesize $S$: service time\\ $\rho$: utilization}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{4 min}{Core}%
    Build the M/M/1 queueing knee (random arrivals, variable service time $S = 3.5$ ms, one server, utilization $\rho = \lambda S$) across five steps. First, at 50 percent busy, mean time $W = S/(1 - \rho)$ doubles to 7.1 ms. Second, at 80 percent, $W$ reaches 17.6 ms. Third, pause and ask students to write down $W$ at 90 percent (linear extrapolators guess about 25 ms). Fourth, reveal the vertical climb: 35.3 ms at 90 percent and 70.6 ms at 95 percent. Fifth, overlay p99 ($W_{99} \approx 4.6\,S/(1 - \rho)$, because exponential response time scales by $\ln 100 \approx 4.6$): at 70 percent busy, a comforting 11.8 ms mean hides a 54.1 ms p99, breaking a 50 ms deadline.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The shaded 70 percent threshold is a planning heuristic, not a physical law; compiled models have tighter service-time variance than M/M/1 assumes, so always validate with a load test.}

  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35cm}
