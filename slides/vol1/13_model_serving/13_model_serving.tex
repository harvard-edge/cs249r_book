\begin{frame}{Requests in the server equal arrival rate times time inside}

      \end{tabular}}\\[0.45cm]

  \note{[2.5 min] Step 1: Little's law. It holds for any stable system, whatever the arrival pattern or scheduling order. Step 2: the terms. N is the average number of requests inside the server, queued or running; lambda (the Greek letter) is arrivals per second; W is the average time each spends inside. Step 3: the picture. Step 4: a service at 1,000 requests per second (QPS, queries per second) with 50 ms mean time inside holds 50 requests on average. ENGAGE: a service holds 50 requests at 10 ms each; what rate does it sustain? Expected: 5,000 per second. \begin{frame}{Waiting time explodes as utilization nears 100 percent}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.72\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=6.0cm,keepaspectratio]{knee_step}{5}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.26\textwidth}
      \uncover<5->{\normalsize Mean\\[0.1cm] $W = \dfrac{S}{1-\rho}$\\[0.4cm] p99\\[0.1cm] $W_{99} \approx \dfrac{4.6\,S}{1-\rho}$\\[0.4cm]

    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{[4 min] [CORE] Utilization, rho, is the fraction of time the GPU is busy: arrival rate divided by the rate it can serve. S is the service time, 3.5 ms. Step 5: the 99th percentile, p99, the time that 99 of every 100 requests beat. It sits 4.6 times above the mean at every load, because in this model time in system is exponentially distributed and the natural log of 100 is 4.6. The two laws: at 70 percent busy, the mean is 11.8 ms but p99 is 54.1 ms. ENGAGE: a mean of 12 ms against a 50 ms deadline: safe? Expected: no, p99 is above 50. WARN: the shaded zone above 70 percent is a planning policy; no law fixes it. Compiled models have steadier service times than M/M/1 assumes; a load test must check the estimate.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35cm}
    \item A provider could offer a slower, cheaper tier that waits to batch. Who should decide how much latency a request is worth?
    \item When a spike exceeds capacity, should a service reject what it cannot finish in time, serve a smaller model, or serve late? Who decides?
    \item Below 3,000 images/s, T4s beat one H100 per image. As traffic grows, when should a team switch, given a 35 s first deploy?
  \end{itemize}
  \note{[1.5 min] [OPTIONAL] No single answer. Pick one for discussion or assign one as a short written response. The second returns to the traffic-spike discussion: rejection lowers availability, fallback lowers quality, and lateness breaks the SLO, so each must be reported. The third combines the crossover with cold start: switching hardware means compiling and warming new replicas before the load arrives.}
\end{frame}
