  \mlsyscredit{Wikimedia Commons: RPi HQ camera, Laserlicht, CC BY-SA 4.0; RealSense D435, M. Auledas, CC BY-SA 4.0; HDL-64E, S. Jurvetson, CC BY 2.0; SparkFun, CC BY 2.0; Prophesee, Auledas, CC BY 4.0.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Walk left to right across five hardware sensors. A monocular camera (Raspberry Pi HQ Sony IMX477, cited in our readout budget) captures bearing and appearance without metric range. Active stereo (Intel RealSense D435) projects infrared speckle and triangulates depth, with error growing as range squared. Spinning lidar (Velodyne HDL-64E) times laser return pulses directly ($r = c\,t/2$). An IMU measures angular rate and specific force between frames. An event camera (Prophesee) fires on log-brightness changes, going blind to a stationary worker (while tactile and radar sensors add contact force and Doppler speed). For a worker standing motionless in dim light at 4 m, lidar sees range directly while stereo needs texture and event cameras see nothing.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Adding more sensors improves safety only when their physical failure modes are genuinely independent.}

  \notecue{Next}{Inspect real KITTI camera-and-lidar data showing how sparse laser returns supply metric depth to dense pixels.}

  Step through Napkin Math 8.2's front-end capture timeline at 1.5 m/s (1.5 mm/ms). First, the navigation camera exposes for 16 ms in dim warehouse aisles (traveling 24 mm during integration), so its true capture epoch $t_0$ sits at the exposure midpoint. Second, rolling readout (16.6 ms), link transfer, and DMA finish 26.6 ms after $t_0$; stamping on host arrival pairs pixels with a robot pose 39.9 mm farther down the aisle. Third, latching a hardware trigger at exposure start sits 8 ms early, misplacing the frame by 12 mm unless metadata adds half the current exposure (essential under auto-exposure).

\begin{frame}{The observation contract lets a consumer refuse before reading}
  \centering{\small Record 6 of the record chain. The payload is read last.}\\[0.08cm]
  \footnotesize\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{0.88}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}P{3.6cm}P{5.2cm}P{5.2cm}@{}}\toprule
    \textbf{Field} & \textbf{Navigation camera} & \textbf{Consumer check} \\\midrule
    \urow{1}{capture time, clock}{exposure midpoint, cam\_nav}{age on its own clock}
    \urow{1}{clock bound}{0.2 ms: dispatch limit 51.8 ms}{refuse if missing}
    \urow{2}{frame, calibration}{nav\_cam, revision}{reject outside valid range}
    \urow{3}{covariance, inset}{inset from p99 residual}{inflate the occupied region}
    \urow{4}{health, sequence}{saturation, sync, OOD bits}{refuse on critical bits}
    \urow{4}{CRC, build digest}{CRC-32, SHA-256}{integrity only}\bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \mlsyscredit{Sensor Perception, \S8.8, equation 12 and table 5 (navigation camera values; clock bound illustrative).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Build Record 6 of the course chain across four row groups for the navigation camera. First, time: capture epoch (exposure midpoint on named monotonic clock \texttt{cam\_nav}) plus clock-conversion bound (0.2 ms, setting the 51.8 ms dispatch cutoff) so every consumer computes current age locally. Second, place: frame ID and calibration revision with valid range. Third, error: covariance and measured p99 clearance inset inflating occupied space. Fourth, health and integrity: saturation/sync/OOD bits, monotonic sequence counter exposing drops or reordering, CRC-32, and firmware/model SHA-256 digest. Consumers verify metadata in strict order (integrity and health, clock conversion and freshness, frame validity, error) before ever parsing the payload.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A valid CRC detects accidental bit corruption rather than spoofing or semantic hallucination; a fixed contract schema also enables Lecture 15's field-by-field fault injection.}

  \notecue{Next}{Compare this observation contract against standard ROS 2 message headers to see which physical fields are absent by default.}
}
\end{frame}
