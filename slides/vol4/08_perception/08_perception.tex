\begin{frame}{Each sensor measures one physical quantity}

  \photocap{2.75cm}{imu-itg3200-adxl345.jpg}{\textbf{IMU (inertial)}\\ rotation rate, acceleration}\hfill
  \photocap{2.75cm}{prophesee-event-camera.jpg}{\textbf{Event camera}\\ change in brightness}\\[0.35cm]
  \small A single camera pixel fixes a direction and leaves the range open.
  \mlsyscredit{Wikimedia Commons: RPi HQ camera, Laserlicht, CC BY-SA 4.0; RealSense D435, M. Auledas, CC BY-SA 4.0; HDL-64E, S. Jurvetson, CC BY 2.0; SparkFun, CC BY 2.0; Prophesee, Auledas, CC BY 4.0.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Choosing sensors for the rack end begins with the physical quantity each transducer measures. A single-lens (monocular) camera, such as the Raspberry Pi HQ board with the Sony IMX477 sensor in our readout budget, captures bearing and appearance without metric range. Active stereo (Intel RealSense D435) projects infrared speckle and triangulates depth, with error growing as range squared. Spinning lidar (Velodyne HDL-64E) times laser return pulses directly ($r = c\,t/2$, half the speed of light times round-trip time). An IMU, met in Lecture 2, tracks rotation rate and non-gravitational acceleration (specific force) between frames. An event camera (Prophesee) fires on log-brightness changes, leaving it blind to a stationary worker, while tactile and radar sensors add contact force and radial speed from radar frequency shift (Doppler). For a motionless worker in dim light at 4 m, lidar measures range directly, stereo needs texture, and event cameras see nothing.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Adding more sensors improves safety only when their physical failure modes are independent.}

  \notecue{Next}{Inspect real KITTI camera and lidar data to see how sparse laser returns supply metric depth to dense pixels.}
}
\end{frame}

\section{Capture}

\begin{frame}{A frame\textquoteright s capture epoch is the midpoint of its exposure}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=14.6cm,height=4.3cm,keepaspectratio]{capture_step}{3}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Capture epoch: the instant the measurement represents.}\\[0.03cm]\uncover<2->{\small The arrival stamp pairs the frame with a pose 39.9 mm ahead of where the camera stood.}
  \mlsyscredit{Sensor Perception, \S8.2 and \S8.3; Napkin Math 8.2 stage values (illustrative).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Because a camera gathers light over a finite interval while the base drives at 1.5 m/s, or 1.5 mm/ms, dating a frame starts by fixing which instant it represents (Napkin Math 8.2). In dim warehouse aisles, the navigation camera exposes for 16 ms, covering 24 mm of travel, so its capture epoch $t_0$ sits at the exposure midpoint. Rolling readout over 16.6 ms, link transfer, and DMA into host memory finish 26.6 ms after $t_0$. Stamping on host arrival therefore pairs those pixels with a pose 39.9 mm farther down the aisle. Latching a hardware trigger at exposure start instead sits 8 ms early, misplacing the frame by 12 mm unless metadata adds half the current exposure, which matters whenever automatic exposure varies shutter duration.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Software arrival timestamps also inherit Lecture 4's operating-system scheduling jitter. During a 0.5 rad/s turn, every millisecond of timestamp slip rotates the projected scene by 0.5 mrad (milliradians).}

  \notecue{Next}{Inspect raw KITTI timestamp files that show 6 ms of host-clock skew between cameras sharing one physical trigger.}
}
\end{frame}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Even an exact timestamp for the top row leaves a second capture defect. Recall from Lecture 2 that a rolling shutter reads rows sequentially, curving turning propeller blades. In Napkin Math 8.1, 3,040 rows take 16.6 ms while the base moves forward at 1.5 m/s and yaws at 0.5 rad/s past an upright at 1.10 m. Compute per-row time, forward shift, and sideways shift.

  \notecue{Check}{Each row reads $16.6\text{ ms}/3{,}040 = 5.46\ \mu$s after the previous one. Forward motion shifts the upright by $1.5\text{ m/s} \times 16.6\text{ ms} = 24.9$ mm, half the 50 mm localization allowance. Turning adds $0.5 \times 1.10 \times 16.6 = 9.1$ mm sideways, or 6.1\% of the 150 mm side clearance. Slowing slightly is a tempting fix that barely helps. \begin{frame}{Zero-copy capture buffers pin pages until consumers finish}

  \mlsyssteps[width=14.6cm,height=4.75cm,keepaspectratio]{capture_ring_step}{3}\\[0.06cm]
  {\small\uncover<3->{A pinned slot trades a dropped new frame for an intact capture epoch.}}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Where do those dropped frames come from? Copying raw $4056 \times 3040$ images at 60 Hz would flood the shared Orin memory bus, so camera DMA and inference share a pre-allocated zero-copy ring buffer, a fixed circle of memory slots read in place. When DMA fills slot 0 with frame 10, it passes a descriptor holding the buffer pointer and capture epoch. Because the 22 ms backbone outlives one 16.7 ms frame period, the consumer pins slot 0 with one active reader (reference count 1) while DMA advances through slots 1 to 3. Without pinning, DMA wraps onto slot 0 during inference and silently blends pixels from two capture epochs with zero fault bit raised. Pinning forces DMA to skip or cleanly drop the newest frame until the consumer releases the slot.

\begin{frame}{The observation contract lets a consumer refuse before reading}
  \centering{\small Record 6 of the record chain. The payload is read last.}\\[0.08cm]
  \footnotesize\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{0.88}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}P{3.6cm}P{5.2cm}P{5.2cm}@{}}\toprule
    \textbf{Field} & \textbf{Navigation camera} & \textbf{Consumer check} \\\midrule
    \urow{1}{capture time, clock}{exposure midpoint, cam\_nav}{age on its own clock}
    \urow{1}{clock bound}{0.2 ms: dispatch limit 51.8 ms}{refuse if missing}
    \urow{2}{frame, calibration}{nav\_cam, revision}{reject outside valid range}
    \urow{3}{covariance, inset}{inset from p99 residual}{inflate the occupied region}
    \urow{4}{health, sequence}{saturation, sync, OOD bits}{refuse on critical bits}
    \urow{4}{CRC, build digest}{CRC-32, SHA-256}{integrity only}\bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \mlsyscredit{Sensor Perception, \S8.8, equation 12 and table 5 (navigation camera values; clock bound illustrative).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  To let consumers refuse stale, misframed, or degraded readings before reading payload bytes, the observation contract (Record 6 of the course chain) packages four metadata groups for the navigation camera. Time fields pair the capture epoch, taken at the exposure midpoint on monotonic clock \texttt{cam\_nav}, with the 0.2 ms clock-conversion bound setting the 51.8 ms dispatch cutoff, so each consumer computes current age locally. Place fields supply frame ID, calibration revision, and valid range. Error fields add covariance and the measured p99 clearance inset inflating occupied space. Health and integrity fields carry saturation, sync, and OOD bits, a monotonic sequence counter exposing drops or reordering, a CRC-32 checksum, and a SHA-256 cryptographic digest of firmware and model. Consumers verify integrity and health, clock conversion and freshness, frame validity, and error in that order before parsing the payload.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A valid CRC catches accidental bit corruption rather than spoofing or semantic hallucination. A fixed schema also enables testing consumers by corrupting individual fields (fault injection, Lecture 15).}

  \notecue{Next}{Compare this observation contract against standard ROS 2 headers to see which physical fields are missing by default.}
}
\end{frame}
