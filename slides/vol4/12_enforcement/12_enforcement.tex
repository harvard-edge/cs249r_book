  {\normalsize\choice{A}{a second software check}\quad\choice{B}{the operator}\quad\answerat{2}{C}{nothing; six patients overdosed}}
  \mlsyscredit{Safety Enforcement, War Story 12.1; Leveson and Turner, ``An Investigation of the Therac-25 Accidents,'' IEEE Computer, 1993. Diagram simplified.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  The Therac-25 radiation therapy machine (1985--1987; Leveson and Turner, 1993) offered an electron beam and a high-current X-ray mode requiring a tungsten target rotated into the beam. Whereas its predecessor, the Therac-20, verified turntable position with hardware microswitches, the Therac-25 removed those interlocks and relied on software. A fast operator edit raced the software and fired the 25~MeV beam without the target in place. Reveal Step 2 for answer C: nothing stopped the beam, six patients received 4,000 to 20,000~rads (up to $100\times$ therapeutic dose), and three died.

  \notecue{Check}{Vote across A, B, and C before Step 2. Choice C holds because no hardware interlock existed; choice A fails because a second software check on the same processor shares the same race condition, and choice B fails because the operator received no warning.}

  \notecue{Watch for}{The engineering lesson is not that software fails, but that a protective layer cannot serve as its own backstop.}

  \notecue{Next}{We recall Lecture 11's trajectory record and base stopping time before moving to 1~kHz enforcement.}
}

\begin{frame}{A barrier on distance alone gives the motor no say}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{reldeg_step}{2}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<2->{\normalsize $h(\theta,\omega)=\theta-\dfrac{\omega^2}{2\,\alpha_{\text{net}}}\ \ge 0$}\\[0.1cm]
  \uncover<2->{\footnotesize $h$: margin; $\theta$: clearance; $\omega$: speed; $\alpha_{\text{net}}$: net braking.\enspace Relative degree: integrators from torque to $h$.}
  \mlsyscredit{Safety Enforcement, \S12.5, Definition 12.2, \S12.5.1, and equation 5.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  A control barrier function defines a safety margin $h \ge 0$ and requires every admitted command to keep $h$ from decreasing faster than $\gamma h$, preserving safety forward in time (Definition 12.2). First, in Step 1, setting $h$ to raw clearance $\theta$ gives relative degree two: torque integrates twice (through acceleration and velocity) to reach position, so $\dot{h} = -\omega$ contains no torque and demands infinite torque at contact. Second, Step 2 subtracts stopping distance to form the kinetic barrier $h(\theta,\omega) = \theta - \omega^2 / (2\alpha_{\text{net}}) \ge 0$ (eq.~5). Now $\dot{h}$ contains torque (relative degree one), turning the barrier rule into a bound on the next torque whose $h=0$ boundary (at 79.45~mm and 1.2~rad/s) matches the stopping budget.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Relative degree counts integrators from control input to margin, not physical degrees of freedom; ML students can review the control appendix for details.}

  \notecue{Next}{We plot equation 6 to see how braking torque demand climbs as clearance shrinks toward 79.45~mm.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{An energy tank lets the arm spend only work it has earned}

Fourth, Step 4 is INHIBIT, removing drive enable via safe torque off and setting spring brakes on a hardwired path (illustrated by the emergency stop button and Pilz PNOZ s4 force-guided relay) only when the MCU itself fails (brownout, clock loss, watchdog).

  \notecue{Watch for}{These rungs are state-matched alternatives rather than a sequential staircase; the arbiter jumps directly to the rung matching the failed check and current motion state.}

  \notecue{Next}{Students next vote on whether cutting motor torque immediately is safer than running the resident stop at 1.3~m/s.}
}
\end{frame}

  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Pose the scenario in Step 1: the proposer's host crashes while the base cruises at 1.3~m/s. Have students vote between A (cut torque immediately) and B (run resident STOP first), then reveal Step 2 for answer B. Safe torque off (STO, IEC 60204-1 Category 0) disables inverter power so the motor cannot generate torque, yet it applies no braking: the base coasts unbraked while spring brakes take 30 to 80~ms to close with no credited stopping capacity. \begin{frame}{Cruise 2023: a fallback for the wrong state dragged a person}

  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  On October 2, 2023 in San Francisco (Exponent 2024; Quinn Emanuel 2024; California DMV 2023), a human-driven car struck a pedestrian into a Cruise robotaxi's lane; the robotaxi hit her and completed an emergency stop. Because its collision classifier misidentified a side impact rather than a person beneath the vehicle, it selected a pullover fallback to clear traffic. Intermittent sensor tracking caused the planner to treat the missing track as clear space, accelerating into a pullover that dragged the pedestrian roughly 6.1~m (20~ft) at up to 3.4~m/s (7.7~mph). Regulators suspended permits and Cruise recalled software across 950 vehicles. After an uncertain impact, the correct rung is holding with restraint, never initiating motion without positive evidence; Lecture 17 examines organizational accountability.
