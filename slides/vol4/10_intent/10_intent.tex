Before filtering, human annotators judged 228 of 259 proposed tasks acceptable, or 88\%, leaving 31 unsuitable proposals. Across the 31 unsuitable proposals, the filter rejected 17 and passed 14, which is a 55\% recall and a 45\% miss rate. \section{Grounding}

\begin{frame}{A spoken request names an outcome and leaves the grasp open}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=4.8cm,keepaspectratio]{voxposer_row2.jpg}\\[0.1cm]
  \small Each request omits where to touch, from which side, how wide, how hard, and for how long.
  \mlsyscredit{Huang et al., ``VoxPoser,'' CoRL 2023, Fig.~3 (two of its tasks), real-robot tasks on a Franka Emika Panda.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
  A spoken instruction sounds clear to a person yet leaves contact mechanics open. In VoxPoser (Huang et al., CoRL 2023), a language model turns requests such as ``turn on the lamp'' or ``open the vitamin bottle on the right'' into 3D value maps (spatial grids scoring where to move and avoid), and a Franka Emika Panda arm follows the composed cost. Every request omits grasp pose (position and orientation), approach direction, jaw aperture (gripper opening width), contact force ceiling, and evidence lifetime. A model alone can propose coordinates, as the running machine's intent model does decoding seven pose and gripper values in 336~ms, yet supplies no metric error bound, wrench (force and torque) ceiling, or capture timestamp.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Smooth motion on video proves no safety verification. VoxPoser's planner is optimal only relative to its value maps, so an unobserved obstacle adds no avoidance cost.}

  \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip this slide and open Grounding directly with the five checkable quantities of a grounded affordance.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, convert ``pick the red mug'' into those five concrete, checkable quantities.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A grounded affordance carries five checkable quantities}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{mug_affordance_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<3->{\footnotesize Pose, approach, aperture, wrench (force and torque) ceiling, and dated epoch in base frame (Def.\ 10.1).}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Turning ``pick the red mug'' into checkable physics starts by naming which feature the gripper touches. In 1979, ecological psychologist Gibson coined affordance for what an environment offers an agent able to act on it, and robotics makes that relation quantitative. On one mug, the rim tolerates little force, the wall requires a wide jaw span, and the handle requires six-degree-of-freedom alignment. Grounding the handle (Definition 10.1) maps it via a calibrated camera-to-base chain to a base-frame pose (position and orientation), unit approach vector, jaw aperture (gripper opening width), contact wrench (force and torque) ceiling, and dated evidence epoch. Gripper limits belong inside the affordance because a handle affords a grasp only to fingers able to hold it without cracking it.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Neither a learned grasp score nor a confident object detection grounds an affordance, because neither carries a metric error bound, a wrench ceiling, or a capture epoch.}

  \notecue{Next}{With the affordance defined, the next slide tests how the task's approved tolerance profile enforces its 15~mm goal set.}
}
\end{frame}

  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Once grounded in base coordinates, admission checks how much pose error the grasp absorbs. The goal set is a pose ellipsoid whose semi-axes come from the verified task profile (the approved commissioning specification), here 15~mm along every axis for the mug. Admission requires normalized distance (offset divided by semi-axis) to remain at most 1; a 10~mm offset yields 0.67, safely inside. Before reading on, decide what admission does when an estimate is 20~mm off yet confident.

  \notecue{Check}{The answer is C. A 20~mm offset yields 1.33, exceeding 1, where fingers hit the rim or close on air. Answer A is tempting, yet model confidence cannot shrink metric error. Choice B is the dangerous trap because widening a task specification to admit uncertainty corrupts the physical grasp requirement.}

  \notecue{Watch for}{Both the proposal's error envelope (worst-case spatial error bound) and the controller's tracking reserve (following-error margin) must fit inside the 15~mm ellipsoid. Checking axes individually is only an initial screen.}

  \notecue{Teach}{Vote across A, B, and C, discuss for one minute, and revote before revealing C.}

  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Discrete task planners face the same gap between words and physics. Recall from Lecture 1 that Shakey, the Stanford Research Institute (SRI) mobile robot, planned with STRIPS. When Fikes and Nilsson formalized that operator language in 1971, Shakey's real-world actions often broke its assumptions. Here, \texttt{pick(mug, handle, conveyor)} asserts \texttt{Holding(mug)} even if no inverse-kinematics (IK) joint-angle solution reaches the handle or a bin wall blocks the approach. Each symbolic fact needs a live motion predicate checked at admission, station, or runtime. Task and motion planning (TAMP) discharges facts with geometric samplers, routines testing continuous grasps and paths, where failure means none was found rather than none exists. Letting a language model write Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL) code, as in LLM+P (2023), still omits those physical checks.

  \notecue{Watch for}{\texttt{Holding(mug)} after jaw closure remains a hypothesis until retention checks pass, and a squeeze inside the force band still does not prove the gripper holds the right mug.}

  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
  Suppose a red glass jar sits beside the requested red ceramic mug on the conveyor. Open-vocabulary detectors matching arbitrary text prompts, such as OWL-ViT (2022) and Grounding DINO (2023), score each bounding box with independent sigmoid output heads that never normalize across candidates. Because each head acts alone, the illustrative scores of 0.62 for the mug and 0.48 for the jar do not sum to one and form no probability distribution over objects, echoing Lecture 3's warning against treating raw softmax outputs as calibrated probabilities. Two failures then follow. Two identical mugs can both score high without flagging a tie, and a lone jar can clear a fixed threshold when no mug is present.

  \notecue{Watch for}{These photos illustrate the belt geometry rather than actual detector outputs. Candidate scores must be normalized and calibrated across retained boxes before testing uniqueness.}

Bohannon (1997) reports healthy adults walking comfortably near 1.4~m/s and up to 2.0 to 2.5~m/s at maximum speed, up to $1.58\times$ faster, while ISO 13855 uses 2,000~mm/s for close hand reach, so site-specific speed validation remains mandatory. Before reading on, compute the horizon where growth alone spans the 1.30~m aisle at 1.6~m/s.

  \notecue{Check}{Dividing 1.30~m by 1.6~m/s gives $1.30 / 1.6 = 0.81\text{ s}$ before adding body width or track error. Screening several seconds ahead is tempting, yet it triggers the freezing robot problem (Trautman and Krause, 2010), where independent uncertainty discs block every path even when people would yield.}

  \notecue{Watch for}{Prevent freezing by screening short receding horizons (windows shifted on fresh measurements) over only the admitted motion prefix and its stop, admitting interaction-aware predictors (joint robot-human response models) only with bounded error.}

  \notecue{Teach}{Give pairs one minute to compute the horizon before revealing 0.81~s.}

\mlsysdivider{Who ends the authority, and on whose clock?}{Grounded Intent, \S10.9 and \S10.10}
\section{Lease}

\mlsysnote{
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  An expiring lease answers who ends authority by taking the earliest of three deadlines (Equation 9). The first deadline, $t_0 + \tauev$, runs from sensor capture plus the evidence horizon, so only fresh captures extend it. The second, $t_{\text{adm}} + \Tadm$, runs from admission plus the host-requested administrative lease clamped by the MCU, catching silent or hung hosts. The third, $\tstop$, is the latest instant from which a worst-case stop (22~ms onset plus braking) still finishes inside remaining clearance, recomputed whenever speed or clearance changes. The MCU evaluates the minimum on its own monotonic hardware clock, a local counter that cannot jump like wall time. On expiry, a hardware timer interrupt revokes tracking authority and engages a load-matched fallback (powered hold, controlled deceleration, or torque off).

  \notecue{Watch for}{The intent lease authorizes a goal, whereas Lecture 4's 60~ms chunk lease authorizes motion. Each terminates authority from its own side.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, compare these three terms numerically for the red mug on the conveyor.}
}
\mlsysfocus{The intent lease expires at the earliest of three deadlines}{%
$\texp = \min\big(\underbrace{t_0 + \tauev}_{\text{evidence}},\;
\underbrace{t_{\text{adm}} + \Tadm}_{\text{host silence}},\;
\underbrace{\tstop}_{\text{stopping}}\big)$%
}

\begin{frame}{Whichever margin runs out first ends the lease}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{lease_terms_step}{4}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small The proposal requests a duration; the MCU clamps it. Expiry needs no message from the host.}
  \mlsyscredit{Grounded Intent, \S10.9, equation 9, and the red-mug lease (100 ms administrative lease; 300 mm clearance at the 1 m/s ceiling). Derived.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Evaluating all three deadlines on the MCU's monotonic counter for the red mug shows which margin runs out first. Evidence expires at 219~ms after capture with a qualified tracker, or 60~ms untracked, tightened further by subtracting the controller's tracking reserve. The 100~ms administrative host-silence limit guards against scheduler stalls, garbage-collection pauses, or kernel panics even on static targets. For 300~mm of clearance at 1~m/s, credible deceleration of $3.08\text{ m/s}^2$ plus 22~ms onset consumes 184.4~mm, placing the stopping deadline $\tstop$ at 115.6~ms (about 116~ms) after admission. Taking the minimum leaves the 100~ms administrative limit governing when tracked, while the 60~ms evidence limit governs when untracked. A faster model extends none of the three terms.
