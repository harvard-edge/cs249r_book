\begin{frame}{A model filter passed 14 of 31 unsuitable tasks}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=4.0cm,keepaspectratio]{autort_step}{2}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Kept tasks: 200 of 214 acceptable (93\%), against 228 of 259 before the filter (88\%).}

  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  First, before filtering, human annotators judged 228 of 259 proposed tasks acceptable (88\%), leaving 31 unsuitable proposals. Next, after the constitutional screen, the system kept 214 tasks, of which 200 were acceptable (93\%): it rejected 17 unsuitable tasks and passed 14, a 55\% recall and 45\% miss rate. Improving the kept set from 88\% to 93\% acceptable helps data collection, yet a safety function must stop unsafe tasks reliably rather than passing 45\% of them. For that reason, AutoRT kept physical safety outside the model in joint-force limits, emergency stops, and line-of-sight supervision.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Upgrading the filter model in the same language modality still leaves shared semantic blind spots; physical bounds must live on an independent permission path.}

\section{Grounding}

\begin{frame}{A spoken request names an outcome and leaves the grasp open}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=4.8cm,keepaspectratio]{voxposer_row2.jpg}\\[0.1cm]
  \small Each request omits where to touch, from which side, how wide, how hard, and for how long.
  \mlsyscredit{Huang et al., ``VoxPoser,'' CoRL 2023, Fig.~3 (two of its tasks), real-robot tasks on a Franka Emika Panda.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
  These real Franka Emika Panda runs from VoxPoser (Huang et al., CoRL 2023) show a language model composing 3D value maps on the left from requests such as ``turn on the lamp'' or ``open the vitamin bottle on the right,'' which the arm follows on the right. Every request names an outcome while omitting grasp pose, approach direction, jaw aperture, contact force ceiling, and evidence lifetime. A language model alone can propose coordinates, as our running machine's intent model does when decoding seven pose and gripper values in 336~ms, yet it supplies no metric error bound, wrench ceiling, or capture timestamp.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Smooth motion on video proves no safety verification; VoxPoser's planner is optimal only relative to whatever assumptions its synthesized value maps encode.}

  \notecue{Optional}{For a 50-minute class, skip this slide and open Grounding directly with the five checkable quantities of a grounded affordance.}

  \notecue{Next}{We convert ``pick the red mug'' into those five concrete, checkable quantities.}

\begin{frame}{A grounded affordance carries five checkable quantities}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{mug_affordance_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<3->{\footnotesize Pose, approach, aperture, wrench (force and torque) ceiling, and dated epoch in base frame (Def.\ 10.1).}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Gibson defined an affordance as what an object offers an agent able to act on it; robotics makes that relation quantitative. First, in Step 1, one mug presents distinct grasp sites: the rim tolerates little force, the wall requires a wide jaw span, and the handle requires full six-degree-of-freedom alignment. Second, Step 2 expresses the handle site as a base-frame pose, a unit approach vector, and a jaw aperture. Third, Step 3 attaches a contact wrench (force and torque) ceiling and a dated evidence epoch through a calibrated camera-to-base chain (Definition 10.1). Gripper limits belong inside the affordance because a handle affords a grasp only to fingers whose aperture and wrench band can hold without cracking it.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Neither a learned grasp score nor a confident object detection grounds an affordance; neither carries a metric error bound, a wrench ceiling, or a capture epoch.}

  \notecue{Next}{With the affordance defined, we vote on how the verified task profile enforces its 15~mm goal set.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The task profile sets the 15 mm goal set}
  \mlsysactivity{Vote}{2 min}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{goalset_step}{2}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \small The estimate is 20 mm off and the model is confident. What should admission do?\\[0.25cm]
      \choice{A}{admit; the model is confident}\\[0.1cm]
      \choice{B}{widen the goal set to 25 mm}\\[0.1cm]
      \answerat{2}{C}{refuse: 1.33 is outside}\\[0.25cm]
      \uncover<2->{\footnotesize The goal set is a specification of what the grasp can absorb. Error envelope plus tracking reserve must fit inside it.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Grounded Intent, \S10.2 and equation 1 (all semi-axes 15 mm; one slice drawn). Normalized distances derived.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  The goal set is a position-and-orientation ellipsoid around the target whose semi-axes come from the verified task profile, here 15~mm along every axis for the mug. Admission evaluates normalized distance (offset divided by semi-axis), requiring it to remain at most 1. In Step 1, a 10~mm offset yields 0.67, safely inside; in Step 2, a 20~mm offset yields 1.33, outside, where fingers strike the rim or close on air.

  \notecue{Check}{Vote across A, B, and C, discuss for one minute, and revote before revealing Step 2. Choice C is right because 1.33 exceeds 1; choice A fails because model confidence cannot shrink metric error, and choice B is the dangerous trap because widening a task specification to admit an uncertain estimate corrupts the physical grasp requirement.}

  \notecue{Watch for}{Both the proposal's error envelope and the controller's tracking reserve must fit inside the 15~mm ellipsoid; checking axes individually is only an initial screen.}

  \notecue{Next}{We see that classical symbolic operators face the same grounding gap until measurements discharge their facts.}
}
\end{frame}

        Reachable(handle) & \onslide<2->{in workspace, IK in limits} & \onslide<2->{admission}\\

      \end{tabular}\\[0.2cm]
      \uncover<2->{\footnotesize A step runs only after its facts are discharged by measurement.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: The wub, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons (Computer History Museum). Grounded Intent, \S10.3 and table 1.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Symbolic planning started on a physical robot when Fikes and Nilsson built STRIPS in 1971 for Shakey, whose actions often failed to match operator assumptions. First, in Step 1, the operator \texttt{pick(mug, handle, conveyor)} declares preconditions and adds \texttt{Holding(mug)} even if no inverse-kinematics solution reaches the handle or a bin wall blocks the approach. Second, Step 2 pairs every symbolic fact with a live motion predicate checked at admission, the station, or runtime. Task and motion planning (TAMP) discharges these facts with geometric samplers, where a sampler failure means no solution was found rather than none exists. Letting a language model generate PDDL (as in LLM+P) still populates only the left column.

  \notecue{Watch for}{\texttt{Holding(mug)} after jaw closure remains a hypothesis until retention checks pass, and a squeeze inside the force band still does not prove the gripper holds the right mug.}

  \notecue{Next}{We next inspect OK-Robot's empirical failure breakdown to see what learned grasp scores miss in real homes.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimate: does the producer refuse the jar?}
  \mlsysactivity{Estimate}{2.5 min}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
      \begin{mlsyscard}{mlsysaccent}
        \footnotesize\textbf{Given}\\[0.03cm]
        Raw: mug 0.62, jar 0.48\\
        200 scenes, $\alpha = 0.10$\\
        Rank $\lceil 201 \times 0.9 \rceil = 181$: $\hat{q} = 0.60$\\
        Keep if normalized $\ge 0.40$
      \end{mlsyscard}
      \footnotesize
      \uncover<2->{$0.62/(0.62+0.48)=0.56$, jar $0.44$: \textbf{\textcolor{figveto}{size 2, refuse}}\\[0.03cm]}
      \uncover<3->{Jar 0.10: $0.86, 0.14$: \textbf{\textcolor{figperm}{size 1}}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.4cm,keepaspectratio]{conformal_step}{2}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Grounded Intent, \S10.4.1 and equation 3 (worked example; qhat assumed in the chapter). Derived.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Conformal prediction replaces a single top guess with a prediction set offering calibrated coverage. Across 200 labeled calibration scenes with $\alpha = 0.10$, the nonconformity score is one minus the true object's normalized score, and the threshold lies at rank $\lceil 201 \times 0.9 \rceil = 181$. With the chapter's assumed $\hat{q} = 0.60$, any candidate scoring at least $1 - 0.60 = 0.40$ after normalization stays in the set. In Step 2, 0.62 and 0.48 normalize to 0.56 and 0.44, so both remain (size 2) and the producer refuses with \texttt{ERR\_GROUNDING\_AMBIGUITY} before crossing the board link; entropy (0.685 of 0.693 nats) shows spread without giving a coverage bound. In Step 3, a 0.10 jar score normalizes to 0.14 and drops out, leaving a singleton at 0.86.

  \notecue{Check}{Give pairs two minutes to normalize both cases and decide refusal. Both candidates survive when normalized to 0.56 and 0.44, forcing refusal; comparing raw scores alone hides the ambiguity.}

  \notecue{Watch for}{Coverage requires exchangeability between calibration and deployment; new lighting or belt layouts break it silently, so calibration records expire per station.}

\mlsysdivider{Who ends the authority, and on whose clock?}{Grounded Intent, \S10.9 and \S10.10}
\section{Lease}

\mlsysnote{
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  Read equation 9 term by term as the lecture's central contract. The first deadline, $t_0 + \tauev$, runs from sensor capture plus the evidence horizon; only fresh captures extend it. The second, $t_{\text{adm}} + \Tadm$, runs from admission plus the host-requested administrative lease clamped by the MCU, catching silent or hung hosts. The third, $\tstop$, is the latest instant from which a worst-case stop (22~ms onset plus braking) still finishes inside remaining clearance, recomputed whenever speed or clearance changes. The MCU evaluates the minimum on its own monotonic hardware clock, never wall time; on expiry, a timer interrupt revokes tracking authority and engages a load-matched fallback (powered hold, controlled deceleration, or torque off).

  \notecue{Watch for}{The intent lease authorizes a goal, whereas Lecture 4's 60~ms chunk lease authorizes motion; each terminates authority from its own side.}

  \notecue{Next}{We compare these three terms numerically for the red mug on the conveyor.}
}
\mlsysfocus{The intent lease expires at the earliest of three deadlines}{%
$\texp = \min\big(\underbrace{t_0 + \tauev}_{\text{evidence}},\;
\underbrace{t_{\text{adm}} + \Tadm}_{\text{host silence}},\;
\underbrace{\tstop}_{\text{stopping}}\big)$%
}

  {\small The proposal requests a duration; the MCU clamps it. Expiry needs no message from the host.}
  \mlsyscredit{Grounded Intent, \S10.9, equation 9, and the red-mug lease (100 ms administrative lease; 300 mm clearance at the 1 m/s ceiling). Derived.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Trace all three deadlines on the MCU's monotonic counter across four clicks. First, Step 1 plots evidence expiry at 219~ms after capture with a qualified tracker (60~ms untracked), tightened further by subtracting the controller's tracking reserve. Second, Step 2 adds the 100~ms administrative host-silence limit, guarding against scheduler stalls, garbage-collection pauses, or kernel panics even on static targets. Third, Step 3 computes the stopping deadline for 300~mm clearance at 1~m/s: credible deceleration of $3.08\text{ m/s}^2$ plus 22~ms onset requires 184.4~mm, placing $\tstop$ at 115.6~ms (about 116~ms) after admission. Fourth, Step 4 takes the minimum: 100~ms administrative governs when tracked, and 60~ms evidence governs when untracked. A faster model extends none of the three terms.
