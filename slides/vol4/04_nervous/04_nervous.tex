\begin{frame}{Predict: can a faster tick close the loop?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{2 min}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=14.6cm]{phase_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small \textbf{Phase margin:} the extra lag a loop absorbs before it oscillates. Delay spends phase margin; each stage subtracts $\omega_c \times$ delay.}
  \mlsyscredit{The Nervous System, \S4.3.1, equation 1 (20 Hz crossover and 5$^\circ$ allowance illustrative; worst-case execution time 250\us declared).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Can the permission tick close a 20 Hz joint loop across a 1 ms fieldbus? Pure delay subtracts $\omega_c \times \text{delay}$ from phase margin. At a 20 Hz crossover (where open-loop gain falls to 1), delay costs $360^\circ \times 20\text{ Hz} = 7.2^\circ/\text{ms}$. Against a 5$^\circ$ budget (0.69 ms maximum), sample-and-hold (holding a command across one 1 ms tick, adding 0.5 ms of effective delay) spends 3.6$^\circ$, and declared 250 $\mu$s compute spends 1.8$^\circ$.   \notecue{Check}{Neither works. The 1 ms bus spends 7.2$^\circ$, pushing total lag (1.75 ms) to 12.6$^\circ$, or 2.5$\times$ the 5$^\circ$ allowance. An infinitely fast tick removes hold and compute yet leaves 7.2$^\circ$ of transport lag. A faster tick is tempting, confusing sampling with transport delay. Closing current and velocity loops inside the drives resolves it, keeping bus-closed loops below $5^\circ / (360^\circ \times 1.75\text{ ms}) = 7.9\text{ Hz}$.}

\begin{frame}{The permission path runs where the proposer cannot reach}
  \centering
  \vspace*{-0.15cm}\mlsyssteps[width=14.6cm]{twoproc_step}{3}\\[0.0cm]
  \uncover<3->{\footnotesize\textbf{Independence requirement:} under any proposer load, the tick meets its bound and can still refuse.\\ \textbf{Actuation authority:} the tick is the sole writer of every drive register.}
  \mlsyscredit{The Nervous System, \S4.2 (figure 2), \S4.6 (figure 7), redrawn.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Because a general-purpose host cannot shield a 1 kHz tick from proposer load, the architecture separates two processors. The application processor runs perception, the 2 Hz intent model, and the 20 Hz chunk policy, sending proposals over an untrusted board link with cyclic redundancy checks (CRCs), sequence numbers, and zero shared memory. A safety microcontroller (MCU) holds frames in local SRAM and runs the 1 kHz permission tick, checking freshness, lease expiry, and kinematic limits (position, speed, and reach bounds) against measured encoder state. As sole drive-register writer, it relies on lockstep cores (two cores compared cycle-by-cycle, Lecture 13), error-correcting code (ECC) SRAM against bit flips, a memory protection unit (MPU) confining task memory, clock and voltage monitors, and an independent oscillator watchdog. No wire links the host to drive registers.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Permission checks must read measured encoder state rather than prior commanded setpoints, because a stripped gear hides motion failure. Two chips can still share power rails, clocks, heat, or reset lines, inventoried resource by resource in Lecture 13.}

  \notecue{Next}{See why actuation authority requires auditing every debug and diagnostic path to drive registers.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Only the tick writes a drive register}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=13.2cm,height=4.2cm,keepaspectratio]{solewriter_step}{2}\\[0.04cm]
  {\small \textbf{Actuation authority:} the exclusive ability to change drive states. Every service port needs an interlock or a hardware jumper in production.}
  \mlsyscredit{The Nervous System, \S4.6 (actuation authority; the 35.0~A calibration write is the chapter's illustrative example).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  Keeping the host off the drive bus still fails if another physical port bypasses the permission tick. Actuation authority is the exclusive physical ability to alter power-stage drive registers, including current references, pulse-width modulation (PWM) settings, and inverter bridge enables (control lines allowing power switches to energize motor phases). Normally every admitted motion flows solely through the 1 kHz tick, yet an unmonitored serial service port on the wheel drives breaks that boundary. If a diagnostic daemon writes an illustrative 35.0 A calibration current through that port, the mailbox still reports zero velocity, so software checks pass while the wheels generate unapproved torque. Production hardware must sever or physically jumper-interlock every service port, JTAG (hardware debug) interface, and auxiliary bus-master path (any peripheral able to write registers directly).

  \notecue{Watch for}{It is easy to picture authority as a clean software arrow, yet true actuation authority covers every physical wire and bus master capable of modifying motor-inverter registers.}

\mlsysdivider{How do two clocks hand over a proposal without waiting?}{The Nervous System, \S4.3.2}
\section{Mailbox}

\begin{frame}{A queue hands the tick old plans; a mailbox the newest}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=14.6cm]{queue_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small A \textbf{state message} replaces the old value in place. Backpressure cannot pause moving mass.}
  \mlsyscredit{The Nervous System, \S4.3.2 (event and state messages, after Kopetz 2011).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Bridging an asynchronous proposer and the permission tick first requires choosing the right message semantics. A conventional first-in, first-out (FIFO) queue preserves every message in order, which fails under physical motion. If the reader lags or the writer bursts after a stall, the tick consumes obsolete proposal 5 while proposal 9 already reflects newer sensors, and backpressure (slowing the writer when a buffer fills) cannot pause moving mass. Replacing the queue with a one-slot mailbox overwrites the old proposal in place, so the tick always inspects the freshest complete plan. Following Kopetz (2011) on time-triggered real-time systems, real-time control separates overwrite state messages (live commands and sensor state) from queued event messages (diagnostic logs and fault records stored in ordered audit buffers).

  \notecue{Watch for}{Overwriting unread proposals does not discard future waypoints of an already admitted chunk. Those execute on schedule under the active lease until a valid newer chunk replaces them.}

  \notecue{Next}{Predict how long a 1 kHz reader waits under a blocking mutex, a try-lock, and a one-attempt seqlock.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict: how long does each reader wait?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{2 min}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=11.0cm]{mailbox_meas_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small 1 kHz reader; 20 Hz writer stalls 3 ms mid-write on every fourth write; 20,000 reads each.}
  \mlsyscredit{Measured by the lecture authors, 19 Oct 2026: C, pthreads, reader as a macOS real-time thread, Apple M5 Max (images/data/mailbox.c).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  A one-slot mailbox still needs synchronization without waiting. This C POSIX thread (pthread) benchmark (\texttt{images/data/mailbox.c}) runs 20,000 reads per scheme on macOS. A 1 kHz real-time reader faces a 20 Hz writer stalling 3 ms mid-write every fourth 16-waypoint chunk, modeling a page fault (loading memory from disk) or preemption. Predict worst-case reader wait for a blocking mutex, a try-lock (returning immediately if busy), and a one-attempt seqlock (a lock-free protocol checking a sequence counter once without waiting and discarding torn copies).

  \notecue{Check}{The blocking mutex stalled the reader up to 3,766 $\mu$s, pushing 99 reads past a 1 ms tick by inheriting writer stalls. Assuming locks stay short repeats Pathfinder's mistake. \begin{frame}{The seqlock reader makes one bounded attempt per tick}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=14.6cm]{seqlock_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Odd counter: a write is open. Changed counter: a torn copy. Either way, keep the active chunk.}
  \mlsyscredit{The Nervous System, \S4.3.2 (figure 4 panel b, redrawn); Lamport 1977; Kopetz and Reisinger 1993.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  To catch partial writes without locks, the safety microcontroller's receive interrupt increments a sequence counter to odd, copies payload bytes, and increments to even. The higher-priority 1 kHz tick reads counter, payload, and counter once without retrying. On a clean read (8 before and 8 after), it checks freshness, lease, and limits. On an odd 9 mid-write, it discards the partial copy in constant time, keeps the active chunk, and reads 10 next tick. If a writer runs during a read (on another core, as in the thread benchmark, or at higher priority), both reads can be even yet differ, likewise flagging a torn copy. During a host stall, 10 persists while the lease counts down. Because the host never touches microcontroller SRAM, a crash never leaves the counter odd.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Retry loops lack an execution bound under repeated collisions. Hardware that reorders memory accesses needs memory barriers, such as the Data Memory Barrier (\texttt{DMB}) on embedded Arm Cortex-M microcontrollers, so payload reads never reorder past counter checks. Triple- or quadruple-buffered slots avoid collisions at higher memory cost.}

  \notecue{Next}{Compare retry-loop seqlock code in Linux with single-attempt try-lock code in \texttt{ros2\_control}.}

\begin{frame}{Pathfinder's blocking term had no bound}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=4.1cm,keepaspectratio]{pathfinder_step}{2}\par\vspace{0.05cm}
  {\footnotesize\itshape ``After some time had passed, a watchdog timer would go off ... and initiate a total system reset.''}\par\vspace{0.08cm}
  {\small $R = C + \textcolor{RedLine}{\mathbf{B}} + I$\uncover<2->{: priority inheritance (Sha et al.\ 1990), one flag uploaded to the lander, bounded $B$.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Jones 1997 (after Wilner, RTSS 1997 keynote), as archived at cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs614; Reeves 1997; The Nervous System, War Story 4.1.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  With $R = C + B + I$ in hand, the opening Mars Pathfinder case resolves directly. The bus task's execution time $C$ and preemption $I$ were bounded, yet its blocking term $B$ had no upper bound because a medium-priority communications task indefinitely preempted the low-priority weather task while it held the bus mutex, firing the total-reset watchdog. The fix comes from Sha, Rajkumar, and Lehoczky (1990), who formalized priority inheritance to bound blocking. Priority inheritance temporarily raises the lock holder to the blocked high-priority task's priority so medium-priority work cannot preempt it, bounding $B$ to one critical section (the code block holding the lock). NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) reproduced the bug on a ground testbed with full tracing and uplinked a patch enabling the mutex's inheritance flag.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Engineers had left priority inheritance disabled to avoid minor mutex overhead and dismissed one or two pre-flight resets as hardware glitches. Here, the permission tick eliminates mutexes altogether by using the seqlock.}

  \notecue{Next}{Extend timing bounds onto the physical drive bus by comparing CAN FD and EtherCAT across all nine axes.}
}
\end{frame}

  \thumbline[1.15cm]{thumb_rta_step3.pdf}{Deadlines hold only when $C$, $B$, and $I$ are bounded; p99 and measured maxima prove neither.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Four principles carry forward from the Nervous System into the rest of the course. First, physics sets lower control rates, fast loops close in the drive, and only the tick writes drive registers. Third, the chunk lease sits inside the stopping budget, from a 55 ms floor to a 121.4 ms upper limit, and heartbeats never renew it. Fourth, deadlines require analytical bounds on $C$, $B$, and $I$ recorded with their bound kind in the handoff record.
