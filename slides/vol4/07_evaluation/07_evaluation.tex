\begin{frame}{A success rate belongs to a population of trial conditions}
  \centering
  {\small\textcolor{midgray}{The closed-loop estimand}}\\[0.12cm]
  {\Large $p(\mathcal{D}) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{c\,\sim\,\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\,\phi(\text{Traj}(c))\,\bigr]$}\\[0.20cm]
  {\large $c = (\,
    \uncover<2->{\textcolor{BlueLine}{\mathbf{x}_0}},\;
    \uncover<2->{\textcolor{GreenLine}{e}},\;
    \uncover<3->{\textcolor{OrangeLine}{m}},\;
    \uncover<4->{\textcolor{VioletLine}{\pi_\theta}},\;
    \uncover<4->{\textcolor{midgray}{\kappa}}\,)$}\\[0.18cm]
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}P{0.175\textwidth}P{0.175\textwidth}P{0.175\textwidth}P{0.175\textwidth}P{0.175\textwidth}@{}}
    \uncover<2->{\textcolor{BlueLine}{\textbf{start state}}} &
    \uncover<2->{\textcolor{GreenLine}{\textbf{environment}}} &
    \uncover<3->{\textcolor{OrangeLine}{\textbf{this unit}}} &
    \uncover<4->{\textcolor{VioletLine}{\textbf{frozen policy}}} &
    \uncover<4->{\textcolor{midgray}{\textbf{protocol}}} \\
    \uncover<2->{pose, speed} &
    \uncover<2->{friction, light, heat} &
    \uncover<3->{gear play, battery, wear} &
    \uncover<4->{weights, precision} &
    \uncover<4->{deadline, goal, predicate} \\
  \end{tabular}\\[0.22cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<5->{Change friction or battery voltage and $p$ changes with no weight touched.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Closed-Loop Evaluation, \S7.2, equation 1 and Definition 7.1 (closed-loop evaluation estimand).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  What those clean runs leave unstated is the population they sampled. Definition 7.1 formalizes closed-loop success as $p(\mathcal{D}) = \mathbb{E}_{c \sim \mathcal{D}}[\phi(\text{Traj}(c))]$, the expected value of a frozen pass-fail predicate $\phi$ over trajectories from a declared trial-condition distribution $\mathcal{D}$. Five parts define each condition. First come initial state $\mathbf{x}_0$ (pose and speed) and environment $e$ (floor friction, lighting, ambient temperature). Next comes the specific hardware unit $m$, capturing gear backlash, camera mount alignment, and battery charge, which recalls rig-to-rig divergence across nominally identical units from Lecture 5. Finally come frozen policy $\pi_\theta$ (weights, precision, permission parameters) and evaluation protocol $\kappa$ (deadline, goal, predicate). Changing floor friction, wearing a tire, or depleting the battery therefore changes $p$ without altering a single weight.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A common mistake from supervised machine learning is treating held-out accuracy as an intrinsic property of weights. Physical success belongs to the closed loop and sampled population, so any policy scores 100\% if tested only in benign conditions.}

  \notecue{Next}{Map the twenty clean runs onto the six-cell scenario table beside a real multi-setting benchmark.}
}
\end{frame}

  \end{tabular}\\[0.3cm]
  {\small\uncover<5->{ACR (autonomous completion) counts only $A = 1$; MTBI (mean time between interventions) prices burden.\\Intervened runs are censored: unassisted outcome stays unknown.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Closed-Loop Evaluation, \S7.2.2 (four outcomes; ACR, MTBI, intervention density, reset latency).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Fixing the tested cell settles the sampled population, yet a single pass rate still hides whether the policy acted alone or needed rescue under Lecture 1's proposal boundary. Four door outcomes separate credit. First, the policy succeeds alone ($Y=1, A=1$, 0 interventions). Second, the permission path clips force and the door opens ($Y=1, A=0$, 1 clip), so the machine succeeded safely while the policy did not act alone. Third, the permission path halts the arm ($Y=0, A=0$, 1 stop), protecting hardware while failing the task. Fourth, an operator clears a jammed pin ($Y=1, A=0$, 1 human intervention), where a 60 s manual reset takes 70.6\% of an 85 s trial (derived). Evaluation tracks three channels separately, task completion $Y$, autonomous completion rate (ACR, counting $A=1$ only), and mean time between interventions (MTBI) for supervisory burden.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A safety clip means the architecture worked rather than failed, yet it still counts against policy autonomy. Every intervened trial leaves the unassisted crash outcome censored, meaning unknown.}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  To test whether low offline loss predicts closed-loop competence, Mandlekar et al. (CoRL 2021) rolled out every training checkpoint of behavior-cloning recurrent neural network (BC-RNN) manipulation policies in closed loop. On Square and Transport, offline loss inverted the ranking. On Square, the best closed-loop checkpoint succeeded 80.7\% of the time against 2.7\% for the lowest validation-loss checkpoint; on Transport, the split was 64.0\% against 0.7\%. Across 2,000 training epochs on Square, validation loss bottoms out near epochs 100 to 300 and climbs while closed-loop success keeps rising. Treating lower offline loss as better execution is the offline evaluation illusion, because replay resets to demonstrator states each step and never visits states from the policy's own errors, Lecture 5's endogenous covariate shift.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Offline metrics remain useful as a fast first screen for broken checkpoints, memory footprint, and inference latency, yet they cannot rank closed-loop competence.}

\begin{frame}{A 1° heading bias: how far down a 30 m aisle?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{3 min}
  \centering
  {\small Base at 1.5 m/s, 15 cm clearance each side. Replay MSE: $3.05\times10^{-4}$ rad$^2$.\uncover<2->{\enspace $y(t) = v\sin(\Delta\psi)\,t$.}}\\[0.10cm]
  {\normalsize\choice{A}{Never hits}\quad\choice{B}{About 25 m}\quad\answerat{3}{C}{About 9 m}\quad\choice{D}{About 1 m}}\\[0.10cm]
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.3cm,keepaspectratio]{aisle_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Closed-Loop Evaluation, \S7.3.1, eq.\ 2, fig.\ 1 (derived). Explore: Open-Loop vs.\ Closed-Loop Drift on mlsysbook.ai.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  A navigation policy has a steady $1^\circ$ heading bias, giving a tiny $3.05 \times 10^{-4}$ rad$^2$ replay MSE because replay resets to the centerline every step. Before reading on, predict how far the base travels at 1.5 m/s before spending its 15 cm side clearance.

  \notecue{Check}{The answer is C, about 9 m. A tempting answer is A, because replay error stays flat, or B, underestimating small angles. Under closed-loop planar unicycle kinematics (a wheeled base that cannot move sideways), lateral offset grows as $y(t) = v \sin(\Delta\psi)\,t$. Clearance runs out in $0.15 / (1.5 \sin 1^\circ) = 5.7$ s, hitting racking at 8.6 m, under one third of the 30 m aisle (derived). A permission path must act earlier to cover detection and braking.}

  \notecue{Watch for}{Tiny radian-squared scores hide real drift from tire wear or floor camber, and a constant turning-rate (yaw-rate) bias makes drift grow quadratically with time.}

\begin{frame}{Against a stiff surface, 2 mm of error is 1,000 N}

  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
  Aisle drift builds gradually, whereas contact against a rigid latch is discontinuous. On a stiff $5 \times 10^5$ N/m latch interface, contact force scales linearly with penetration, so the 15 N tripwire triggers at 0.03 mm and the 100 N structural ceiling arrives at 0.2 mm. A 2 mm error demands 1,000 N (derived, though actual peaks depend on compliance and active torque limits). Because mean squared error scores 2 mm in empty air the same as 2 mm into steel, a checkpoint with higher free-space MSE and lower contact error can beat a lower-MSE rival, an effect called rank inversion. Cosine similarity, which measures only directional alignment and ignores magnitude, also fails here, because a predicted force pointing in nearly the target direction scores 0.999 even when its magnitude is too small to overcome the latch's static preload, leaving the arm stalled.

\begin{frame}{Each evidence regime trades one blind spot for another}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.9cm,keepaspectratio]{regimes_step}{4}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Because offline scores miss drift and contact, evaluation arranges four evidence regimes across two axes, causal feedback horizontally and physical realism vertically. Next, closed-loop simulation restores feedback across thousands of parallel runs, yet tests only the modeled world without real backlash, slip, or contact physics. Because the baseline drives the robot, shadow operation suffers trajectory confinement and cannot reveal drift caused by the candidate (used as a release filter in Lecture 16). Finally, constrained physical testing closes the loop on hardware inside an enforced safety envelope of tripwires, tethers, or permission-path limits, which right-censors outcomes past that boundary.

\begin{frame}{Twenty clean runs still allow one failure in seven}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{inversion_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\normalsize\uncover<2->{$p^{20} = \asig$ gives $\plower = 0.05^{1/20} = $ \textbf{0.861}, so failure may reach 13.9 percent.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Closed-Loop Evaluation, \S7.6, figure 3, equation 3 and Napkin Math 7.1; Clopper and Pearson 1934. Derived.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  When observed success is $\hat{p} = 1$, the normal-approximation (Wald) confidence interval $\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$ collapses to zero width, so evaluation uses exact binomial likelihood (Clopper and Pearson 1934). The curve plots the probability of 20 consecutive independent successes, $p^{20}$, across candidate true success probabilities $p$. Setting the 5\% significance level $\alpha_{\text{sig}} = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05$ for 95\% confidence and solving $p^{20} = 0.05$ gives $p_{\text{lower}} = 0.05^{1/20} = 0.861$. Any true $p$ below 0.861 has less than a 5\% chance of producing 20 clean runs in a row, so the record rules it out, leaving failure rates up to 13.9\% (about one in seven) consistent. For intuition, a policy failing one in eight runs still logs 20/20 clean runs in 6.9\% of campaigns (derived).

  \notecue{Watch for}{Confidence of 95\% describes the procedure's long-run coverage across repeated campaigns, rather than a 95\% probability that this fixed policy exceeds 86.1\%.}

  \notecue{Teach}{Repeat the coverage-rate distinction slowly for students encountering confidence intervals for the first time.}

  \notecue{Next}{Distill this exact exponential bound into the rule of three.}
}
\end{frame}

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  For rare failures, that exact bound simplifies into the governing scaling law of closed-loop testing. Approximating $\ln(1 - p_{\text{fail}}) \approx -p_{\text{fail}}$ in $(1 - p_{\text{fail}})^n = 0.05$ gives $n\,p_{\text{fail}} \approx \ln 20 = 2.996 \approx 3$. That approximation yields the rule of three (Hanley and Lippman-Hand 1983, from clinical trials with zero adverse events), under which $n$ clean trials upper-bound the failure rate near $3/n$. For 20 runs, $3/20 = 15\%$ against the exact 13.9\%. Solving for trial count gives $n \approx 3 / (1 - p_{\text{target}})$. Reaching 90\% requires 29 clean runs, 99\% requires 299, and 99.9\% requires 2,995, so every added nine multiplies clean trials tenfold.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Because the numerator near 3 is fixed by the 95\% confidence level, only additional independent physical trials can tighten the bound.}

  \notecue{Next}{Convert those clean-run counts into physical test-cell hours and operator shifts for the 40 s door cycle.}
}

\begin{frame}{Bounding $10^{-9}$ hazards per hour needs 3.0 billion clean hours}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
  Beyond rare-event exposure, average metrics can also hide destructive first-impact loads. Across 100 door trials, mean contact force is 12 N, below the 15 N tripwire, yet slight misalignments drive the 99th-percentile peak to 115 N, past the 100 N structural limit. In an undamped spring-mass impact, the first peak rises in one quarter oscillation, faster than sensing, inference, and braking can react, so no reactive monitor can clip it. Protection requires passive compliance and a pre-contact speed cap, $v \le F_{\text{limit}} / \sqrt{m_{\text{red}} k}$ (equation 10, from elastic energy balance $\frac{1}{2} m_{\text{red}} v^2 = \frac{1}{2} k x^2$ with $F = k x$), with reduced colliding mass $m_{\text{red}}$ and contact stiffness $k$, the same physics behind ISO/TS 15066's 65 to 140 N transient human-contact limits. Our campaign's twenty peaks cannot fit a tail for exceedance above 100 N, so Lecture 15 introduces extreme-value theory for block maxima (the largest peak in each block, such as each trial).
