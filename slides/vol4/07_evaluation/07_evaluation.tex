  \end{tabular}\\[0.22cm]

  \mlsyscredit{Closed-Loop Evaluation, \S7.2, equation 1 and Definition 7.1 (closed-loop evaluation estimand).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Build Definition 7.1 across five steps: closed-loop success $p(\mathcal{D}) = \mathbb{E}_{c \sim \mathcal{D}}[\phi(\text{Traj}(c))]$ evaluates a frozen pass-fail predicate $\phi$ over trajectories drawn from a declared trial-condition distribution $\mathcal{D}$. Step 2 adds initial state $\mathbf{x}_0$ (pose, speed) and environment $e$ (floor friction, lighting, ambient temperature). Step 3 adds specific hardware unit $m$ (gear backlash, camera mount, battery charge, recalling rig-to-rig divergence from Lecture 5). Step 4 adds the frozen policy $\pi_\theta$ (weights, precision, permission parameters) and protocol $\kappa$ (deadline, goal, predicate). Step 5 lands the core point: changing floor friction, wearing a tire, or depleting the battery changes $p$ without altering a single weight.

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
  Against a stiff $5 \times 10^5$ N/m latch interface, contact force scales with penetration: the 15 N tripwire triggers at 0.03 mm, the 100 N structural ceiling hits at 0.2 mm, and a 2 mm error demands 1,000 N (derived; actual peak depends on compliance and torque clamps). Because mean squared error scores 2 mm in empty air identically to 2 mm into steel, a checkpoint with slightly higher free-space MSE and lower contact error outperforms a lower-MSE rival (rank inversion), while cosine similarity ignores force magnitude and can stall against preload at 0.999 cosine.

\begin{frame}{Each evidence regime trades one blind spot for another}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.9cm,keepaspectratio]{regimes_step}{4}
  \mlsyscredit{Closed-Loop Evaluation, \S7.4.1, figure 2 and table 2, redrawn at slide size.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Build the two-axis diagram across four regimes (causal feedback horizontally, physical realism vertically). First, recorded replay tests compute latency, memory, and numerics on real inputs yet misses closed-loop drift entirely. Second, closed-loop simulation restores feedback across thousands of parallel runs yet tests only the modeled world without real backlash, slip, or contact. Third, shadow operation runs the candidate passively on the live robot beneath a validated baseline, exposing real buses, heat, and sensor noise while suffering trajectory confinement because the baseline prevents seeing candidate-induced drift (used as a release filter in Lecture 16). Fourth, constrained physical testing closes the loop on hardware inside a safety envelope (red dashed), right-censoring outcomes past the boundary.

\begin{frame}{Twenty clean runs still allow one failure in seven}

  {\normalsize\uncover<2->{$p^{20} = \asig$ gives $\plower = 0.05^{1/20} = $ \textbf{0.861}, so failure may reach 13.9 percent.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Closed-Loop Evaluation, \S7.6, figure 3, equation 3 and Napkin Math 7.1; Clopper and Pearson 1934. Derived.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  When $\hat{p} = 1$, the textbook Wald interval $\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$ collapses to zero width; use exact binomial likelihood instead (Clopper and Pearson 1934). First, plot the probability of observing 20 consecutive independent successes, $p^{20}$, across candidate true success probabilities $p$. Second, setting $p^{20} = \alpha_{\text{sig}} = 0.05$ yields $p_{\text{lower}} = 0.05^{1/20} = 0.861$: twenty clean runs rule out true success below 86.1\% at 95\% confidence, leaving true failure rates up to 13.9\% (about one in seven) consistent with the data. For intuition, the orange marker shows a policy failing one in eight runs still logs 20/20 clean runs in 6.9\% of campaigns (derived).

\mlsysnote{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Core}%
  Pause on the governing scaling law of closed-loop testing. Approximating $\ln(1 - p_{\text{fail}}) \approx -p_{\text{fail}}$ in $(1 - p_{\text{fail}})^n = 0.05$ gives $n\,p_{\text{fail}} \approx \ln 20 = 2.996 \approx 3$, yielding the clinical rule of three (Hanley and Lippman-Hand 1983): $n$ clean trials upper-bound failure rate near $3/n$ ($3/20 = 15\%$ versus exact 13.9\%). Inverted, demonstrating target reliability requires $n \approx 3 / (1 - p_{\text{target}})$ clean runs (29 for 90\%, 299 for 99\%, 2,995 for 99.9\%), so every added nine multiplies clean trials tenfold.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Because the numerator near 3 is fixed by the 95\% confidence level, only additional independent physical trials tighten the bound.}

    \end{column}

Three evaluation rules follow: falls require their own estimand, denominator, and confidence bound separate from task completion; disturbance strata (spills, turf, curbs, pushes) must be declared and weighted upfront because polished concrete supports nothing else; and retargeted human demonstrations carry human balance rather than robot dynamics, sending missing recovery regimes into contained testing and the gap catalog.
