  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3.5 min}{Core}%
  Verifying that reach exposes a coordinate mismatch. Clearance and the 1~m/s tool-center-point (TCP) ceiling live in tool space, whereas motors enforce the 2.175~rad/s joint-rate limit. Recall Lecture 5's warning that end-effector commands depend on posture. At $10^\circ$, demand hits 37.8~rad/s as $\det J = L_1 L_2 \sin q_2$ approaches zero at a singularity, where mobility vanishes in one direction. A tempting yes assumes a tool cap bounds joint speeds, yet checks must map back to joint space.}

\begin{frame}{Damping caps the joint step and gives up tool accuracy}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{dls.pdf}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small 50 mm damping (illustrative): 8.3 rad/s at 10°; the tool moves \textbf{21.9\%} of the request.}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Planning, \S11.2.1, equation 2 (Wampler 1986; Nakamura and Hanafusa 1986). Derived.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
  Reversing that map exposes why singularities break solvers. Recall from Lecture 5 that inverse kinematics maps tool motion to joint angles; in velocity form, it inverts $J$ by dividing each direction by its singular value, the ellipse's axis length. As the elbow straightens, exact demand (red) climbs from 4.9 to 37.8~rad/s per 1~m/s. To cap that blow-up, Wampler (1986) and Nakamura and Hanafusa (1986) introduced damped least squares (green), minimizing tool error plus $\lambda^2$ times joint motion, like regularized regression penalizing large weights. With $\lambda = 50\text{ mm}$, gain caps at $1/(2\lambda) = 10\text{ rad/s}$ per m/s, asking 4.6~rad/s (94.3\% realized) at $90^\circ$ and 8.3~rad/s (21.9\%) at $10^\circ$. Because 8.3~rad/s exceeds 2.175~rad/s, time parameterization, setting speed along the path, must slow the tool.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Damping bounds solver step size without guaranteeing feasibility, and its tool lag becomes tracking error, a gap between commanded and actual motion that clearance checks must absorb.}

  \notecue{Teach}{In a 50-minute class, skip this slide and state that damping caps joint steps while sacrificing tool accuracy.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, examine what a standard ROS 2 trajectory message carries and which safety fields it omits.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Every generator still owes the same checks at admission}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.90\textwidth,height=3.15cm,keepaspectratio]{gen_timing.pdf}\\[0.04cm]
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}P{4.2cm}P{4.0cm}P{4.7cm}@{}}\toprule
    \textbf{Family} & \textbf{Work (latency)} & \textbf{Still unproven} \\\midrule
    Sampling (RRT*, MoveIt 2) & collision (200--1,000+ ms) & timing; timeout proves nothing \\
    Optimization, MPC (cuRobo) & solve (30--60 ms GPU) & finishing before deadline \\
    Learned chunks & inference (40 ms here) & limits, seams, and the stop \\\bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \mlsyscredit{Ruckig: Berscheid and Kröger 2021 (table 4); other bars: \S11.1, \S11.3.1 (illustrative).}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Switching the generator changes the compute budget, never admission checks. Sampling planners such as Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM), Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT*), and MoveIt 2 spend 200 to 1,000+~ms in configuration space (joint angles minus collisions), where collision checks dominate and timeouts prove nothing. Optimization and MPC solvers, which re-optimize a short-horizon plan each cycle, include the GPU-parallel optimizer cuRobo (30 to 60~ms); they require a verified stopping state at horizon end (a terminal set) for recursive feasibility, keeping a valid next plan available, and lose guarantees on missed deadlines. Learned chunk policies take 40~ms and enforce no limits, seams, or stops. Ruckig solves seven axes in 19.8~$\mu$s mean and 123~$\mu$s worst case on a desktop CPU (Berscheid and Kröger, 2021), inside a 1~ms tick against 114.9~ms for full replacement.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Satisfying per-axis motion bounds by construction is only kinematic feasibility, not admission, because coupled torques and clearance stay unchecked, and desktop Ruckig timings never transfer to a safety microcontroller.}

}

  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Planning, \S11.6.1, fig.\ 6; Fox et al.\ 1997. Explore: Nonholonomic Base on mlsysbook.ai. Photo: Auledas, CC BY 4.0.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
  Unlike the arm, a wheeled base cannot follow straight lines between poses. Two coaxial wheels steering by speed difference, a differential drive, roll without slipping, creating a nonholonomic constraint that forbids sideways motion. Reaching a lateral offset takes a turn-drive-turn maneuver, so base planners search velocity controls rather than straight paths. Fox et al. (1997) introduced the dynamic window approach for that search, evaluating arcs of speed and turn rate reachable within 50~ms ($\pm 0.1\text{ m/s}$ at 2~m/s$^2$) and keeping arcs passing a constant-deceleration stop test. On 1.10~m clearance, that textbook test admits 2.10~m/s, above the 1.5~m/s drive limit, whereas our full jerk-limited budget fails at 1.5~m/s.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Classical planners in the ROS 2 navigation stack (Nav2), including its modular dynamic-window controller (DWB) and model predictive path integral control (MPPI), which scores sampled control sequences, remain proposers that must pass permission-side admission.}
