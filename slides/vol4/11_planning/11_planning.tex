  \note{[3.5 min] [CORE] Clearance and the tool speed limit live in tool space; the drives enforce joint limits. The manipulator Jacobian $J$ maps joint rates to tool velocity, and it changes with the pose. \begin{frame}{Damping caps the joint step and gives up tool accuracy}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{dls.pdf}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small 50 mm damping (illustrative): 8.3 rad/s at 10°; the tool moves \textbf{21.9\%} of the request.}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Planning, \S11.2.1, equation 2 (Wampler 1986; Nakamura and Hanafusa 1986). Computed in calcs.py.}
  \note{[2 min] Inverse kinematics runs the map backward: find the joint step that moves the tool by a requested amount. The exact inverse divides each tool direction by its singular value, so the weakest direction gets the largest step (red), climbing from 4.9 to 37.8 rad/s per 1 m/s as the elbow straightens. Damped least squares (green) minimizes tool error plus $\lambda^2$ times joint motion; ML students will recognize ridge regression, with $\lambda^2$ as weight decay. Its gain never exceeds $1/(2\lambda)$, 10 rad/s per m/s at $\lambda = 50$ mm. At 90° it asks 4.6 rad/s and still delivers 94.3 percent of the tool motion; at 10° it asks 8.3 rad/s and delivers 21.9 percent. ENGAGE: is 8.3 rad/s feasible? (Expected: no; still above 2.175, so time parameterization must slow the tool.) WARN: damping bounds the step; it does not make it feasible, and the lag it creates is tracking error the planner must carry into its clearance checks. FLEX: [OPTIONAL] Cut if short; say the bold line.}
\end{frame}
