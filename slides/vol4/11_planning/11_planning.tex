\begin{frame}{Predict: does 1 m/s at the tool fit the joints?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{2 min}
  \begin{columns}[c]

      {\footnotesize Manipulator Jacobian $J$: tool velocity $= J \times$ joint rates. Ellipse: tool speeds from unit joint rate.}\\[0.12cm]
      Elbow at 90°: does the weakest direction fit?\\[0.1cm]
      \uncover<2->{\textbf{\textcolor{figveto}{No.}} One joint needs at least \textbf{3.5 rad/s}.\\[0.08cm]}
      \uncover<3->{At 10° elbow: \textbf{\textcolor{figveto}{37.8 rad/s}} (near singularity).}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Planning, \S11.2.1, fig.\ 1; Franka Robotics 2023 (derived). Explore: Manipulability Ellipse on mlsysbook.ai.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3.5 min}{Core}%
  Clearance and the 1~m/s tool-center-point (TCP) ceiling live in tool space, whereas motors enforce the 2.175~rad/s joint-rate limit. The pose-dependent manipulator Jacobian $J$ maps joint rates to tool velocity; its manipulability ellipse shows tool speeds reachable from unit joint-rate norm, wide along easy directions and thin along stiff ones (the same map Lecture 5 used for cross-arm labels, and the same derivative structure autograd builds). In Step 2, at a $90^\circ$ elbow, 1~m/s along the weakest axis requires a joint-rate norm of 4.9~rad/s, so at least one of the two joints exceeds $4.9/\sqrt{2} = 3.5\text{ rad/s}$. In Step 3, straightening to $10^\circ$ demands 37.8~rad/s as $\det J = L_1 L_2 \sin q_2$ approaches zero at singularity.

  \notecue{Check}{Give one minute to predict whether 1~m/s fits at $90^\circ$ before Step 2. The answer is no: a 1~m/s tool cap never bounds joint speeds near thin ellipse axes or singularities, so tool checks must map back to joint space.}

  \notecue{Next}{We next look at damped least squares, which caps joint steps near singularities at the cost of tool accuracy.}
}

\begin{frame}{Damping caps the joint step and gives up tool accuracy}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{dls.pdf}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small 50 mm damping (illustrative): 8.3 rad/s at 10°; the tool moves \textbf{21.9\%} of the request.}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Planning, \S11.2.1, equation 2 (Wampler 1986; Nakamura and Hanafusa 1986). Derived.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
  Inverse kinematics inverts $J$ by dividing each tool direction by its singular value, driving exact joint demand (red) from 4.9 to 37.8~rad/s per 1~m/s as the elbow straightens. Damped least squares (green; Wampler 1986, Nakamura and Hanafusa 1986) minimizes tool error plus $\lambda^2$ times joint motion, matching ridge regression with $\lambda^2$ as weight decay, capping gain at $1/(2\lambda) = 10\text{ rad/s}$ per m/s for $\lambda = 50\text{ mm}$. At $90^\circ$ it asks 4.6~rad/s and delivers 94.3\% of requested motion; at $10^\circ$ it asks 8.3~rad/s and delivers 21.9\%. Even 8.3~rad/s still exceeds the 2.175~rad/s joint ceiling, so time parameterization must slow the tool.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Damping bounds solver step size without guaranteeing feasibility, and the tool lag it introduces becomes tracking error that clearance checks must absorb.}

  \notecue{Optional}{For a 50-minute class, skip this slide and state the bold takeaway that damping caps joint steps while sacrificing tool accuracy.}

  \notecue{Next}{We now examine what a standard ROS 2 trajectory message carries and what safety fields it omits.}
}
\end{frame}
