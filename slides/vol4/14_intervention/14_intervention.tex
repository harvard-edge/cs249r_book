  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  That sixfold timing mismatch raises the lecture's driving question of why handing a moving robot to a person can cause the collision it should prevent. Four segments answer it. First, price takeover travel and solve for allowed speed and deadline. Second, enforce one writer per channel with leases on the safety microcontroller (MCU). Third, size bumpless quintic transfers, smooth command transitions without sudden jumps.   \safeimg[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{propose_admit.pdf}\\[0.02cm]
  {\footnotesize Token: the right to propose on a channel. MCU: the safety microcontroller.}
  \mlsyscredit{Supervisory Intervention, \S14.2, table 1 and Definition 14.1.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Supervision is often conflated with safety enforcement, so examine them side by side. On the left and right, the same 1~kHz safety MCU enforcer from Lectures 4 and 12 stands guard across Lecture 1's proposal boundary. During enforcement, the learned policy keeps proposing while the enforcer alters any unsafe proposal. During intervention, the policy's proposal token is revoked on named channels and granted to an authenticated operator, yet every manual command passes through that same enforcer. Enforcement changes a proposal and leaves the proposer in place, whereas intervention changes the proposer and leaves the check in place. If a startled operator commands a rollover turn, the enforcer clamps or rejects it just like a policy command.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A takeover grants only the right to propose, never direct actuator access. The third law, proposal is not permission, governs people and models alike, and a local stop must act without waiting for the operator.}

\begin{frame}{An in-loop takeover spends 605 ms before any command}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.35cm,keepaspectratio]{strip_step}{3}\\[0.04cm]
  {\small In-loop: hands on controls, expecting the event. $T_\Sigma = t_{\text{perceive}} + t_{\text{decide}} + t_{\text{act}} + t_{\text{console}}$}\\[0.04cm]
  {\footnotesize Wired lab link: 104~ms; warehouse 4G/5G teleop links typically add 80--250~ms round-trip.}
  \mlsyscredit{Supervisory Intervention, \S14.2.2 (eq.~1, figure 2); Card, Moran, and Newell 1983 (slow-bound cycles); Schimpe et al. 2022 (104 ms, wired link).}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Placing every stage from camera to hand controller onto one timeline tests an attentive, in-loop operator against the 100.8~ms (131~mm) spare from recall. First, 104~ms of glass-to-glass (camera-to-screen) video latency measured on a wired teleoperation link (Schimpe et al., 2022) exhausts the 100.8~ms spare before the operator sees the scene. Next comes human reaction. In 1983, Card, Moran, and Newell introduced the Model Human Processor, modeling a simple response as three serial cycles (perceptual, cognitive, and motor), each spanning a range. Taking the slow end so slower normal operators still fit adds 200~ms to perceive, 170~ms to decide, and 100~ms to act (470~ms total). Finally, Segment 2's 31~ms authority-transfer handshake completes at 605~ms; together with video, it forms the 135~ms console term $t_{\text{console}}$. At 1.3~m/s, that wait covers 786.5~mm, or $6.0\times$ the spare (derived), and even an instantaneous network trims at most 135~ms.

  \notecue{Watch for}{The 104~ms video figure came from a wired lab link. Warehouse 4G/5G links typically add 80 to 250~ms round-trip and must be measured on site.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, test whether Lecture 12's aisle stopping budget can absorb this 605~ms takeover delay.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The deadline is the same budget solved for the wait}

  \mlsyssteps[width=0.88\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{deadline_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small $T_{\text{deadline,fallback}} = \dfrac{D_{\text{clear}}-\delta-d_{\text{suffix}}(v)}{v}-\tau_{\text{delay}}$\quad Configured timeouts are clipped to it.}

  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%

  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Lufthansa Flight 2904 shows that reverse failure. In Warsaw in 1993, as documented in the 1994 accident report, the airliner landed fast down a waterlogged runway with an unreported tailwind. The pilots immediately commanded brakes and thrust reversers, yet interlock logic (a safeguard blocking braking devices until touchdown sensors confirm landing) required both main gear struts compressed or wheels spun up. Because the left strut stayed uncompressed for 9~s and the wheels slid on standing water (aquaplaned), spoilers and reversers stayed locked out for 9~s, and wheel brakes stayed blocked for about 13~s. The aircraft overran at 72 knots and burned, killing two people. The report cites interlock design alongside crew actions without claiming an override alone would have prevented the overrun; a faulty robot wheel-slip or docking sensor can similarly block braking.
