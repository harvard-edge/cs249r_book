\begin{frame}{Spirit drove onto soil that looked like ground it had crossed}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.4cm,keepaspectratio]{spirit-troy-crust.jpg}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \small
      \textbf{Troy, Mars, April 2009.} Spirit's front hazard camera took this view.\\[0.3cm]
      The bright soil is loose sand. A wheel broke through the darker crust above it.\\[0.3cm]
      \uncover<2->{The cameras saw the surface. Nothing on the rover measured the soil's strength.\\[0.3cm]
      \textcolor{RedLine}{\textbf{Spirit never left. In January 2010 it became a stationary platform.}}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: NASA/JPL-Caltech, public domain, Wikimedia Commons (``Spirit's Robotic Stretch''). Story: Spatial Memory, War Story 9.1; Arvidson et al., JGR Planets 2010.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  A robot can hold a well-formed belief about the ground ahead and still fail when its sensors never measure the property that supports motion. In April 2009, at a Martian site west of Home Plate that the team named Troy, NASA's rover Spirit (previewed in Lecture 0) had driven backward for three years while dragging an inoperative right-front wheel. On a dark crust matching earlier ground, its left-front wheel broke through into bright, loose sand. Cameras record surface appearance rather than soil shear strength, the soil's internal resistance to shearing under a wheel's load, so no reading contradicted the terrain belief until the wheels sank. After months of failed extrication attempts, NASA declared Spirit a stationary platform in January 2010.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Do not treat Troy as a camera malfunction. The optics worked as designed while supporting a belief about soil shear strength, a property no sensor observed. The wrap-up returns to Troy alongside the warehouse case.}

  \notecue{Next}{Shift from an unobserved property to hidden (occluded) space and predict how long one empty camera frame certifies a warehouse cross aisle.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A filter predicts, then each update narrows its spread}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.60\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{kalman_step}{3}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.37\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=2.2cm,keepaspectratio]{ouster-os1-pointcloud.jpg}\\[0.1cm]
      {\footnotesize Ouster OS1-64 lidar: 30 mm range precision (datasheet).\\Stereo at 1.10 m: 3.8 mm.\\[0.15cm]
      \textbf{Gain}: how far the estimate moves toward a reading, between 0 and 1.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Point cloud: Daniel L. Lu, CC BY 4.0, Wikimedia Commons. Numbers: Spatial Memory, \S9.2 (odometry disturbance 0.1 m/s is illustrative). Bars: $\pm$1 standard deviation; gray bars are the sensors' own spreads.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Formulated by Kalman in 1960 and carried by MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory into Apollo lunar navigation, the Kalman filter keeps only the latest estimate and covariance rather than a full log. On the rack-end range ($\pm 1\sigma$, one standard deviation), the prior (starting belief) sits at $\pm 20$ mm. Predicting across one 50 ms lidar sweep under an illustrative 0.1 m/s odometry (self-motion) disturbance adds 5 mm in quadrature (root sum of squares), widening spread to 20.6 mm. Each scalar update sets gain $K=\sigma_{\text{pred}}^2/(\sigma_{\text{pred}}^2+\sigma_{\text{meas}}^2)$ and scales variance by $(1-K)$, weighting sharper readings higher while setting both from modeled spreads alone, independent of the measured value. Lecture 8's Ouster OS1-64 lidar (30 mm datasheet precision) gives gain 0.32 and 17.0 mm spread. Active stereo depth at 1.10 m (3.8 mm precision) earns gain 0.95 and 3.7 mm spread; doubling stereo noise to 7.6 mm leaves gain 0.83 and spread 6.9 mm.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A zero-mean Gaussian filter cannot remove the Ouster's 25 mm datasheet accuracy bias; systematic bias (fixed offset) must be calibrated out before fusion.}

  \notecue{Next}{See what happens to that 3.7 mm filter when the stereo update arrives stamped at host arrival instead of capture.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A misdated update moves the estimate and keeps the spread}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{kalman_step4.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.39\textwidth}
      \small
      Stamped on arrival, the stereo frame is paired with a pose 39.9 mm farther along the aisle.\\[0.25cm]
      \uncover<2->{The filter reports $\pm$3.7 mm and sits 38.0 mm off.\\[0.25cm]
      \textbf{Fix:} apply the reading at its capture epoch. A 52-slot buffer covers 51.6 ms at 1 kHz.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Spatial Memory, \S9.2 and \S9.2.3. 39.9 mm arrival-stamp travel: the observation contract (Lecture 8). 38.0 mm and 52 slots (derived).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  That high 0.95 stereo gain makes the filter acutely sensitive to timing errors. Applying Lecture 8's 39.9 mm arrival-stamp slip inside the update pairs the stereo frame with a pose 39.9 mm farther along the aisle, pulling the range estimate 38.0 mm off truth. Because the covariance formula depends only on modeled spreads rather than the actual residual (the gap between reading and prediction), the filter still reports $\pm 3.7$ mm. The estimate now sits about ten reported standard deviations from truth, whereas dropping the frame would leave an honest $\pm 17.0$ mm. Out-of-sequence replay prevents this error by buffering 52 ticks of 1 kHz predictions to cover 51.6 ms, rolling back to the capture epoch to apply the update, replaying inertial measurement unit (IMU) inputs forward, and rejecting any reading older than the buffer.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Timestamp errors are not minor software blemishes. On a moving robot they turn directly into silent 10$\sigma$ spatial biases.}

  \notecue{Next}{Predict whether a single-step residual test catches that misdated update reliably.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Does a residual test catch a misdated update?}
  \mlsysactivity{Vote}{3 min}
  \small
  \textbf{Innovation}: reading minus prediction. \textbf{NIS}: innovation$^2$ over predicted variance (mean 1 when honest).\\[0.06cm]
  Misdated update raises expected NIS to 6.25; test rejects NIS $>$ 3.84.\\[0.04cm]
  {\normalsize\choice{A}{Always caught}\qquad\choice{B}{About 2 in 3}\qquad\choice{C}{Never}}\\[0.04cm]
  \uncover<2->{\centering\safeimg[width=0.86\textwidth,height=2.65cm,keepaspectratio]{gates.pdf}\\}
  \uncover<3->{\footnotesize Over 50 innovations the mean NIS sits near 6.25, far above the honest 0.65 to 1.43.}
  \mlsyscredit{Spatial Memory, \S9.2.1 (NIS, NEES, chi-square tests; Bar-Shalom et al. 2001). Probabilities from the noncentral chi-square distribution (derived).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Because covariance misses misdating, online audits check residuals. Normalized innovation squared (NIS) divides squared innovation by predicted variance (prior plus sensor variance). One normalized unbiased Gaussian residual squared (a chi-square variable) averages 1 with 95\% threshold 3.84 (nearly two spreads squared). The misdated offset raises expected NIS to 6.25; predict how often 3.84 catches it.

  \notecue{Check}{The answer is B, about 2 in 3. Option A is tempting because 6.25 exceeds 3.84, yet biased residuals still fluctuate randomly (a noncentral chi-square distribution). When noise opposes the offset, draws fall below threshold, catching only 63\% at 3.84 (95\%) and 39\% at 6.63 (99\%), so over one third pass. Averaging 50 independent innovations separates honest filters (95\% band 0.65 to 1.43) from persistent misdating near 6.25. Offline against ground truth, normalized estimation error squared (NEES) checks state error against covariance; online, NIS checks innovations (Bar-Shalom et al. 2001).}

  \notecue{Watch for}{Filters can absorb steady errors and quiet NIS, so residual tests never replace capture-epoch validation.}

  \notecue{Teach}{Vote, discuss 30 seconds in pairs, and revote before showing the bars and 50-sample band.}

  \notecue{Next}{Check how fast an uncalibrated IMU drifts between external range fixes.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Over one second, the IMU's bias outgrows its noise}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.2cm,keepaspectratio]{imu.pdf}\\[0.15cm]
  \small Bosch BMI088, uncalibrated: gyro bias 1$^\circ$/s, accelerometer offset 20 mg (datasheet). Bias grows as $t$ (gyro) and $t^2$ (accel.\ position); noise as $\sqrt{t}$ and $t^{3/2}$.
  \mlsyscredit{Bosch Sensortec BMI088 data sheet, rev.\ 1.9 (2024), via Spatial Memory, \S9.2. Errors after 1 s (derived).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
  Between external range fixes, the filter coasts on Lecture 2's Bosch BMI088 IMU, whose datasheet figures show why inertial prediction bridges only short gaps. Over 1 second, uncorrelated random (white) gyroscope noise grows with the square root of time to 0.014$^\circ$ of heading spread, whereas its uncalibrated $1^\circ$/s bias grows linearly to $1^\circ$, shifting a point at 1.10 m sideways by 19.2 mm. Because acceleration integrates twice into position, accelerometer white noise grows faster than a square root (with time to the three-halves power, reaching 0.9 mm at 1 second), whereas its 20 mg offset (thousandths of Earth gravity) grows quadratically (half acceleration times time squared), reaching 98.1 mm at 1 second (nearly twice Lecture 2's 50 mm localization allowance) and quadrupling whenever coasting time doubles. Estimating offsets online helps only while motion makes them observable (distinguishable from true motion, Section 5).

  \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip this IMU breakdown and state in one sentence that inertial prediction alone drifts rapidly without periodic external fixes.}

  \notecue{Next}{Move from one static upright to dynamic obstacles and see why a multi-object tracker's confirmation delay must never hold up occupancy checks.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A tracker's identity must not delay occupancy}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.97\textwidth}
      {\footnotesize\ttfamily\bfseries
      def \_\_init\_\_(self, \textcolor{BlueLine}{max\_age=1, min\_hits=3}, ...):\\
      \hspace*{1em}...\\
      \hspace*{1em}if (...) and (\textcolor{BlueLine}{trk.hit\_streak >= self.min\_hits} or ...):\\
      \hspace*{2em}ret.append(...)\hspace*{1em}\textcolor{black!60}{\# only confirmed, currently seen tracks}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \centering\safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=3.3cm,keepaspectratio]{sort_timeline.pdf}
  \mlsyscredit{Code: abewley/sort, sort.py @ 2236dff (GPL-3.0), shortened; Bewley et al., ICIP 2016. Timeline: illustrative frames. Spatial Memory, \S9.2.2.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  In an active aisle, multiple moving people need separate filters and consistent identities across frames. In 2016, Bewley and colleagues published Simple Online and Realtime Tracking (SORT), a standard multi-object tracker baseline; its \texttt{abewley/sort} defaults (\texttt{min\_hits=3}, \texttt{max\_age=1}) withhold a new track until three consecutive frames match and drop output once occluded. When a worker enters view on frame 1, SORT withholds frames 1 and 2; at 60 Hz, that two-frame delay costs 33.3 ms, or 50.0 mm at 1.5 m/s, and SORT drops occluded frame 5. Planners using tracker velocity must charge that delay to their budget. Architecturally, raw occupied geometry enters permission checks on frame 1, occluded space reverts to unknown (treated as occupied), and tracking supplies identity and velocity only for planning.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Deleting a coasting track (one predicted forward without fresh detections) never clears physical space. Across Section 2, test every covariance claim, apply updates at capture epoch, and send raw occupancy ahead of track identity.}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Standard robotics messages defeat evidence-freshness checks by collapsing distinct times into one field. ROS 2's \texttt{PoseWithCovarianceStamped} carries one \texttt{stamp}, a \texttt{frame\_id}, a pose, and a 36-element covariance. Separating three instants shows what is missing, distinguishing the acquisition or evidence epoch (when photons hit the sensor), the estimate epoch (the time predicted to), and the publication epoch (when sent). Collapsing them lets a republished prediction disguise stale evidence as fresh. The belief record, seventh in Lecture 1's record chain, adds an evidence epoch, an error-growth law, a validity region (the operating conditions where that law holds), and an expiry. Small clock slips cost real distance; Lecture 8's 0.2 ms clock-conversion bound costs 0.3 mm at 1.5 m/s, and a 2.5 ms camera-encoder timing skew costs 4.25 mm at 1.7 m/s.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Softmax confidence (a classifier's probability-like score from Lecture 3) specifies neither millimeters nor error growth over time.}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  To enforce physical deadlines without trusting publishers, the belief record tracks four lifecycle states. It stays VALID while actively measured, enters DEGRADED once occluded as uncertainty grows under its declared error law, and turns EXPIRED upon reaching tolerance or leaving its validity region. It returns to VALID only through fresh observations passing innovation checks, never through prediction or republishing, while UNOBSERVABLE (labeled rank-deficient because measurements over time cannot uniquely separate distinct physical states, examined next) requires restoring physical observability. In Napkin Math 9.3, for a mug on a 0.20 m/s conveyor with 3 mm initial error and 15 mm tolerance, 35 ms elapsed plus Lecture 8's 0.2 ms clock-conversion bound (35.2 ms) gives $3\text{ mm} + 0.20\text{ mm/ms} \times 35.2\text{ ms} = 10.04\text{ mm} \le 15\text{ mm}$. That leaves 4.96 mm and 24.8 ms before expiry at 60 ms, with 59.8 ms latest use age, matching Lecture 2's 60 ms deadline.
