\begin{frame}{Can a neural network delay a safety check that runs on its own core?}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.86\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{tesla-fsd-board.jpg}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Two systems-on-chip (SoCs), each with its own memory, on one board supply.}
  \mlsyscredit{Tesla FSD computer board. Photo: Votpuske, CC BY 4.0, Wikimedia Commons.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  A dedicated core looks separate on a software diagram, yet real hardware shares resources underneath. Tesla's Full Self-Driving (FSD) computer, a production automotive board, places two system-on-chip (SoC) packages (chips combining processors and controllers on one die) beside separate dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) chips, yet both draw from shared board power supplies and connectors. The warehouse mobile manipulator makes a similar split between its learned proposer and Lecture 12's 1~kHz safety check. If that check runs on its own core, can the neural network still make it miss its deadline? Saying no is tempting because cores and software look separate, yet shared silicon and board hardware can still couple their timing.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Equating a separate core with an independent failure domain, where faults cannot reach the other path, misses physical couplings absent from software diagrams.}

  \notecue{Teach}{Ask for a show of hands on the title question before moving on, without naming the couplings yet.}

\begin{frame}{Predict: when is the tick on time after inference stops?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{2 min}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.05cm,keepaspectratio]{hook_step}{3}\\[0.0cm]
  {\normalsize\choice{A}{at once}\qquad\choice{B}{next tick}\qquad\answerat{3}{C}{about 2 s later}\qquad\choice{D}{never}}
  \mlsyscredit{Workload Placement, Napkin Math 13.2 (illustrative: 45\,°C bay, 2 K/W, 8 s, 26 W loaded, trip 95\,°C, clear 85\,°C). Tick on a cluster that shares the SoC's clock governor.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  To test that coupling, place the 1~kHz permission tick hypothetically on an application-SoC cluster sharing the die's clock governor, which slows clocks with heat (Napkin Math 13.2, illustrative). Under load, the silicon junction (active transistor layer) heats from 53 toward $97^\circ\text{C}$ with thermal time constant $\tau = 8\text{ s}$. Nominally, 250~$\mu$s of tick execution (300,000 cycles at 1.2~GHz) plus 90~$\mu$s of local overhead (held constant as a lower bound) finish at 340~$\mu$s inside the 400~$\mu$s deadline. At 24.7~s, temperature hits $95^\circ\text{C}$, halving the clock to 600~MHz, doubling execution to 500~$\mu$s, and pushing completion to at least 590~$\mu$s (190~$\mu$s, or 47.5\%, late). If inference stops at the trip, predict when the tick returns on time.

\begin{frame}{The proposer's weight stream is the long burst ahead of the read}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.80\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{socmem.pdf}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small $\text{wait} = \dfrac{\text{bytes queued ahead}}{\text{peak bandwidth}} + \text{row conflicts}\times(t_{\text{precharge}}+t_{\text{activate}})$}\\[0.05cm]
  {\footnotesize\textcolor{midgray}{One backlog (weight tile, camera, telemetry): 360,448\,B at 204\,GB/s + 3 $\times$ 37.5\,ns = \textbf{1.879\,µs}}}
  \mlsyscredit{Workload Placement, \S13.3.1, equation 1 and Napkin Math 13.1 (LPDDR5 at 6,400 MT/s; backlog illustrative).}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Recall from Lecture 3 that neural inference streams model weights against the memory wall. On unified SoC memory, the CPU cluster, the proposer's neural accelerator (labeled neural processing unit, or NPU), and real-time cores all share one interconnect fabric, last-level cache, and low-power DRAM (LPDDR5) controller. That places the safety tick's 4~KB sensor read behind long weight bursts. Every request passes through issue, interconnect arbitration (where hardware selects which requester gets the bus), controller queueing, and DRAM banks. The SoC's LPDDR5 memory runs at 6,400~MT/s (megatransfers per second per pin). Switching an open DRAM row requires a precharge and an activate, each at an 18~ns minimum rounded up to 15 cycles of the 1.25~ns command clock, or 37.5~ns per row conflict. Under the FIFO wait formula, one illustrative backlog combines a 256~KB weight tile split into 64 transactions, a camera transfer, and a telemetry record. Serving those 360,448~B at 204~GB/s plus three 37.5~ns conflicts adds 1.879~$\mu$s (derived), showing how low average utilization hides bursts queued ahead.

  \notecue{Next}{The next slide counts how many queued backlogs push the tick past its 250~$\mu$s WCET and 310~$\mu$s execution limit.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{61 queued backlogs reach the declared bound}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.05cm,keepaspectratio]{backlog_step}{5}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<4->{\small Between 61 and 93 backlogs the tick is \textbf{on time} yet \textbf{refutes} the placement.}
  \mlsyscredit{Workload Placement, Napkin Math 13.1, fig.\ 2 (derived); Explore: Loaded Tick on mlsysbook.ai.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3.5 min}{Core}%
  Stacking that queue wait and the 0.02~$\mu$s DRAM snapshot transfer onto the unloaded 135~$\mu$s tick (40~$\mu$s sense read plus 95~$\mu$s for evaluation and staging) reveals three thresholds. At full interleaved bandwidth, 21 backlogs fill the 40~$\mu$s sense allowance, ending at 174~$\mu$s. Reaching 61 backlogs spends the 115~$\mu$s before the 250~$\mu$s declared WCET, while 93 backlogs spend 175~$\mu$s to hit the 310~$\mu$s ceiling left after the 90~$\mu$s reserve. Entering that 60~$\mu$s slack band between 61 and 93 backlogs still meets the deadline yet refutes the placement's zero-interference premise. That wide margin depends on interleaving. When traffic lands on one of 16 channels, each backlog takes 28.4~$\mu$s and 4 reach 249~$\mu$s, making address mapping (how physical addresses spread across channels) a major hazard.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A real first-ready, first-come-first-served (FR-FCFS) DRAM controller prioritizes row hits and can repeatedly bypass the safety read, making this FIFO model optimistic.}

  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Relocating onto a discrete MCU keeps the proposer off the tick's memory bus, yet the two chips still exchange proposals across a mailbox. Recall from Lecture 4 that unlike Linux kernel v6.12's unbounded \texttt{do ... while} seqlock retry loop, our enforcer makes one attempt per 1~ms tick, discarding any record with an odd or changed sequence counter and holding the active chunk while its lease and bounds hold, entering Lecture 12's fallback ladder on expiry or violation. Placement splits cross-chip exchange into two separately timed obligations. First, an ingress peripheral or DMA engine moves the frame across the board link into local memory; second, local mailbox publication exposes that immutable record to the tick without waiting on host stalls. The boundary protocol governs only the second stage, while qualification must bound both.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Placement also requires memory attributes enforcing cache coherency (consistent reads across shared-memory buffers such as Bao's 64~KB region or DMA into cached MCU memory) and a worst-case payload copy bounded inside WCET.}

  \notecue{Next}{While the mailbox shields the tick from a hung proposer, a windowed watchdog checks that the enforcer itself stays alive and on schedule.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A windowed watchdog trips on early feeds as well as late ones}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.90\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{watchdog_step}{3}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Early feed: skipped checks or a fast clock.\enspace Late feed: a stalled enforcer.}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<3->{\footnotesize Machine's late trip: 5\,ms, \textbf{6.5\,mm} of travel at 1.3\,m/s before a dead enforcer is detected.}
  \mlsyscredit{Workload Placement, \S13.5.2 and equation 5. Barr 2013 (Bookout v.\ Toyota). Derived.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Lecture 4's self-watchdog catches only late feeds when the enforcer stalls. A windowed watchdog adds an early threshold rejecting feeds arriving too soon, catching skipped checks, corrupted control flow, or a runaway clock. Its valid service window depends on the tick period, period jitter, and the spread between earliest and latest loaded completion (early-exit paths finish faster than 135~$\mu$s), while the machine's 5~ms late trip allows 6.5~mm of travel at 1.3~m/s (derived). However, a windowed watchdog verifies cadence rather than task completion, so a periodic timer interrupt still feeds inside the window after the control task dies (the same timer-interrupt failure mode seen on a standard watchdog in Bookout v. Toyota, expert testimony by Barr, 2013, developed in Lecture 15). Catching that fault requires challenge-response checks, where an external supervisor verifies a signature built from fresh tick checks.

  \notecue{Watch for}{If the MCU itself dies, the drives' certified hardware stop must still act without MCU power or control.}

  \notecue{Next}{Section 4 priced each remedy and guarded the cross-chip boundary; Section 5 next defines the loaded test that can refute a placement.}
}
\end{frame}

\mlsysdivider{What measurement could prove the placement wrong?}{Workload Placement, \S13.7 and \S13.8}
\section{Refutation}

\mlsysnote{
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  This inequality is the lecture's central falsification rule. Loaded execution time equals isolated time plus shared-hardware interference, $\Tload = \Tiso + \Dint$, where isolated time stays within declared WCET below the deadline, $\Tiso \le \Twcet < \Tdead$. Because an independent placement claims $\Dint = 0$, any loaded tick above $\Twcet$, such as 260~$\mu$s (350~$\mu$s with the 90~$\mu$s local reserve, still 50~$\mu$s inside the 400~$\mu$s deadline), refutes the placement, since every downstream stopping margin assumed $\Dint = 0$. Finishing below 250~$\mu$s never proves $\Dint = 0$ universally; it supports the claim only across tested states.

  \notecue{Watch for}{One slipped tick adds 1.3~mm at 1.3~m/s, which can look small; once the isolation premise breaks, nothing bounds the next stall.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, apply this falsification rule to a 300~$\mu$s loaded cycle on a shared-die candidate.}
}
