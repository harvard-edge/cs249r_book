  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  The two latch policies represent just two rows of a five-regime trade space. Demonstrations supply expert actions, logged rewards stitch better segments from past logs, simulation offers cheap parallel rollouts (full trials), physical trials test the real plant, and pretrained bases supply broad visual and motor competence. Each source also leaves a silent dependency, namely recovery coverage, logged action support, contact-solver fidelity (how accurately the simulator models contact and friction forces, explaining Dactyl), calendar days and drivetrain wear, and someone else's robots and scenes. No training loss or cumulative-reward return curve reports those dependencies, because model weights keep what earned reward without recording which experience produced it.

\begin{frame}{Squared error sends the policy between two valid routes}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
      \centering
      \mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{mode_avg_step}{3}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.41\textwidth}
      \small
      Half the demos pass left, half pass right.\\[0.3cm]
      \uncover<3->{The squared-error optimum is the mean:\\[0.15cm]
      {\normalsize $\pi^*(o) = \mathbb{E}[a \mid o]$}\\[0.15cm]
      $[v, +\omega]$ and $[v, -\omega]$ average to $[v, 0]$, straight ahead.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Policy Training, \S6.2.3, equation 3 and Definition 6.1 (schematic).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Suppose a mobile base approaches a warehouse column where half the recorded demonstrations steer left with $[v, +\omega]$ and half steer right with $[v, -\omega]$, making the dataset 100\% collision-free. Under mean squared error, a deterministic regressor (a model emitting one point action) minimizes loss at the conditional expectation $\pi^*(o) = \mathbb{E}[a \mid o] = [v, 0]$, steering straight into the column along the one route zero demonstrators chose. Definition 6.1 names this failure mode averaging. More data, larger networks, and lower held-out squared error pull predictions closer to that unsafe mean. Fixing it requires a multimodal output head, such as a mixture of distributions or an iterative generative head like diffusion or flow matching that refines noise into one coherent action.

  \notecue{Watch for}{In image regression, averaging causes a harmless blur. Across Lecture 1's causal boundary, averaging valid robot motions causes a collision, a hazard Lecture 3 previewed for temporal ensembling across overlapping chunks.}

  \notecue{Next}{See how iterative denoising commits to one valid route on a real manipulation benchmark.}
}
\end{frame}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  To see multimodal decoding in practice, recall Diffusion Policy from Lecture 3's chunk-horizon examples (Chi et al. 2023/2025), tested across 40 rollouts per method on Push-T, a block-pushing benchmark where valid demonstrations pass left or right around a block. Diffusion Policy starts from random noise and removes noise step by step (iterative denoising) to commit cleanly to left or right on each rollout while covering both sides across trials. \begin{frame}{A 16-step diffusion head leaves 2 ms of its 50 ms renewal}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=3.95cm,keepaspectratio]{decoder_step}{3}\\[0.06cm]
  {\small $t_{\text{infer}} = t_{\text{encode}} + N \cdot t_{\text{eval}} = 8 + 16 \times 2.5 = 48$ ms; renewal slack against jitter decides.}\\[0.04cm]
  {\footnotesize\textcolor{midgray}{Published samplers: Diffusion Policy DDIM uses 10--16 steps; $\pi_0$ uses 10 flow steps.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Policy Training, Napkin Math 6.1, eq.\ 4 (illustrative; ACT 40 ms p99); Chi et al.\ 2023; Black et al.\ 2024. Derived.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  An expressive multimodal decoder helps only if it finishes before the next command is due. Napkin Math 6.1 tests three decoders against two ceilings, the 25 ms setpoint step for synchronous supply and the 50 ms chunk renewal period beside an active prefix. Single-pass ACT finishes in 40 ms at p99 (99th percentile), missing 25 ms and running asynchronously with 10 ms of renewal slack. An illustrative 16-step denoising diffusion implicit model (DDIM) head spends 8 ms encoding images plus $16 \times 2.5 = 40$ ms in sequential evaluations, totaling 48 ms with 2 ms of slack, while an illustrative 4-step flow-matching head (few-step generative decoder) at that evaluation cost takes 18 ms and clears 25 ms. Adding the 5 ms timing-jitter allowance (arrival-time variation from Lecture 4's lease rule) pushes 16-step DDIM to 53 ms, or 3 ms late, while ACT finishes at 45 ms inside renewal.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Meeting deadlines reliably depends on slack against tail jitter rather than mean latency. When a decoder misses renewal silently, Lecture 4's chunk lease caps stale motion.}

  \notecue{Next}{Invert that latency formula to find the largest step count each deadline allows, with jitter reserved on renewal only.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{At 40 Hz, a discount of 0.99 sees only the last 2.5 s}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{discount.pdf}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Return $G_t = \sum_j \gamma^j r_{t+j}$ discounts once per decision, so its horizon is counted in steps.}
  \mlsyscredit{Policy Training, \S6.2.1 ($\gamma = 0.99$, 25 ms steps, 8 s opening). Derived.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
  Even a balanced reward fails if the optimizer barely sees the terminal payout from step 0. Return $G_t = \sum_j \gamma^j r_{t+j}$ multiplies by the discount factor $\gamma$ at each decision step, so the effective horizon $1/(1-\gamma)$ counts control ticks rather than seconds. At $\gamma = 0.99$, that horizon is 100 steps, spanning only 2.5 s on a 40 Hz (25 ms) controller. A door-open reward arriving 8 s (320 steps) later therefore keeps only $0.99^{320} = 0.04$ of its weight at step 0 (red curve). Keeping half weight across 8 s requires $\gamma \ge 0.5^{1/320} = 0.9978$ (blue curve, derived), and doubling the rate to 80 Hz at $\gamma = 0.99$ halves the physical horizon to 1.25 s.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Copying a default $\gamma$ from a 10 Hz benchmark onto a 40 Hz or 80 Hz robot shrinks the physical planning horizon without warning.}

\begin{frame}{Value and advantage score one action against the usual one}

  \begin{columns}[T]

      \begin{mlsyscard}{propblue}\footnotesize
        \textbf{ML:} $Q$ is expected return after $a$; $A > 0$ beats the policy's usual choice.

    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{mlsyscard}{nervgreen}\footnotesize
        \textbf{Control:} with cost $\ell = -r$, $V$ is negated cost-to-go (LQR in closed form).
      \end{mlsyscard}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Policy Training, \S6.2.1, equation 2; Bellman 1957; Appendix: Control (LQR and Riccati equations).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
  To turn discounted return into a per-action decision rule, RL borrows Bellman's 1957 dynamic-programming recursion (Equation 2) from optimal control, the discipline of choosing inputs that minimize cumulative cost over time. Action value $Q^\pi(s,a)$ adds immediate reward to discounted expected future value. State value $V^\pi(s)$ averages $Q^\pi$ under policy $\pi$, and advantage $A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)$ tests whether action $a$ beats the policy's usual choice, so a fast plunge has positive advantage only when weights prize speed over force. In control, $-V$ is cost-to-go; for linear dynamics and quadratic cost, a linear-quadratic regulator (LQR) solves that recursion in closed form via its algebraic Riccati matrix equation (control appendix), whereas RL estimates $Q$ and $V$ from samples when dynamics are unknown.

  \notecue{Teach}{In a 50-minute class or with a cohort comfortable with RL basics, skip this Bellman bridge, note that RL value functions match negated control cost-to-go, and move to offline RL.}

  \notecue{Next}{See why maximizing a fitted $Q$-function over a fixed log selects unsupported actions the log never tried.}

\begin{frame}{Greedy offline learning trusts actions the log never tried}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{offline_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Offline RL fits a fixed log.\enspace\uncover<2->{A conservative fit lowers values off it but adds no support.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Policy Training, \S6.2.7; Kumar et al., Conservative Q-Learning (CQL), NeurIPS 2020 (schematic).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Real logs mix clean runs with imperfect ones, so offline RL fits an action-value function $Q(s,a)$ (expected return after taking action $a$ in state $s$) over a fixed reward-labeled log and picks $\arg\max_a Q(s,a)$ to stitch together high-reward segments. Outside the logged actions (green ticks), however, the network has zero supervision and invents optimistic peaks on the red curve. Taking $\arg\max$ selects those peaks and feeds upward bias backward through recursive Bellman updates. Conservative Q-Learning (CQL, Kumar et al. 2020, blue curve) counters this by pushing predicted values down across the action space while pulling logged actions up, nudging choices toward logged support without guaranteeing physical safety or supplying unlogged states such as a displaced strike plate.

  \notecue{Watch for}{CQL establishes a lower bound on expected value under theoretical assumptions. It never bounds a single physical action or replaces runtime limits.}
