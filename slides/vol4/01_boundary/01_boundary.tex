  \note{%

  Moving mass strips away three conveniences that two digital regimes take for granted across time, recovery, and interface. An advisory classifier runs on servers where time waits, a wrong label can be discarded before any machine moves, and the output is a score. A software agent closes a loop, yet an isolated software sandbox can pause while the model thinks for 200 ms or 30 s, roll back failed transactions, and check structured software calls. Kinetic energy and Joule heat (electrical energy dissipated as heat in motor windings) cannot be rolled back, and the output becomes winding current.   \mlsyscredit{The Causal Boundary, \S1.4.4 and the first law (\S1.10). Photo: Martin Brown, public domain (NARA), Wikimedia Commons.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Once current drives torque across the causal boundary, recovery no longer works like software undo. An aborted database transaction rolls cleanly back to its initial state $S_0$, which is why retry works in software. On a moving machine, applied torque carries the plant from $S_0$ to $S_1$, and braking brings it to a new state $S_2$ with heated windings, friction loss, and possibly a deformed fixture or broken gear tooth. Recovery is always a new forward motion bounded by actuator torque, friction, and speed limits. The permission path must therefore check, before admitting an action, that a feasible recovery will still exist from the resulting state. The governing physical margin depends on the task, whether stopping clearance for the base, contact force for an arm, or winding heat for a held load.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Driving a joint back to its initial coordinates does not reverse heat dissipation, mechanical wear, or plastic deformation (permanent shape change).}

  \notecue{Next}{Test your understanding of the causal boundary with three true-or-false statements.}
}

\section{Budget}

\begin{frame}{Stopping takes travel during the delay plus braking}
  \centering
  {\normalsize $\StoppingDistance = \underbrace{\SpeedAtDetection\PrebrakeDelay}_{\text{travel during delay}} + \underbrace{\dfrac{\SpeedAtDetection^2}{2\CredibleBrakingDeceleration}}_{\text{travel while braking}} \le \ClearDistance$}\qquad

\begin{frame}{Each relabeling discarded the evidence of motion}
  \centering
  \vspace{-0.2cm}
  \mlsyssteps[width=5.45in,height=1.92in,keepaspectratio]{tempe_track_step}{2}\\[0.0cm]
  {\footnotesize Red lines: class changed; earlier locations dropped.}
  \mlsyscredit{NTSB HAR-19/03, table of selected parameters recorded by the ADS (pp.~15--16) and \S1.5.6.1 (quotation, p.~17). The Causal Boundary, \S1.8.1.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Section 2 priced Tempe's final second of action suppression, yet the vehicle first detected the pedestrian at 5.6 s. The automated driving system's recorded log (NTSB HAR-19/03, pp. 15 to 16) explains why emergency recognition came so late. Radar first classified her as a vehicle at 5.6 s, and lidar then alternated among other, vehicle, and bicycle across the red transition lines. Whenever the label changed, the software treated her as a new object and omitted her prior locations from its path predictor. Without position history, the predictor repeatedly assumed a stationary object or travel in an adjacent lane, placing her in the SUV's lane only at 1.2 s, when action suppression began. Because velocity comes from successive positions, dropping track history destroyed motion evidence even though every component ran as designed.

  \notecue{Watch for}{The NTSB report notes that prior locations were omitted on class changes without specifying a Kalman-filter reset (a tracking filter developed in Lecture 9), so do not claim that detail. The design also never accounted for pedestrians crossing outside crosswalks.}

  \notecue{Next}{Map all four NTSB findings onto the five-level machine architecture.}
}

  \mlsyscredit{The Causal Boundary, \S1.9 (illustrative friction pair; the card's 2 m/s$^2$ assumes at least 0.204) and \S1.8 (Quinn Emanuel 2024). Photo: Dietmar Rabich, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  In pure software, safety, security, and accountability usually sit in separate layers; on a robot, all three meet at the permission path because every threat becomes motion. A hydraulic-fluid slick invisible to cameras, dropping friction from 0.60 to 0.05 (illustrative), multiplies traction-limited braking twelvefold, breaking the card's 2 m/s$^2$ premise (friction coefficient $\mu \ge 0.204$). A forged (spoofed) bus packet injects false data, and a policy hallucinates a clear aisle with high confidence. Admission must therefore verify physical feasibility now, demanding three unified requirements. It needs fresh, tamper-checked sensor readings (authenticated telemetry) from wheel slip, current, and an inertial measurement unit (IMU) tracking acceleration and rotation. It needs deterministic refusal with a feasible fallback, unlike the October 2023 Cruise robotaxi that stopped after striking a pedestrian, then attempted a pullover without detecting her beneath it and moved 6.1 m at up to 7.7 mph. Finally, it needs synchronized records for post-incident reconstruction.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Lecture 12 develops the ordered fallback ladder that prevents unsafe pullovers, and Lecture 14 covers trustworthy audit logs.}

  \notecue{Next}{Ask which enduring principles generalize beyond Tempe and Cruise to organize the course.}
}

\begin{frame}{Four laws: one physical, three design principles}
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}P{5.2cm}P{8.6cm}@{}}
    \textbf{Law (part)} & \textbf{Documented failure} \\\midrule
    \textbf{Irreversibility} (I), physical & Tempe: 18.7 m with braking withheld \\
    \leavevmode\uncover<2->{\textbf{Endogenous data} (II)} & \leavevmode\uncover<2->{ALVINN: no recoveries in its logs} \\
    \leavevmode\uncover<3->{\textbf{Proposal is not permission} (III)} & \leavevmode\uncover<3->{Therac-25: software replaced interlocks} \\
    \leavevmode\uncover<4->{\textbf{Permission must be earned} (IV)} & \leavevmode\uncover<4->{Tempe operator; Cruise moved without fresh evidence} \\\bottomrule
  \end{tabular}\\[0.25cm]
  \uncover<4->{\small Only the first is a law of physics. The book elevates the other three by choice.}
  \mlsyscredit{The Causal Boundary, \S1.10 and table 3; Pomerleau 1991 (\S1.4.1, note 2); Leveson and Turner 1993; UL 4600 (2023).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Four organizing rules follow from these failures, one per part, yet only the first is a law of physics. Under Irreversibility (Part I), physical work cannot roll back, as Tempe's 18.7 m braking hold showed. Under Endogenous data (Part II), actions select future inputs so mistakes compound as states drift (covariate shift, Ross et al. 2011), as when human drivers kept the 1989 ALVINN test van centered in its lane, leaving its training logs without examples of steering back from the shoulder (Pomerleau 1991; Lecture 5). Under Proposal is not permission (Part III, Simplex, Sha 2001), the Therac-25 radiation therapy machine overdosed patients after software replaced mechanical safety interlocks (Leveson and Turner 1993). Under Permission must be earned (Part IV), machines may run only within tested operating conditions (operational design domain, or ODD, Lecture 5) backed by structured evidence arguments (assurance cases, standard UL 4600), unlike Tempe's unmonitored takeover and Cruise's unverified pullover.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Irreversibility follows from mechanics and thermodynamics, whereas laws two through four are design choices; violating them incurs predictable engineering risk.}

  \notecue{Next}{Preview the chain of records putting these four laws into practice.}
}
\end{frame}
