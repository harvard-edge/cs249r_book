  \note{[2 min] [CORE] Peer instruction: vote on all three, one minute with a neighbor, vote again. Uber disabled the Volvo's factory forward-collision warning and emergency braking during autonomous operation over possible radar interference, removing an independent protective path; Volvo's simulations suggest it might have prevented or mitigated the crash. The design relied on the operator as the primary emergency countermeasure, yet nothing monitored her attention; she was looking down for about five seconds before impact. That is a governance failure, because authority rested on evidence nobody collected. Step 3: the NTSB's probable cause was the operator's distraction, with Uber ATG's safety culture, oversight, and risk assessment as contributing factors. Back to the hook: D was half right. WARN: the investigation does not prescribe a hardware architecture. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}

  \note{[2.5 min] [CORE] One law per part of the course. Step 1: irreversibility. Work done on the plant cannot be rolled back, so every admissibility check must finish before the clearance it protects is spent; Part I turns this into budgets for the body and timing for the controllers. Step 2: endogenous data. A robot's actions choose its next observations, so errors compound with the task horizon, quadratically in the worst case (Ross et al.); ALVINN's drivers kept the van centered, so its training data had no recoveries. Lecture 5 owns this. Step 3: proposal is not permission. Therac-25 overdosed patients when software took over protection that hardware interlocks had provided. Step 4: permission must be earned.
