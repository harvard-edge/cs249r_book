\begin{frame}{A robot's actions choose the states it sees next}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{loops_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small\uncover<2->{\textbf{Endogenous covariate shift:} the policy's own actions lead it to states its data never held.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Physical Data, \S5.1, \S5.2, and definition 5.1.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Why does a small mistake hurt a robot more than an image classifier? In an open-loop classifier running on a fixed test set, mislabeling image 1 never alters image 2, so covariate shift (a change in the input distribution) comes only from external (exogenous) changes such as afternoon glare. A closed-loop robot policy behaves differently, as the second loop shows. Each action moves the body, shifting camera viewpoints and contact forces, so a small mistake alters the very next observation. Definition 5.1 names this endogenous covariate shift, illustrated when a gripper drifts 1 mm off centerline and sees the latch at an unseen angle.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Do not mistake closed-loop shift for unlucky test-set drift. Because the policy itself drives the machine into unseen states, collecting more identical centered demonstrations cannot eliminate the shift.}

  \notecue{Next}{Inspect the narrow demonstration corridor on the latch strike plate to see where training support vanishes.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Clean demonstrations fill the corridor and nothing beside it}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.5cm,keepaspectratio]{corridor_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Physical Data, figure 1 and \S5.3, redrawn at slide size.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  To see where training support vanishes on our cage-door latch, look down at the strike plate from above. Skilled operators stay centered along a narrow approach corridor, and routine curation discards aborted or messy trajectories, packing thousands of straight-ahead pushes inside that band. When the deployed gripper drifts 3 mm off center toward the fixture, as the second view shows, zero recorded examples return from that displaced state to the corridor, leaving the policy's output unconstrained. Compliant hardware might guide a small offset back, whereas a stiff arm keeps drifting until the permission path or an operator intervenes.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Aggressive filtering of imperfect runs makes recovery worse because it deletes the only off-corridor states ever captured. The defect is missing state support rather than noisy labels.}

\begin{frame}{Cloning's worst-case cost grows as the horizon squared}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.88\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{bound_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small \textbf{Behavioral cloning}: supervised learning of the demonstrator's action at each recorded state.}
  \mlsyscredit{Physical Data, \S5.3 and equation 1 (Ross and Bagnell 2010); comparison at $S = 500$ steps (5 s at 100 Hz), $\varepsilon = 0.001$, \S5.8. Curves derived.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  How fast does missing off-corridor support compound over an episode? Behavioral cloning minimizes supervised error only on demonstrated states, with per-step mistake probability at most $\varepsilon$ across $S$ steps, here $S = 500$ (5 s at 100 Hz) and $\varepsilon = 0.001$. In open loop, errors never feed back and expected cost grows linearly as $S\varepsilon = 0.5$. In closed loop, one mistake at step $t$ can strand the policy off corridor for all remaining $S - t$ steps, summing to roughly $S^2\varepsilon / 2$. Ross and Bagnell (2010) proved this classic worst-case imitation bound under a zero-one action loss, giving $S^2\varepsilon = 250$, half the 500-step ceiling. Above $\varepsilon = 1/S = 0.002$, or 0.001 at $S = 1{,}000$, the bound exceeds the maximum episode cost and becomes vacuous.

  \notecue{Watch for}{This worst-case task-cost bound differs from geometric position drift. Policies trained with logarithmic loss (maximizing expert-action probability) or Lecture 3's chunked multimodal architectures follow different scaling regimes (Foster et al. 2024; Simchowitz et al. 2025).}

  \notecue{Next}{Test whether more clean hours, a larger model, or a faster logger fills off-corridor support.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Leader arms copy joint angles to the follower directly}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.80\textwidth,height=4.6cm,keepaspectratio]{gello-teleop.jpg}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Seven collection paths, each with its own artifact in the recording.}
  \mlsyscredit{Wu et al., GELLO, IROS 2024, arXiv:2309.13037, Figure 1 (two UR5s, an xArm, a Franka). Physical Data, \S5.4 and table 1.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  Every recorded action carries the physical signature of the rig that gathered it. GELLO (Wu et al. 2024) is a low-cost teleoperation rig whose miniature leader arms mirror the joint layout of commercial follower arms such as UR5, xArm, and Franka, copying joint angles directly without inverse kinematics (solving for joint angles from a hand pose). Each of the chapter's seven collection paths leaves a distinct artifact. Kinesthetic teaching, guiding the arm by hand, puts the human body in camera views; teleoperation adds display latency and hesitation; scripted motions repeat rigid paths; autonomous exploration risks collisions. Deployment logs omit hazardous states that safety checks stop early, hand-held grippers omit robot joint dynamics, and simulation records numerical contact approximations. Targeted teleoperation with deliberate perturbations remains the standard path for gathering recovery states safely.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A passive GELLO leader arm passes only its own joint friction to the operator's hand, hiding contact forces on the follower.}

}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One command exists as four logged signals}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{taps_step}{4}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small\uncover<4->{\textbf{Action-label semantics}: the tap a dataset keeps decides what the policy learns.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Physical Data, \S5.5.1, fig.\ 2, redrawn; PTP (IEEE 1588 hardware clock). Explore: Four Action Taps on mlsysbook.ai.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Once a rig issues a move, that single command crosses the control stack and becomes four distinct signals, all dated on one shared hardware clock using IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol (PTP) matching Lecture 4's handoff record. Tap 1 (Asked) logs the raw operator or policy request on the application processor before checks. Tap 2 (Mapped) converts that request into a target joint setpoint on the proposal side. Crossing the proposal boundary, Tap 3 (Allowed) records what the 1 kHz permission path admitted or clamped for the drives. Tap 4 (Done) records what joint encoders (angle sensors on each joint) and current sensors measured. For the latch task, Tap 2 is the training label whenever Tap 3 equals Tap 2 (admitted unchanged); any clamped step is logged as an attributed intervention rather than a normal demonstration.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Tap 4 (Done) is the resulting next state, so it belongs in the observation input rather than the action label.}

  \notecue{Next}{Diagnose three deployment bugs by matching each symptom to the action tap the policy imitated.}
}
\end{frame}

Dataset Aggregation (DAgger, Ross et al. 2011), added as the linear curve here, queries an expert on the states the learner itself reaches. At $S = 500$ steps, $\varepsilon = 0.001$, and recoverability constant $u = 1$, worst-case cost drops from behavioral cloning's $S^2\varepsilon = 250$ to $uS\varepsilon = 0.5$. Linearity requires that $u$, how much one wrong step increases remaining expert cost, stay bounded. A recoverable withdraw-and-reseat keeps $u$ small, whereas shearing a cable or slamming 100 N into a latch makes $u$ scale with $S$, collapsing the bound back to $S^2\varepsilon$. The first law, irreversibility, therefore bounds imitation theory directly. No dataset can teach recovery from unrecoverable states, so the permission path must prevent the robot from reaching them.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Because cloning and DAgger evaluate different losses over different state distributions, treat this curve comparison as conceptual.}

  \notecue{Next}{Check this theoretical gain against physical cable-routing results from ThriftyDAgger.}
}
\end{frame}
