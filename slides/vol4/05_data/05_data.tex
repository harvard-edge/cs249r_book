\section{Recovery}

\begin{frame}{A robot's actions choose the states it sees next}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{loops_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small\uncover<2->{\textbf{Endogenous covariate shift:} the policy's own actions lead it to states its data never held.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Physical Data, \S5.1, \S5.2, and definition 5.1.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  First, an image classifier runs open loop on a fixed test set: mislabeling image 1 never alters image 2, and covariate shift arises only from external (exogenous) changes such as afternoon glare. Second, a physical policy runs closed loop: each action moves the body, shifting camera viewpoints and contact forces, so a small mistake alters the next observation itself. Definition 5.1 names this endogenous covariate shift (for example, a gripper drifting 1 mm off centerline and seeing the latch at an unseen angle).

  \notecue{Watch for}{Students often mistake closed-loop shift for unlucky test-set drift; because the policy itself drives the machine into unseen states, collecting more identical centered demonstrations cannot eliminate it.}

  \notecue{Next}{Visualize the narrow demonstration corridor on the latch strike plate to see where training support vanishes.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Clean demonstrations fill the corridor and nothing beside it}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.5cm,keepaspectratio]{corridor_step}{2}
  \mlsyscredit{Physical Data, figure 1 and \S5.3, redrawn at slide size.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  View the latch from above across two build steps. First, skilled operators stay centered along a narrow approach corridor, and routine curation discards aborted or messy trajectories, packing the archive with thousands of straight-ahead pushes inside the corridor. Second, once the deployed gripper sits 3 mm off center toward the fixture, zero examples exist returning from that state to the corridor, leaving the policy's action unconstrained. While compliant hardware might guide a small offset back, a stiff arm continues drifting until the permission path or an operator intervenes.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Aggressive filtering of imperfect runs makes recovery worse by deleting the only off-corridor states ever captured; the defect is missing state support rather than noisy labels.}

  \notecue{Next}{Look back at ALVINN in 1989 to see an early recovery-synthesis solution and why it fails under physical contact.}
}

\begin{frame}{Cloning's worst-case cost grows as the horizon squared}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.88\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{bound_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small \textbf{Behavioral cloning}: supervised learning of the demonstrator's action at each recorded state.}
  \mlsyscredit{Physical Data, \S5.3 and equation 1 (Ross and Bagnell 2010); comparison at $S = 500$ steps (5 s at 100 Hz), $\varepsilon = 0.001$, \S5.8. Curves derived.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Behavioral cloning minimizes supervised error strictly along demonstrated states, leaving per-step mistake probability bounded by $\varepsilon$ over an $S$-step episode ($S = 500$ steps for 5 s at 100 Hz, $\varepsilon = 0.001$). First, without closed-loop feedback, expected errors grow linearly as $S\varepsilon = 0.5$. Second, in closed loop, an initial error at step $t$ can maroon the policy off corridor for all remaining $S - t$ steps, summing to roughly $S^2\varepsilon / 2$, with the published worst-case bound $S^2\varepsilon = 250$ (Ross and Bagnell 2010), half the 500-step ceiling. Above $\varepsilon = 1/S = 0.002$ (or 0.001 at $S = 1{,}000$), the bound exceeds the maximum possible cost and becomes vacuous.

  \notecue{Watch for}{This worst-case task-cost bound differs from geometric position drift, and modern log-loss or chunked multimodal policies exhibit different scaling regimes (Foster et al. 2024; Simchowitz et al. 2025).}

  \notecue{Next}{Test whether scaling up clean hours, model size, or logging frequency resolves off-corridor support.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One command exists as four logged signals}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{taps_step}{4}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small\uncover<4->{\textbf{Action-label semantics}: the tap a dataset keeps decides what the policy learns.}}
  \mlsyscredit{Physical Data, \S5.5.1, fig.\ 2, redrawn; PTP (IEEE 1588 hardware clock). Explore: Four Action Taps on mlsysbook.ai.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Build the four action taps in order on one hardware clock (IEEE 1588 PTP, matching Lecture 4's handoff record). First, Tap 1 (Asked) is the raw operator or policy request on the application processor before any check. Second, Tap 2 (Mapped) converts that request into a joint setpoint on the proposal side. Third, Tap 3 (Allowed) records the command after the 1 kHz permission path has admitted or clamped it for the drives. Fourth, Tap 4 (Done) records what joint encoders and current sensors actually measured. For the latch task, Tap 2 serves as the training label whenever Tap 3 equals Tap 2 (admitted unchanged); any clamped step is logged as an attributed intervention rather than a normal demonstration.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Tap 4 (Done) is the resulting next state, an observation input rather than an action label.}
