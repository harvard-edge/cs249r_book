  \begin{columns}[c]

      {\footnotesize Booster field joint: primary and secondary O-ring. In 1982 NASA reclassified the pair from redundant (1R) to single-point (1): joint rotation could unseat both.}

  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Deployment Release, equations 2 and 3 (stipulated model). Drawing: Rogers Commission, vol.~1, p.~57, public domain; reclassification: Rogers ch.~VI.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Contrast conjunctive premises against independent legs across two clicks (equations 2 and 3). First, Step 1 models ten required child claims each holding at 0.99: together they hold at $0.99^{10} = 0.904$, weaker than any single premise (derived). Because clear distance, deceleration, onset, observation age, localization, and tracking enter one stopping budget, assessors hunt the weakest premise rather than counting pages. Second, Step 2 shows two sufficient legs at 0.90 yielding $1 - 0.1^2 = 0.99$ only when failures are independent. Beside the diagram, NASA reclassified the booster's primary and secondary O-rings from redundant (1R) to single-point (1) in December 1982 after finding joint rotation could unseat both (Rogers ch.~VI).

  \notecue{Watch for}{A policy and enforcer sharing processors, memory, or rails fail together (claim C5, Lecture 13), and Knight and Leveson (1986) showed coincident failures even across independently coded programs.}
