\begin{frame}{A safety case links each claim to evidence through a warrant}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.97\textwidth,height=5.05cm,keepaspectratio]{cae_step}{3}\\[0.06cm]
  {\footnotesize Goal Structuring Notation (GSN) aligns with DO-178C (aviation) and ISO 21448 SOTIF (intended function).}
  \mlsyscredit{After Deployment Release, \S16.3.1 to \S16.3.3 (claim-argument-evidence; GSN; Toulmin 1958). Values illustrative.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Because a single unverified input erases that stopping margin, release organizes claims and evidence into a safety case, a structured argument that a named machine meets physical criteria inside a stated envelope and fault model. That anatomy comes from philosopher Stephen Toulmin's 1958 book \emph{The Uses of Argument}, which split practical reasoning into claim, data, warrant, and rebuttal; engineers write it in Claim-Argument-Evidence (CAE) or Goal Structuring Notation (GSN, aligned with DO-178C for aviation and ISO 21448 SOTIF for safety of the intended functionality). Here the stopping claim links envelope context and calibrated evidence through an architectural warrant, contrasted against 1,000 clean runs, which remain a demonstration without coverage over untested conditions. Pure testing hits Lecture 7's exposure wall ($3.0 \times 10^9$ failure-free hours at 95\% confidence for $10^{-9}$/h). Defeaters, meaning realizable conditions such as clear oil near $\mu = 0.05$, walking coworkers, or worn brakes, break that warrant.

\begin{frame}{Required premises multiply; only independent legs add}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.64\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.5cm,keepaspectratio]{legs_step}{2}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.34\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=3.3cm,keepaspectratio]{srb-field-joint.jpg}\\[0.05cm]
      {\footnotesize Booster field joint: primary and secondary O-ring. In 1982 NASA reclassified the pair from redundant (1R) to single-point (1): joint rotation could unseat both.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Deployment Release, equations 2 and 3 (stipulated model). Drawing: Rogers Commission, vol.~1, p.~57, public domain; reclassification: Rogers ch.~VI.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  A safety case weakens rapidly whenever multiple premises must all hold at once (equations 2 and 3). When ten required child claims each hold with probability 0.99, their conjunctive probability drops to $0.99^{10} = 0.904$ (derived), weaker than any single premise. Clear distance, deceleration, brake onset, observation age, localization, and tracking all enter one stopping budget, so assessors hunt the weakest link rather than counting pages. Two sufficient legs at 0.90 rise to $1 - 0.1^2 = 0.99$ only when their failures are genuinely independent. For the shuttle booster, NASA reclassified its primary and secondary O-rings from redundant (1R) to single-point (1) in December 1982 after finding that joint rotation could unseat both seals together (Rogers Commission, ch.~VI).

  \notecue{Watch for}{A policy and safety enforcer sharing processors, memory, or power rails can fail together (claim C5, Lecture 13), and Knight and Leveson (1986) found coincident failures well above independent predictions even across independently coded programs.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, test these ideas on four concrete claims about safety-case math, independence, and dim warehouse light.}
}
\end{frame}

The Update Framework (TUF) pioneered role-separated root, targets, and release keys so one compromised key cannot sign fleetwide firmware, and Uptane extended TUF to automotive and robotics systems whose separate electronic control units require independently authenticated images. Drawn from the \texttt{python-tuf} reference repository, this metadata signs key IDs, signatures, an expiry timestamp, SHA-256 hashes, and file lengths alongside a hardware security module (HSM) holding private keys. Valid TUF metadata attests exact file bytes and expiry, yet proves nothing about physical camera pose or brake health, which is why a robotic release manifest must also bind calibration, standing conditions, and wear budgets.

  \notecue{Teach}{For a 50-minute class, skip this slide and move straight to the change-impact matrix showing which claims updates reopen.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, map five concrete software and hardware changes against the exact safety-case claims each reopens.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{An update reopens every claim whose evidence it touches}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.97\textwidth,height=4.9cm,keepaspectratio]{reopen.pdf}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small An unchanged enforcer binary does not mean an unchanged containment argument.}
  \mlsyscredit{After Deployment Release, \S16.9.1, \S16.7, and \S16.8. Lecture 6: every updated checkpoint is a new policy.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Once a manifest binds one configuration, any software or hardware change reopens every claim whose evidence it touches, not only the edited module. Retraining a policy on the same neural network backbone still creates a new checkpoint whose prior evaluations lapse (Lecture 6), reopening capability and contact evidence. Shipping a larger backbone with a byte-identical enforcer binary raises memory traffic and power-rail droop, reopening timing (C2), observation age (C4), and hardware independence (C5); an unchanged enforcer binary never preserves the containment argument, meaning the full defense linking timing, sensing, and isolation against physical harm. Driver or thread-priority edits reopen those same claims. An operating system (OS) reflash resetting calibration reopens stop and contact through localization, swapping a camera lens reopens stop, contact, and potentially observation age, and a worn brake lining reopens stopping with zero software change.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Rolling back a binary never repairs worn brakes or stale calibration. Treat the software image and calibration schema as one rollback unit swapped only while parked with drives unpowered.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, evaluate which field rollout strategy actually qualifies an updated policy.}
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \thumbline{thumb_budget_bar_aisle_step8.pdf}{Release decides inequalities over an envelope; the aisle stop leaves 131.0~mm spare (48.7~mm after admission).}\\[0.16cm]
  \thumbline{thumb_legs_step2.pdf}{Required premises multiply (ten at 0.99 give 0.904); only independent legs add confidence.}\\[0.16cm]
  \thumbline{thumb_tree_step3.pdf}{Every unmeasured claim refuses; refusals name their measurement, and COND needs its own evidence.}\\[0.16cm]
  \thumbline{thumb_silent_step2.pdf}{Verdicts hold only while detectors read known-true; unknown refuses like a breach.}\\[0.16cm]
  \thumbline{thumb_manifest_step3.pdf}{Manifests bind records, binaries, and calibration by digest; updates reopen every claim they touch.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  Five core principles carry deployment release from that comparison into engineering practice. Release decides physical inequalities over a declared envelope, where the aisle stop leaves 131.0~mm spare and 48.7~mm after admission. Required conjunctive premises multiply downward ($0.99^{10} = 0.904$), whereas only independent legs add confidence. Every unmeasured claim refuses and names its missing measurement, and COND needs its own evidence. Verdicts hold only while standing conditions read known-true, and unknown refuses like a breach. Finally, digest-bound manifests tie records, binaries, and calibration to one configuration, and updates reopen every claim they touch.

  \notecue{Teach}{Ask students which slide established each principle as you review the five lines. If short on time, emphasize the second and fourth lines.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, consider three open release questions on update cadence, signers, and rare fleet intervals.}
}
