\begin{frame}{Oversight needs time, information, and authority}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{uber-test-vehicle.jpg}\\[0.05cm]
      {\footnotesize An Uber self-driving test vehicle. In Tempe in 2018, its backup operator answered in court.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \small A role is oversight a person can perform only if its holder has\\[0.12cm]
      \textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{time}} to act before the hazard arrives,\\
      \textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{information}} about what the machine is doing, and\\
      \textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{authority}} to change it.\\[0.2cm]
      A role that fails any of the three moves blame onto its holder.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: foam from Elgaland Vargaland, CC BY-SA 2.0, Wikimedia Commons (not the Tempe vehicle). Maricopa County Attorney 2023; Elish 2019; Responsible Physical AI, \S17.3, Definition 17.1.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  A person in the loop adds protection only when three conditions hold. Under Definition 17.1, matching the general conditions for meaningful human control that Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven (2018) drew from debates over autonomous weapons, a cited oversight role is performable only if its holder has time before the hazard arrives, accurate information about machine state, and authority to alter motion. Failing any one condition shifts blame without protecting anyone. Recall Lecture 1's 2018 Tempe crash, illustrated by an Uber test vehicle of the same class. Its backup operator pleaded guilty to endangerment in 2023, illustrating the moral crumple zone that Elish (2019) named after tracing accident blame across aviation and nuclear power, where a nearby human absorbs blame to shield system design.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Placing a person in the loop never makes an unsafe design responsible unless all three conditions hold.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, diagnose whether the remote assistant passes the time test at the warehouse rack end.}
}
\end{frame}

  \mlsysactivity{Diagnose}{2 min}

\begin{frame}{One assistant for twenty machines is busy half the time}

    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=4.95cm,keepaspectratio]{queue_step}{2}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Responsible Physical AI, Napkin Math 17.1 (inputs illustrative); M/M/1 queue. Utilization, waits, and the 99th percentile derived.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{3.5 min}{Core}%
  Even when machines wait at standstill, one assistant serving a fleet becomes a single-server M/M/1 queue with random arrivals and service (Napkin Math 17.1). At an illustrative 0.5 requests per machine-hour and mean service time $\IntervalMean = 180\text{ s}$, 10 machines yield utilization $\ServingUtilization = 0.25$ and mean wait $W_q = 60\text{ s}$, while 20 machines reach $\ServingUtilization = 0.50$ and $W_q = 180\text{ s}$. Extending to 32 machines pushes $\ServingUtilization = 0.80$ and $W_q = 720\text{ s}$, or 12~min. Even at 20 machines, the 99th-percentile wait hits 1,408~s, or 23.5~min; for fleet context, Cruise used about 1.5 assistants per car in 2023. In Checkpoint 17.1, 24 machines at 0.8 req/h and 75~s each give $\ServingUtilization = 0.40$ and $W_q = 50\text{ s}$, still far beyond 0.85~s. Staffing ratios therefore govern productivity rather than safety, with Lecture 14 admitting sessions only from standstill, and capping $\ServingUtilization \le 0.5$ limits the fleet to 20 machines under a named owner.

    \end{column}

  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%

  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Every safety record captures people. Lecture 14's authority record names operators, black-box video captures aisle workers, and Lecture 5's provenance record identifies the operators who recorded demonstrations. Retained indefinitely with open access, safety stores become workplace surveillance and violate the minimization rules in European Union (EU) privacy law, General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) Article 5(1)(c) and (e). Instead of continuous recording across a 16-hour, or 960-minute, day, event-triggered retention captures 20 daily triggers and keeps only 5~s before and 25~s after each trigger, or 30~s per event. That policy retains 10~minutes a day, about 1\%, a $96\times$ reduction (derived). Turning data declarations into release premises makes them Lecture 16 standing conditions backed by access and retention audits whose failure suspends use.

People records require declared bounds, and 20 machines shift 17.3 person-hours daily.
