  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Once unstructured tasks require a learned proposer, its input format sets the workload. OpenVLA-7B's \texttt{config.json} pairs a Llama 2 7B language-model transformer backbone in 16-bit brain floating point (bfloat16) with two vision encoders (DINOv2 and SigLIP, which turn $14 \times 14$-pixel patches into token vectors) on $224 \times 224$ images and 256 discrete action bins (output buckets). Slicing one view yields $224 / 14 = 16$ patches per side, or $16^2 = 256$ visual tokens. Three cameras (768 patches), 32 instruction tokens, and 16 state tokens sum to 816, rounded to 800. Their internal token embeddings occupy 6.55 MB ($800$ tokens $\times$ $4{,}096$ hidden width $\times$ $2$ bytes in 16-bit precision), whereas 16-bit weights take 14 GB, over 2,000$\times$ larger. Our one-view intent model feeds $256 + 32 = 288$ tokens. Token count governs compute per weight and the key-value (KV) cache storing past token state rather than embedding storage.

\begin{frame}{Every retained token costs 0.52 MB of key-value cache}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=12.4cm]{kv_ladder.pdf}\\[0.15cm]
  {\small OpenVLA keeps no key-value (KV) cache between frames. Keeping 5 s of history at 10 Hz would cost \textbf{\textcolor{figred}{21.0 GB}}, more than the weights.}
  \mlsyscredit{Ch.\ 3, equation 1 and \S3.5.2: $2 \times 32 \times 4{,}096 \times 2$ B per token, 800 tokens per step; hypothetical history. Module: 64 GB ($64 \times 2^{30}$ B $=$ 68.7 GB).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
  Because token count governs the KV cache, keeping visual history across frames grows expensive quickly. To attend over past tokens without recomputing them, every transformer layer caches a key and value vector per token. Across 32 layers of width 4,096 in 16 bits, each token occupies $2 \times 32 \times 4{,}096 \times 2 = 524{,}288$ bytes, or about 0.52 MB, so one 800-token step adds 419 MB. Single-frame models like OpenVLA and our intent model rebuild context each frame and carry no cross-frame KV cache. Retaining 5 seconds of history at 10 Hz would consume 21.0 GB, exceeding the 14 GB of weights. Together they would take 35.0 GB, over half the Orin's 68.7 GB memory, while adding traffic to the same bus carrying camera frames without making answers fresher.

  \vspace{0.02cm}
  \centering\uncover<3->{\small $t_{\text{sweep}} = M_{\text{weights}} / BW_{\text{sus}} = 14~\text{GB} / 142.8~\text{GB/s} = 98.0~\text{ms}$, so $f \le 1/t_{\text{sweep}} = \mathbf{\textcolor{figred}{10.2~\text{Hz}}}$}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: Auledas, CC BY 4.0, Wikimedia Commons. Ch.\ 3, Definition 3.1 and Napkin Math 3.1: 70\% of the 204 GB/s peak sustained (illustrative).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Why does the Jetson AGX Orin, rated at 43 TFLOP/s dense FP16, stall on a 7B model? Fast on-chip memory (SRAM) holds only megabytes, so 14 GB of 16-bit weights sit in off-chip low-power DRAM (LPDDR5, at 204 GB/s shared by CPU, GPU, and camera direct memory access, or DMA). Because those weights do not stay on chip, every fresh batch-1 evaluation must stream all 14 GB across the bus at least once. At an illustrative 70\% sustained bandwidth (142.8 GB/s), one weight sweep takes $t_{\text{sweep}} = 14\text{ GB} / 142.8\text{ GB/s} = 98.0\text{ ms}$. Fresh evaluations therefore top out at 10.2 Hz before vision encoding or multi-token output generation, 98$\times$ slower than the 1 kHz permission tick.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Fewer weight bytes or higher memory bandwidth raises this ceiling more than faster arithmetic units. The diagram labels off-chip memory high-bandwidth memory (HBM), whereas the Orin uses LPDDR5. Heat from repeated DRAM sweeps also warms the enclosure, raising ambient temperature against Lecture 2's motor heat budget.}

  \notecue{Next}{See how this ceiling falls across four orders of magnitude of parameter count for FP16 and INT4.}
}
\end{frame}

  That 10.2 Hz ceiling scales inversely with weight bytes, so plotting parameter count from 10 million to 100 billion against fresh evaluations per second on logarithmic axes at 142.8 GB/s sustained bandwidth reveals the gap across model sizes. \begin{frame}{Prefill reuses each weight 288 times; decode uses it once}

  \mlsyssteps[width=14.2cm]{roofline_step}{3}\\[0.05cm]

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  A single weight sweep does not tell the whole latency story, because reading inputs and emitting outputs stress different hardware limits. Williams, Waterman, and Patterson (2009) introduced the roofline model to separate those regimes by plotting arithmetic intensity, floating-point operations (FLOPs) per off-chip memory byte (counting weight bytes only here), against attainable TFLOP/s. On the Orin, the slanted 204 GB/s bandwidth roof meets the flat 43 TFLOP/s compute roof at a ridge point of 210.8 FLOP/byte. During decode, each output token runs a matrix-vector multiply using each 2-byte weight for one multiply-add, or 1 FLOP/byte, capping throughput at 0.204 TFLOP/s, only 0.47\% of peak. During prefill, all 288 input tokens (256 image patches plus 32 instruction tokens) share every fetched weight at once, landing past the ridge in the compute-bound regime.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Compressing weights with 4-bit quantization speeds up bandwidth-bound decode rather than compute-bound prefill. Dividing FLOPs by the 43 TFLOP/s peak overestimates batch-1 decode speed by two orders of magnitude. The systems appendix reviews rooflines.}

  \mlsyscredit{github.com/tonyzhaozh/act, commit 742c753, imitate\_episodes.py (indentation removed). Black, Galliker, and Levine, NeurIPS 2025. Ch.\ 3, \S3.5.1.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Replacing one chunk abruptly with another can inject velocity and acceleration jumps at seams, so ACT smooths transitions through temporal ensembling. Its \texttt{imitate\_episodes.py} code averages all overlapping chunks covering step $t$ with weights $e^{-k i}$ for $k = 0.01$. Index 0 is the oldest chunk, receiving 2.7$\times$ the newest chunk's weight across 100 overlapping chunks, whereas our machine overlaps $16 / 2 = 8$ chunks. Blending removes seam jumps yet introduces two hazards. Averaging two valid paths passing left and right of an obstacle creates a collision straight through it (mode averaging, Lecture 6), and a blend inherits its oldest kept capture time, so expired chunks must be dropped. Real-time chunking (Black, Galliker, and Levine 2025) avoids averaging by freezing setpoints running during inference and generating the new chunk's remainder to join them smoothly.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Lecture 4 interpolates admitted setpoints across 1 ms bus ticks, and Lecture 11 joins trajectory segments smoothly within acceleration and torque limits.}

  \notecue{Next}{With supply and freshness bounded, move to Section 4 to specify what every proposal must carry so the permission path can refuse an on-time wrong plan.}
}
\end{frame}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
  Filtering hallucinations by uncalibrated confidence fails under shift, as the four illustrative regimes plotted here (Chapter 3, Table 4) show. In distribution, confidence (0.91) matches success (0.89) within 0.02. Near out of distribution (OOD, inputs shifted by glare or lens droplets), confidence stays 0.89 while success falls to 0.52; under degraded sensing it is 0.84 vs. 0.31, and far OOD it is 0.78 vs. 0.08. Softmax normalizes raw scores to sum to one by algebra, modern deep networks are often overconfident even in distribution (Guo et al. 2017), and rectified linear unit (ReLU) networks approach high confidence far from training data (Hein et al. 2019). Continuous chunk policies like ACT emit no softmax score, needing calibrated trajectory spread (dispersion), disagreement across separate models (ensembles), or conformal coverage cutoffs (thresholds, Lecture 10) tied to \texttt{model\_id}. Admitting scores at or above 0.78 passes all four regimes, accepting a 48\% near-OOD and 92\% far-OOD failure rate.

  \notecue{Watch for}{These four regime pairs are stipulated values clearly marked illustrative on the slide.}
