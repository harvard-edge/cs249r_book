\mlsysnote{
  \mlsysnotemeta{0.5 min}{Core}%
  Earlier lectures built protective responses such as expiring leases, resident stops, and handover fallbacks that normal operation almost never triggers. This lecture asks how to verify that those responses actually work after a fault. Testing a claim about fault behavior requires injecting the fault on purpose, witnessing the response independently, and scoring it against an oracle, a verdict and margin rule fixed before the run. Students from machine learning know adversarial attacks and need precommitted oracles and independent witnesses. Students from embedded systems know hardware faults and need the rare-event statistics in the final segment, supported by this volume's machine learning appendix. Students from control engineering know reachability analysis, which bounds the states a dynamical system can reach under bounded disturbances, and need physical injection rigs.

  \notecue{Teach}{Read the thesis aloud once. Full notes total 71 minutes, or 57 Core without Optional frames (DriveFI, shared rail F4, published attacks, proof scale, rare-event data, extreme values, open questions). Split two lectures after Fault List (\S15.6).}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, predict from Bookout v. Toyota whether clean driving mileage can expose a dormant watchdog flaw.}
}

  \small\uncover<2->{Without the oracle, engineers read plots after the run and the trial becomes an anecdote.}\\[0.15cm]
  \uncover<3->{\footnotesize A software build repeats bit for bit; a physical trial repeats only within declared tolerances.}
  \mlsyscredit{Adversarial Verification, \S15.1 (the cyber-physical test contract).}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Injecting F1 through F4 alone produces an anecdote rather than evidence unless all five parts of this contract are in place. A trial begins with a bounded claim (here that the base stops within 1.10~m) paired with declared physical and internal conditions. Before anything runs, an automated precommitted oracle locks down the verdict rules, while an explicit injection procedure fixes where and how the fault enters. Finally, cryptographic provenance binds hash digests of model weights, calibrations, oscilloscope traces, and bus logs. Without a precommitted oracle, engineers fall into post-hoc rationalization, excusing a late brake because the base happened to stop short of the rack. Unlike a bit-reproducible software build, a physical trial repeats only within declared tolerances, details that demonstration videos almost always omit.

  \notecue{Watch for}{This lecture tracks the base stopping claim. The arm's latch-force claim requires its own declared envelope and oracle.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, published fault-injection data from DriveFI shows why random faults miss hazards hiding in narrow envelope corners.}
}

\begin{frame}{Random faults found no hazard; a targeted search found 561}
  \centering\safeimg[width=0.95\textwidth,height=3.9cm,keepaspectratio]{drivefi.pdf}\\[0.1cm]
  \small Weeks of 5,000 random injections into two driving stacks: \textbf{no hazard}.\\[0.08cm]
  \uncover<2->{Hazards lived in \textbf{68 of 7,200} scenes (0.94 percent).}\\[0.08cm]
  \uncover<3->{A fault matters when it meets the wrong condition.}
  \mlsyscredit{Jha et al., DriveFI, DSN 2019, arXiv:1907.01051, \S I and \S VI (simulated DriveAV and Apollo). 0.94 percent derived.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
  Once a test contract is set, choosing where to inject faults decides whether hazards ever appear. In DriveFI (Jha et al., DSN 2019), researchers injected software faults into two simulated autonomous-driving stacks, NVIDIA DriveAV and Baidu Apollo. Weeks of 5,000 random injections produced zero safety hazards. In DriveAV, 2,400 random GPU faults corrupted actuation in 1.9\% of runs and crashed or hung software in 7.35\%, yet none caused a hazard. Testing all 98,400 faults exhaustively would take 615 days. Switching to targeted Bayesian search, which prioritizes states likely to fail, found 561 faults in under 4 hours, with 460 causing hazards concentrated in 68 of 7,200 scenes (0.94\%, derived). Uniform sampling pushes observed hazard counts toward zero while narrow dangerous corners go untested.

  \notecue{Watch for}{DriveFI injects simulated software faults, a low rung of the qualification ladder examined in Segment 4.}

  \notecue{Teach}{In a 50-minute class, skip this slide, note that random injection misses rare hazardous corners, and move directly to the speed-and-friction prediction.}

  \notecue{Next}{Next, predict whether two individually valid envelope points still pass when combined.}
}
\end{frame}

\section{Ladder}

\begin{frame}{Each qualification rung makes another part of the loop real}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.97\textwidth,height=4.8cm,keepaspectratio]{ladder_step}{4}\\[0.05cm]
  {\footnotesize Ladder cells, green: real item.\enspace Dashed: model.\enspace SIL, PIL, HIL: software, processor, hardware in the loop.}
  \mlsyscredit{Adversarial Verification, \S15.7.1, figure 2, and table 3, redrawn at slide size.}
  \note{
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  A simulator can stall a proposer overnight yet cannot sag a power rail, so verification climbs a four-rung qualification ladder making more of the loop real at each step. Software in the loop (SIL) runs logic across millions of parallel trials with modeled drives and floor, omitting instruction timing, bus timing, and supply transients. Processor in the loop (PIL) runs compiled enforcer code on the real MCU for real timing and call-stack memory headroom over a modeled bus. Hardware in the loop (HIL) adds the real fieldbus, regulators, and drives against a real-time plant model or drive-bench dynamometer.   \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  When a rare failure sits past the exposure wall, direct counting fails, as an illustrative exponential queue delay with mean 5~ms shows. Exceeding the 100.8~ms spare occurs at rate $p = 1.8 \times 10^{-9}$ per stop. Naive Monte Carlo, drawing unweighted trials from nominal operation, needs $5.7 \times 10^{10}$ runs for 10\% relative error, while a zero-failure bound needs $1.7 \times 10^9$. Importance sampling draws from an alternative proposal $q(x)$ and weights each run by the likelihood ratio $p(x)/q(x)$, where $p(x)$ is the nominal density (distinct from failure rate $p$), correcting for oversampling failures to stay unbiased. High-energy inductive kicks (voltage spikes when motor current switches off) crash loggers on the exact trials most likely to hang safety controllers, and a test cell has an end stop that a warehouse aisle lacks. Report all four verdict counts transparently and diagnose why the witness crashed before repeating the trial.}

  \notecue{Teach}{Take a True or False vote on both statements before revealing the explanations.}

  \notecue{Next}{Segment 5 sized failure-rate campaigns and bound every verdict and gap into the record; next, apply the complete lecture to settle the opening Toyota case.}
}
