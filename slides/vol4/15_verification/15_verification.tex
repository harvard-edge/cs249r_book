The physical margin is at least the simulated margin minus L times Delta. Step 2: for the rack end, with position normalized by 1 m and unit weight, L is 1 m per unit. A simulated stop that keeps the whole 131.0 mm reserve guarantees a positive physical margin only if Delta is below 0.131, a position error under 131 mm. A simulator validated to a few centimeters transfers most of its margin; one never compared with a measured stop transfers none. The photo is the point: only physical tests, like this crash test at the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, measure Delta. LINK: measuring it reuses Lecture 6's paired probing, with excitation at full-rate deceleration and external instruments. WARN: a Delta measured on an unloaded, cool machine does not cover a loaded, throttled one. Land the segment: choose the rung that contains the mechanism, search for small margins, and discount simulated ones.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Weighting runs near failure cuts the runs about $2 \times 10^7$ times}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.86\textwidth,height=4.1cm,keepaspectratio]{is_ladder_step}{3}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small One rare-event rate: a delay past the 100.8 ms spare, $p = 1.8 \times 10^{-9}$ per stop (illustrative).}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<3->{\footnotesize Runs weighted by $p(x)/q(x)$: 38.6\% fail, yet the estimate stays $1.8 \times 10^{-9}$.}
  \mlsyscredit{Adversarial Verification, \S15.10.2 and its figure (exponential delay, mean 5 ms; 10 percent relative error; cross-entropy proposal). Recomputed in images/calcs.py.}
  \note{[2 min] [CORE] Worked example: estimating one rare rate. The model: queue delays are exponential with mean 5 ms, an illustrative assumption; a delay beyond 100.8 ms spends the spare, probability $1.8 \times 10^{-9}$ per stop. Step 1: plain Monte Carlo needs about $5.7 \times 10^{10}$ runs to estimate it to 10 percent, because almost every run ends in a safe stop. Step 2: a zero-failure campaign needs $1.7 \times 10^9$ runs just to bound it. Step 3: importance sampling draws from a proposal q that puts runs near failure, then weights each run by the likelihood ratio, the operational density over the proposal density, to recover an unbiased estimate. The cross-entropy method finds q in four steps (levels 11.5, 38.0, 99.1, then 100.8 ms; final mean 105.8 ms). 2,709 runs suffice. WARN: 38.6 percent of proposal runs fail, and that fraction says nothing about real frequency; the same holds for falsification runs, which carry no weights at all. A rate belongs in a safety case only with its declared model, proposal, and weights. ENGAGE: what happens if the proposal misses a failure path entirely? (Expected: the estimate is biased low, with deceptively small variance, and no diagnostic sees it.)}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Are these two claims true or false?}
  \mlsysactivity{Concept question}{2 min}

  \textbf{1.} Independent watchdogs on each task guarantee the end-to-end deadline.\\[0.05cm]
