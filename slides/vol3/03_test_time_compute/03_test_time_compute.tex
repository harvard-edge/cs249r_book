\begin{frame}{Depth, breadth, and feedback spend one budget three ways}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.97\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{axes_step}{3}\\[0.1cm]
  {\footnotesize Budgeted deliberation: three allocation axes for one budget of tokens, calls, checks, and seconds.}
  \mlsyscredit{Test-Time Compute, \S3.2, figure 2 and Definition 3.1 (redrawn).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Before choosing between longer reasoning, parallel samples, or diagnostic tests, the runtime bounds all three under one allowance. Definition 3.1 frames the test-time compute budget as the per-decision allowance of prompt tokens, output tokens, model calls, checks, and wall-clock time before the runtime must select, abstain, or escalate. First, depth extends a single reasoning chain sequentially. Second, breadth samples parallel independent rollouts, or bounded candidate-generation paths, and delegates choice to a selector. Third, feedback runs a check in an isolated sandbox and feeds the observation into the next call, the only axis that supplies information absent from both prompt and weights.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Parallel sampling does not guarantee genuinely distinct hypotheses. As Lecture 2 showed, sampling reshapes candidates without verifying them, and independent draws from one model share its blind spots, so eight samples can all propose raising the timeout.}

\begin{frame}{One 4,096-token ceiling yields three latencies and three bills}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.3cm,keepaspectratio]{alloc_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Breadth: 4 streams of 1,024 tokens. Feedback: 3 rounds, each with a 1.5 s check.}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Breadth pays \textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{1.5$\times$}} depth's bill: 12,288 extra prompt tokens, read once per stream.}
  \mlsyscredit{Test-Time Compute, Napkin Math 3.2 and table 2 (illustrative: \$3 and \$15 per million input and output tokens, no cache discount).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  One 4,096-token output ceiling hides wide differences in latency and spend. Napkin Math 3.2 compares all three axes on a 4,096-token prompt at 21.075 ms per token, \$3 per million input tokens, and \$15 per million output tokens without cache discounts. Depth decodes 4,096 tokens serially in 86.3 s for \$0.074. Breadth runs 4 batched streams of 1,024 tokens in 21.6 s, sharing weight reads, yet costs \$0.111 (1.5$\times$ depth) because each stream reads the 4,096-token prompt, adding 12,288 extra input tokens. Feedback runs 3 rounds of 1,024 tokens (3,072 output tokens, below the cap) with three 1.5 s checks in 69.2 s. Appending each prior candidate and a 340-token check trace through round 3 (Lecture 2) totals $4{,}096 + 5{,}460 + 6{,}824 = 16{,}380$ input tokens, costing \$0.095.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Matching output ceilings never implies equal latency, memory, or spend, and only feedback adds a new fact. Prefix caching, reusing stored prompt states across streams (Lecture 5), removes most of breadth's input premium.}

  \notecue{Next}{That accounting prices work without accuracy. Examine measured evidence on how task difficulty determines which allocation wins.}}
\end{frame}

  \end{tabular}\\[0.4cm]

  \mlsyscredit{Snell et al., arXiv 2408.03314, 2024: \S3.2 (5 bins of pass@1 from 2,048 samples), \S5.3 and Fig.~3, \S6.2 and Fig.~7, \S1 and \S7, Fig.~9. One base model; MATH benchmark.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  To test where extra deliberation pays in accuracy, Snell et al. (2024) evaluated test-time budgets on MATH, a competition mathematics benchmark. They sorted problems into five difficulty bins by one model's pass@1 rate, the fraction of single attempts that pass before search, across 2,048 samples. On easy problems in bins 1 and 2, sequential revision worked best, while high-budget beam search, keeping top partial answers per step (Section 4), over-optimized its learned scorer. On medium problems in bins 3 and 4, step-scorer beam search matched best-of-$N$, selecting among $N$ finished samples, with 4$\times$ less compute (16 versus 64 samples). On the hardest problems in bin 5, test-time compute helped little and a larger model won. At equal floating-point operations (FLOPs), a small model with test-time compute beat a 14$\times$ larger model except in bin 5.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A runtime never knows true difficulty beforehand. It estimates difficulty from cheap pilot signals such as agreement across 2 or 3 samples or fast static linters, and when pilots consistently fail, escalating to a stronger model or a human beats sampling wider.}

  \notecue{Next}{Close Section 1 by estimating how breadth's extra prompt reads scale on a larger repository context.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pools cover 98\% of problems; majority vote returns 43\%}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.97\textwidth,height=5.5cm,keepaspectratio]{coverage_step}{3}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  To test how far real selectors lag behind pool coverage, Brown et al. (2024) sampled up to 10,000 answers per MATH problem and released per-sample labels, recomputed here in MLSysIM, the book's systems simulator. First, unbiased pass@$k$ coverage climbs across four orders of magnitude. An 8B-parameter open model (Llama 8B) rises from 27\% at $k=1$ to 98\% at $k=10{,}000$, while a 70B-parameter model rises from 48\% to 98\%. Next, majority voting on those same pools plateaus at 43\% for 8B and 59\% for 70B, with 8B gaining only 0.2 points after 256 samples while coverage gains 10 more.   \notecue{Watch for}{Coverage is only an upper bound, and only for selectors that return an existing candidate. Majority voting returns the model's most common answer rather than truth, so breadth pays only when an executable verifier can isolate a valid fix.}

\begin{frame}{Each search structure prunes at a different point}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{topo_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Test-Time Compute, \S3.6.1, figure 7 and table 6 (redrawn; Explore: Search Topologies on mlsysbook.ai). Beam: Lowerre 1976. Tree: Yao et al.\ 2023; Silver et al.\ 2016.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Modern search structures over model rollouts differ mainly in when they discard broken prefixes. First, best-of-$N$ decodes every candidate to completion before running a final selector, trading serving capacity for low latency while finishing flawed prefixes. Second, beam search, introduced in the HARPY speech system by Lowerre (1976) to bound time and memory at the risk of pruning the best path, keeps at most $w$ partial prefixes per step. It scores extensions and retains top survivors while holding $w \times \text{extensions}$ key-value (KV) caches, the stored attention states of earlier tokens, in memory at once. Third, tree search, adapted to multi-step reasoning in Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023), expands the most promising or under-explored leaf, scores it, and propagates visit counts and mean values back to the root.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Beam and tree search require step scorers. A noisy step scorer injects selection optimism into tree averages and pulls compute toward noise-inflated branches (Lecture 5 shares cached KV memory blocks across common prefixes, and Lecture 8 clones isolated workspaces per branch).}

Policy B runs 8 parallel candidates in 31.07 s, reaching 98.32\% pass@8 at \$0.172 per accepted repair (3.3$\times$ Policy A). Policy C adds feedback with a 340-token trace and 35\% revision pass rate. Only two rounds fit within 75 s because three reach 93.22 s, giving 61\% acceptance and \$0.059 per accepted repair. Because 40\% finish in round 1 (31.07 s) and 60\% run round 2 (62.15 s, under 75 s), mean latency is 49.72 s (1.6 rounds).

  \notecue{Watch for}{Under a 35 s deadline, only A and B fit. No policy dominates everywhere, so report false acceptance, abstention, and deadline misses separately across all started tasks (Lecture 13).}

  \notecue{Next}{Section 4 covered scoped revision, multi-account costing, ordered stopping, and deadlines. Return to the opening stall to diagnose an expensive policy.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3cm}
    \item Reasoning models choose how many hidden tokens to spend. Who should set that budget: the model, the runtime, or the user paying for it?
    \item Difficulty is unknown before the attempt. How cheap can a difficulty estimate be and still route the budget well?
    \item When a check becomes the target of search, how often must it be re-measured on selected candidates?
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Optional}%
  These three open questions highlight active design tensions in test-time compute. The first concerns reasoning models, models trained with reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks to emit extended intermediate scratchpads that a service may hide (recall hidden reasoning tokens from Lecture 2; \S3.2.6), asking who should own that budget when traces stay unseen. The second asks how cheaply a runtime can estimate task difficulty before committing budget (Example 3.1). The third asks how often checks must be re-measured once search targets them (\S3.4.3, Lecture 13).
