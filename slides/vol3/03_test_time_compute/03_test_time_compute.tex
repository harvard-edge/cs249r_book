\begin{frame}{Depth, breadth, and feedback spend one budget three ways}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.97\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{axes_step}{3}\\[0.1cm]
  {\footnotesize Budgeted deliberation: three allocation axes for one budget of tokens, calls, checks, and seconds.}
  \mlsyscredit{Test-Time Compute, \S3.2, figure 2 and Definition 3.1 (redrawn).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Before choosing between longer reasoning, parallel samples, or diagnostic tests, the runtime bounds all three under one allowance. Definition 3.1 frames the test-time compute budget as the per-decision allowance of prompt tokens, output tokens, model calls, checks, and wall-clock time before the runtime must select, abstain, or escalate. First, depth extends a single reasoning chain sequentially. Second, breadth samples parallel independent rollouts, or full candidate generations, and delegates choice to a selector. Third, feedback runs a check in an isolated sandbox and feeds the observation into the next call, the only axis that supplies information absent from both prompt and weights.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Parallel sampling does not guarantee genuinely distinct hypotheses. As Lecture 2 showed, sampling reshapes candidates without verifying them, and independent draws from one model share its blind spots, so eight samples can all propose raising the timeout.}

\begin{frame}{One 4,096-token ceiling yields three latencies and three bills}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.3cm,keepaspectratio]{alloc_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Breadth: 4 streams of 1,024 tokens. Feedback: 3 rounds, each with a 1.5 s check.}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Breadth pays \textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{1.5$\times$}} depth's bill: 12,288 extra prompt tokens, read once per stream.}
  \mlsyscredit{Test-Time Compute, Napkin Math 3.2 and table 2 (illustrative: \$3 and \$15 per million input and output tokens, no cache discount).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  One output cap hides wide differences in latency and spend. Napkin Math 3.2 compares all three axes for a 4,096-token prompt and 4,096-token output ceiling at 21.075 ms per token, \$3 per million input tokens, and \$15 per million output tokens with no cache discount. In wall-clock time, depth decodes 4,096 tokens serially in 86.3 s, breadth runs 4 parallel streams of 1,024 tokens in 21.6 s by sharing weight reads across the batch, and feedback runs 3 rounds of 1,024 tokens with three 1.5 s checks in 69.2 s. In spend, depth costs \$0.074, feedback costs \$0.095 from resending 16,380 cumulative input tokens by round 3 (Lecture 2), and breadth costs \$0.111, or 1.5$\times$ depth, because each stream reads its own 4,096-token prompt, adding 12,288 extra input tokens.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Matching output ceilings never implies equal latency, memory, or spend, and only feedback adds a new fact. Prefix caching, reusing stored prompt states across streams (Lecture 5), eliminates most of breadth's input premium.}

  \notecue{Next}{That accounting prices work without accuracy. Next, examine measured evidence on how task difficulty determines which allocation wins.}}
\end{frame}

  \mlsyscredit{Snell et al., arXiv 2408.03314, 2024: \S3.2 (5 bins of pass@1 from 2,048 samples), \S5.3 and Fig.~3, \S6.2 and Fig.~7, \S1 and \S7, Fig.~9. One base model; MATH benchmark.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  To test where extra deliberation pays in accuracy, Snell et al. (2024) evaluated test-time budgets on MATH, a competition mathematics benchmark. They sorted problems into five difficulty bins by one model's pass@1 rate, the fraction of single attempts that pass before search, across 2,048 samples. On easy problems in bins 1 and 2, sequential revision worked best, while high-budget beam search, keeping top partial answers per step (Section 4), over-optimized its learned scorer. On medium problems in bins 3 and 4, step-scorer beam search matched best-of-$N$, scoring $N$ finished samples, with 4$\times$ less compute (16 versus 64 samples). On the hardest problems in bin 5, test-time compute helped little and a larger model won. At equal floating-point operations (FLOPs), a small model with test-time compute beat a 14$\times$ larger model except in bin 5.

  \notecue{Watch for}{A runtime never knows true difficulty beforehand. It estimates difficulty from cheap signals such as agreement across 2 or 3 samples or fast static linters, and escalates to a stronger model or a human when those signals fail.}

  \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
  Allocating compute at decision time predates neural networks by decades. \begin{frame}{Each search structure prunes at a different point}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{topo_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Test-Time Compute, \S3.6.1, figure 7 and table 6 (redrawn; Explore: Search Topologies on mlsysbook.ai). Beam: Lowerre 1976. Tree: Yao et al.\ 2023; Silver et al.\ 2016.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Modern search structures over model rollouts differ mainly in when they discard broken prefixes. First, best-of-$N$ decodes every candidate to completion before running a final selector, trading serving capacity for low latency while finishing flawed prefixes. Second, beam search, introduced in the HARPY speech system by Lowerre (1976) to bound memory at a small pruning risk, keeps at most $w$ partial prefixes per step. It scores extensions and retains top survivors while holding $w \times \text{extensions}$ key-value (KV) caches, the stored attention states of earlier tokens, in memory at once. Third, tree search, adapted to multi-step reasoning in Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023), expands the most promising or under-explored leaf, scores it, and propagates visit counts and mean values back to the root.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Beam and tree search require step scorers. A noisy step scorer injects selection optimism into tree averages and pulls compute toward noise-inflated branches (Lecture 5 shares cached KV memory blocks across common prefixes, and Lecture 8 clones isolated workspaces per branch).}

  \notecue{Watch for}{If the deadline tightens to 35 s, feedback cannot run a second round and only A and B remain feasible. No policy dominates everywhere, so report false acceptance, abstention, and deadline misses separately across all started tasks (Lecture 13).}

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3cm}
    \item Reasoning models choose how many hidden tokens to spend. Who should set that budget: the model, the runtime, or the user paying for it?
    \item Difficulty is unknown before the attempt. How cheap can a difficulty estimate be and still route the budget well?
    \item When a check becomes the target of search, how often must it be re-measured on selected candidates?
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%

The second asks how cheaply a runtime can estimate task difficulty before committing budget (Example 3.1). The third asks how often checks must be re-measured once search targets them (\S3.4.3, Lecture 13).
