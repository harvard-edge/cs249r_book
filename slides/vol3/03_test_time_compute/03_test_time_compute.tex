\begin{frame}{One 4,096-token ceiling yields three latencies and three bills}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.95\textwidth,height=4.3cm,keepaspectratio]{alloc_step}{2}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Breadth: 4 streams of 1,024 tokens. Feedback: 3 rounds, each with a 1.5 s check.}\\[0.05cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Breadth pays \textbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{1.5$\times$}} depth's bill: 12,288 extra prompt tokens, read once per stream.}
  \mlsyscredit{Test-Time Compute, Napkin Math 3.2 and table 2 (illustrative: \$3 and \$15 per million input and output tokens, no cache discount).}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
  Napkin Math 3.2 compares all three axes given a 4,096-token prompt, a 4,096-token output ceiling, 21.075 ms/token decode, and \$3/\$15 per million input/output tokens with no cache discount. Step 1 compares latency: depth decodes 4,096 tokens serially in 86.3 s; breadth runs 4 parallel 1,024-token streams in 21.6 s (sharing weight reads across the batch); feedback runs 3 rounds of 1,024 tokens plus three 1.5 s checks in 69.2 s. Step 2 compares spend: depth costs \$0.074, feedback costs \$0.095 (resending 16,380 cumulative input tokens by round 3), and breadth costs \$0.111 (1.5$\times$ depth) because each stream reads its own 4,096-token prompt (+12,288 extra input tokens).

  \notecue{Watch for}{Matching output ceilings never implies equal latency, memory, or spend, and only feedback acquires a new fact. Prefix caching eliminates most of breadth's input premium (Lecture 5).}

  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Snell et al. (2024) sorted MATH problems into five difficulty bins by a base model's pass@1 rate across 2,048 samples and compared allocation strategies.
