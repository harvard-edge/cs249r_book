  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
  Definition 1.2 defines a trajectory $\tau = (s_0, a_0, o_1, \dots, s_H)$ as the ordered record of states, proposed actions, runtime decisions, and observations across $H$ turns from initial context to acceptance, stop, or escalation. Walk through turns 11 to 16 of the real \texttt{astropy} run. First, turn 11 reproduces the crash and turn 13 applies a one-line \code{sed} edit in the container. \begin{frame}{The consequence in its environment sets an action's authority}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.4cm,keepaspectratio]{authority.pdf}
  \mlsyscredit{Introduction, \S1.4 and Example 1.1; Lighthouse 1.4 for the seat hold and refund.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
  Read the four side-effect classes from bottom to top. Level $A_0$ reads data only, though reading a secret can still leak it through model output. Level $A_1$ mutates a disposable workspace where tearing down the copy undoes every edit. Level $A_2$ mutates external systems paired with an executable compensating action under checked preconditions, such as releasing a support seat hold. Level $A_3$ lacks automated reversal, such as paying out a refund or deleting unversioned production records. That explains the opening cleanup puzzle: the same delete command is $A_1$ in a clone and $A_3$ on an unbacked workspace.

  \notecue{Watch for}{Classify actions by consequence in the target environment rather than by command verb; even \code{git push} shifts between $A_2$ and $A_3$ depending on branch protection and who has already pulled.}

\begin{frame}{A fail-plausible fault looks like a success}

  \mlsyscredit{After Introduction, \S1.5.1 margin figure; Schlichting and Schneider 1983; Lamport et al. 1982; Avizienis et al. 2004.}
  \note{%
  \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
