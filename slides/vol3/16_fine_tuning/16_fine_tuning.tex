\begin{frame}{The loss mask trains one author and conditions on the other}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{mask_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Fine-Tuning, figure 1 and Definition 16.1, redrawn at slide size (simplified: the schema query e3 and its reply are omitted).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Loss masking (Definition 16.1, equation 1) averages $-\log \pi_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ strictly over positions where $m_t = 1$ while keeping all other tokens in the input. First, Step 1 separates the serialized record into three author lanes (supplied, policy e2/e4/e6, tools). Next, Step 2 sets $m_t = 1$ only on e4 and e6 and $m_t = 0$ everywhere else, including model-written e2 (curation chooses targets, not authorship). Finally, Step 3 shows attention reading masked tokens so gradients from e4 and e6 update weights encoding the refusal without training the model to emit e2 or deleting e2 from context.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Training unmasked causes role confusion: the model learns tool outputs continue its own stream, generates fake tool replies past its call, and wastes output budget.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 10 diagnoses a silent off-by-one bug caused by shifting training labels twice.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why does every target sit one token late?}
  \mlsysactivity{Diagnose}{2 min}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      {\footnotesize\textbf{transformers 5.7.0, loss/loss\_utils.py (excerpt)}}\par\vspace{0.05cm}
      \begin{logblock}[9.5]
        \ind{4}\# Shift so that tokens < n predict n\\
        \ind{4}labels = pad(labels, (0, 1),\\
        \ind{8}value=\textcolor{BlueLine}{\textbf{ignore\_index}})\\
        \ind{4}\textcolor{RedLine}{\textbf{shift\_labels = labels[..., 1:]}}
      \end{logblock}
      \vspace{0.3cm}
      {\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}%
      \begin{tabular}{@{}l*{4}{c}@{}}
        position & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4\\
        labels & \texttt{a} & \texttt{b} & \texttt{c} & \texttt{d}\\
        \textcolor{RedLine}{\textbf{shift\_labels}} & \textcolor{RedLine}{\texttt{b}} & \textcolor{RedLine}{\texttt{c}} & \textcolor{RedLine}{\texttt{d}} & \textcolor{BlueLine}{$-100$}
      \end{tabular}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.39\textwidth}
      \small
      Position $t$ is scored on token $t+1$; $-100$ means no loss.\\[0.3cm]
      A loader that also shifts trains every target one token late.\\[0.3cm]
      \uncover<2->{\textbf{Double shift:} the library already shifts. Shift once, and check on a short example.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Source: github.com/huggingface/transformers, src/transformers/loss/loss\_utils.py, release 5.7.0 (Apache-2.0), ForCausalLMLoss, three lines excerpted; nn.functional shortened. Trajectory Fine-Tuning, \S16.4.2 and table 4.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    This real excerpt from transformers 5.7.0 (loss\_utils.py, ForCausalLMLoss) illustrates two mechanics: ignore\_index $-100$ implements the mask, and shift\_labels = labels[..., 1:] shifts labels by one inside the loss because logits at $t-1$ predict token $t$. Step 2 reveals the double-shift bug when a loader also shifts: every target and mask shifts one token late across the corpus, shifting the mask onto the first token of each observation instead of the stop token while training loss still decreases smoothly.

    \notecue{Check}{Give pairs one minute to diagnose a loader that pre-shifts labels. Because the library shifts internally, shift in exactly one place and decode a short synthetic batch before launching.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{Keep each selected turn's authentic stop token at $m_t = 1$; dropping it makes the model generate past its call until hitting the output limit.}

    \notecue{Optional}{For a 50-minute class, skip this slide and move straight to target dilution on Slide 11.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 11 quantifies how heavily an unmasked loss dilutes gradient weight on true targets.}
  }
\end{frame}

    Trajectory distillation trains a cheaper student on verified trajectories from a stronger or more heavily prompted teacher, taking two forms: context distillation (strip a long procedural prompt and train the student to act from a short prompt) and teacher distillation (filter verified API-teacher samples, since APIs omit full logits). Exercise 8 prices the support agent's 3,000-token checklist across 4 turns and 2 million monthly sessions (24 billion tokens at \$2/M = \$48,000/month): monthly redistillation costs \$15,000, netting \$33,000/month (vanishing at 3.2 revisions/month). Step 2 applies 10 percent prefix caching, cutting checklist cost to \$4,800/month so distillation loses \$10,200/month. Lecture 19 generalizes adaptation payback.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Measure latency gains closed-loop; a single extra recovery turn erases prefill savings.}

\begin{frame}{LoRA trains two thin matrices beside each frozen one}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Low-rank adaptation (LoRA, Hu et al. 2021; Napkin Math 16.5) freezes base weights $W_0$ and trains thin factor matrices $B$ ($d_{\text{out}} \times r$, initialized to zero so training starts at the base model) and $A$ ($r \times d_{\text{in}}$), scaled by $\alpha/r$, adding $r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}})$ parameters per adapted matrix. Adapting two $4{,}096 \times 4{,}096$ matrices at $r = 16$ across 32 layers adds 8,388,608 parameters (0.10 percent of 8 billion). Right: running PEFT 0.21.2 on Meta-Llama-3-8B's config.json (meta device, targeting q\_proj and o\_proj) confirms exactly 8,388,608 trainable parameters (0.1044 percent of 8.04 billion).
