\begin{frame}{A trace ties every span of one request to its parent}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.66\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.9cm,keepaspectratio]{dapper_fig2.png}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \small A \textbf{span}: one unit of work with an id, a parent id, a start, and an end.\\[0.2cm]
      Children can overlap; the parent encloses them.\\[0.2cm]
      \textcolor{mlsysaccent}{\textbf{In an agent:}} each model call, tool run, and check is a span of one trial.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Sigelman et al., ``Dapper, a Large-Scale Distributed Systems Tracing Infrastructure,'' Google Technical Report dapper-2010-1, 2010, Figure 2.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Google's Dapper paper (Sigelman et al. 2010, Figure 2) established modern distributed tracing, carried forward by OpenTelemetry. One front-end request expands into five spans across servers, each recording a name, span ID, parent ID, and start and end timestamps on a shared axis. Because Span 3 (Backend.DoSomething, spanning roughly 24 to 30) invokes Spans 4 and 5 concurrently (about 4 units each), summing child durations exceeds the parent's 6-unit wall time; overlapping children cannot be summed. In an agent, every model call, tool invocation, and check becomes a span of one trial.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Timestamps from different hosts require clock alignment before comparison; apparent overlap can be clock skew rather than concurrency (connecting to Lecture 11's happened-before order).}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 7 replays Napkin Math 14.1 through the real OpenTelemetry SDK to isolate the critical path.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three records carry three different guarantees}

  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{records_step}{3}

    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    A single runtime event often writes to three distinct records sharing an operation ID, each with its own guarantee. First, Step 1 shows the diagnostic trace for timing and debugging, which may legitimately sample, truncate payloads, or drop spans under load. \begin{frame}{Tracing every request cost Google search 16\% in latency}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.88\textwidth,height=4.4cm,keepaspectratio]{dapper_bars.pdf}\\[0.1cm]
  {\small Dapper's first production version kept about 1 trace in 1,024 requests.}
  \mlsyscredit{Sigelman et al., ``Dapper,'' Google Technical Report dapper-2010-1, 2010, Table 2 (web search cluster; measurement error 2.5\% for latency) and \S4.4.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Google measured Dapper's overhead on its production web search cluster across sampling rates (Sigelman et al. 2010, Table 2). Tracing every request added 16.3 percent to average latency; 1 in 16 added 2.12 percent; and 1 in 1,024 changed latency by $-0.20$ percent, inside the 2.5 percent measurement error, while throughput stayed within 1.48 percent. Early Dapper therefore kept roughly 1 trace in 1,024, which worked for high-volume search where bugs recurred thousands of times, yet misses rare failures in an agent fleet running only a few thousand trials per day.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Unlike 2010 web RPCs, agent traces carry large prompts and tool outputs, so storage capacity usually limits retention before CPU overhead does.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 12 asks students to compute how often 5 percent head sampling misses a failure occurring 20 times.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimate: does a 5\% sample keep a rare failure?}
  \mlsysactivity{Estimate}{2 min}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.36\textwidth}
      \small \textbf{Head sampling} decides at the start of a trial, before its outcome.\\[0.15cm]
      P(no trace of a mode seen $n$ times)\\[0.05cm]
      $= (1-p)^{n}$\\[0.2cm]
      {\normalsize A mode occurs 20 times at $p = 5\%$.}\\[0.15cm]
      \uncover<2->{$0.95^{20} = \mathbf{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{36\%}}$ chance of no trace}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.62\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{miss_step}{2}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Operating Agents, \S14.3.1, Purpose, and Checkpoint 14.2. Derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Head sampling chooses independently with probability $p$ at trial start before the outcome is known, missing a failure mode occurring $n$ times with probability $(1-p)^{n}$. First, Step 1 marks two anchors: at 1 percent, a mode seen 10 times leaves zero traces 90 percent of the time; at 10 percent, a mode seen 5 times leaves nothing 59 percent of the time (requiring 50 occurrences to reach 0.5 percent). Then, Step 2 solves Checkpoint 14.2 at $p = 5\%$ and $n = 20$: $0.95^{20} = 36\%$ chance of zero traces.

    \notecue{Check}{Give two minutes to compute $(1-0.05)^{20} = 36\%$. Intuitive guesses underestimate how often uniform pre-outcome sampling leaves engineers with zero examples of a recurring failure.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{Head sampling remains valid for population means when inclusion probabilities are logged, yet fails on the rare incidents postmortems need.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 13 introduces tail selection, which buffers traces until the verdict arrives.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tail selection waits for the verdict before it keeps a trace}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{tail_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Redrawn from Operating Agents, fig.~1 and Table 3 (\S14.3.2; Explore: Tail Sampling Pipeline on mlsysbook.ai).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Tail selection waits for evidence before committing trace storage. First, Step 1 splits ingestion into two paths: every admitted trial enters the accounting record directly (protecting the denominator), while trace bodies wait in a staging buffer across the verdict window (hours for slow regressions). Next, Step 2 applies strict precedence so no trace is saved twice: failed criteria and policy violations first, pending trials provisionally, slow or costly outliers next, and a 2 percent random control sample of ordinary trials. Finally, Step 3 handles burst pressure by evicting ordinary bodies from the bottom first while logging any early drop in the accounting record. Because kept traces enrich failures, population rates still come from the accounting record or inverse-probability weights.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Size staging buffers for outage bursts of long, tool-heavy runs rather than steady averages.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 14 maps this pipeline onto real OpenTelemetry Collector tail-sampling configuration.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tail selection keeps every failure for 14.5\texorpdfstring{$\times$}{x} less storage}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=3.4cm,keepaspectratio]{retention.pdf}\\[0.15cm]
  \small 10,000 trials an hour, 1.4 MB each: 400 failed, 100 pending, 9,500 ordinary.\\[0.08cm]
  \uncover<2->{Floor: failed and pending, \textbf{16.8 GB} a day. Each point of control sampling adds \textbf{3.2 GB}.}
  \mlsyscredit{Operating Agents, Napkin Math 14.2 (\S14.3.2; hypothetical workload, decimal GB, 24 h). Derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Napkin Math 14.2 models 10,000 trials per hour at 1.4 MB each (336 GB/day for full retention): 400 failed, 100 pending, and 9,500 ordinary. Head sampling at 5 percent keeps 500 traces per hour (16.8 GB/day) but expects only 20 of 400 failures and 5 of 100 pending with no guarantee. Tail selection keeps all 400 failed, all 100 pending, and 2 percent of ordinary trials (190), totaling 690 traces per hour or 23.184 GB/day, 14.5 times less than full retention while guaranteeing failure capture. Step 2 splits storage into two terms: a 16.8 GB/day floor set by failures plus pending (which triples if failures triple during an outage) plus 3.2 GB/day per percentage point of control sampling.

    \notecue{Watch for}{These totals count raw payloads only; indexes, replication, accounting records, and ingest buffers add overhead.}
