\begin{frame}{The serial part caps speedup; overhead grows per worker}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.26\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=4.0cm,keepaspectratio]{amdahl.jpg}\\[0.04cm]
      {\footnotesize Gene Amdahl, 2008}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.71\textwidth}
      \centering
      {\large $\text{Speedup}(N)=\dfrac{1}{(1-p)+\dfrac{p}{N}+h(N)}$}\\[0.35cm]
      \small\raggedright
      $p$: the fraction of the work that divides among $N$ workers\\[0.08cm]
      $h(N)$: \textbf{coordination tax}, the time spent on handoffs, as a fraction of the serial time\\[0.08cm]
      With $h(N)=0$: \textbf{Amdahl's law}, capped at $1/(1-p)$\\[0.12cm]
      \uncover<2->{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{\textbf{Any gain needs $h(N) < p\,(1-1/N)$.}}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: Perry Kivolowitz, CC BY 3.0, Wikimedia Commons. Multi-Agent Coordination, equation 1 and Definition 18.1; Amdahl 1967.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Those explicit red boundaries add elapsed overhead alongside work that cannot parallelize. In 1967, computer architect Gene Amdahl argued in prose that serial housekeeping caps parallel speedup regardless of processor count. For agents, planning, integration, and final checks remain the serial fraction $(1-p)$, capping ideal speedup at $1/(1-p)$, or 5$\times$ at $p = 0.8$. Equation 1 (Definition 18.1) adds coordination tax $h(N)$, covering envelope preparation, context delivery, child scheduling, receipt collection, and result reconciliation, normalized to serial time. The highlighted inequality gives the break-even rule, showing that splitting beats the serial reference only when $h(N) < p(1 - 1/N)$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Equation 1 compares against a purely sequential partition. Because a capable single controller already overlaps tools, production choices must benchmark against that real controller.}

    \notecue{Next}{Next, Slide 8 plots Napkin Math 18.1 to show how speedup peaks under linear overhead.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Six workers minimize elapsed time here, at 2.3 times}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.1cm,keepaspectratio]{screen.pdf}\\[0.05cm]
  \small Overhead $h(N) = 0.02\,(N-1)$: eight workers take 0.440 of the serial time, six take 0.433.
  \mlsyscredit{Multi-Agent Coordination, Napkin Math 18.1 (\S18.2.2; hypothetical workload). Derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Evaluating Equation 1 on a concrete partition shows how quickly useful fleets peak. Napkin Math 18.1 plots speedup (the reciprocal of normalized elapsed time) for parallel fraction $p = 0.80$ and linear overhead $h(N) = 0.02(N-1)$ (per-worker slope $c = 0.02$). Four workers finish in 0.460 of serial time. Speedup peaks at 6 workers, near $\sqrt{p/c} = 6.3$, cutting elapsed time to 0.433 of serial time for a 2.3$\times$ speedup. Eight workers slow back to 0.440 as overhead outweighs shrinking parallel gains. Halving overhead to 0.01 moves the peak to 9 workers and lifts speedup only to 2.7$\times$. Only shrinking the serial fraction moves the 5$\times$ ceiling.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Do not misread a flat peak around the optimum as harmless. Past the peak, every extra worker spends tokens while making completion slower.}

    \notecue{Next}{Next, on Slide 9, estimate whether halving coordination overhead or halving serial work buys more speedup.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Past its peak, each added worker lowers capacity}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.62\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{usl.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.36\textwidth}
      \uncover<2->{\small\textbf{Universal scalability law:}\\[0.05cm]
      $\dfrac{N}{1+\sigma(N-1)+\kappa N(N-1)}$\\[0.15cm]
      $\sigma$: work that serializes\\
      $\kappa$: pairwise reconciliation\\[0.15cm]
      Repair: $\sigma = 0.20$, $\kappa = 0.02$\\
      Research: $\sigma = 0.10$, $\kappa = 0.002$}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Multi-Agent Coordination, Theorem 18.1 and Lighthouse 18.1 (stylized coefficients); Gunther 2008. Derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%
    In 2008, Neil Gunther derived the universal scalability law from queueing models (Theorem 18.1, Lighthouse 18.1), giving the mechanism behind Slide 8's declining curve. Dividing top and bottom by $N$ yields $1/[\sigma + (1-\sigma)/N + \kappa(N-1)]$, so pairwise coherency $\kappa$ across $N(N-1)$ ordered pairs generates linear overhead beside serial contention $\sigma$ (planning and the integration queue), bending capacity down past $\sqrt{(1-\sigma)/\kappa}$. The violet repair curve ($\sigma = 0.20, \kappa = 0.02$) matches that 6-worker, 2.3$\times$ peak and runs slower than one worker from $N = 41$ onward (0.981). The green research curve ($\sigma = 0.10, \kappa = 0.002$) peaks at 21 workers and 5.5$\times$, a 3.5$\times$ wider fan-out. Cutting coherency, such as landing the interface first, shifts the repair peak right.

    \notecue{Watch for}{These coefficients are stylized. In practice, fit them across small counts (1, 2, 4) and treat extrapolation as a hypothesis.}

    \notecue{Teach}{Cut this slide first in a 50-minute class. When skipping it, note that pairwise reconciliation bends capacity downward past a peak, and move straight to Slide 11.}

      \begin{logblock}[7]
        For economic viability, multi-agent systems require tasks where the value of the task is high enough to pay for the increased performance.
      \end{logblock}
    \end{column}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Elapsed speedup is only half the decision, because added workers also multiply token spend. Using Slide 2's 15-times ratio, Napkin Math 18.6 plots where delegated cost per accepted task equals a single agent's. Under token-proportional spend, delegated-to-single cost per accepted task equals $m \cdot p_{\text{single}}/p_{\text{multi}}$, with $m = 15/4 = 3.75$. Breaking even requires $p_{\text{multi}} = 3.75\,p_{\text{single}}$, hitting $p_{\text{multi}} = 100\%$ at $p_{\text{single}} = 26.7\%$. To the right of 26.7 percent, delegation cannot win on token cost. For example, a single agent already accepting 60 percent of migrations would demand 225 percent delegated acceptance. In that shaded region, delegation can be justified only by deadline or coverage value, matching Hadfield et al.'s quote. Lecture 19 generalizes this ratio across full task cohorts.

\begin{frame}{Dependencies, messages, and ownership are three graphs}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=5.4cm,keepaspectratio]{structures_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Multi-Agent Coordination, \S18.3 (S supervisor, I interface worker, C caller worker, T test container).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Routing every worker through one supervisor does not erase dependencies between their edits or clarify who cleans up their containers. On the same nodes of migration\_task\_01, the runtime must keep three graphs separate. First, the dependency graph records causal precedence, where the caller patch depends on the interface change and tests depend on the caller patch. Second, the communication topology records message routes. Both workers message only supervisor S and never message each other, yet the dependency edge between them still holds. Third, the supervision tree records lifecycle ownership, where S owns both workers and caller C owns test container T.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Treating the message graph as the dependency graph publishes caller patches against stale interfaces, while treating it as ownership leaks orphaned containers on cancel.}

\begin{frame}{A credit window caps the queue and its staleness}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.37\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=3.4cm,keepaspectratio]{kanban2.jpg}\\[0.04cm]
      {\footnotesize A kanban board: each column holds at most its limit}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.60\textwidth}
      \small Producer: 5 diffs/s. Reviewer: 500 tokens at 40 tokens/s, $\mu =$ \textbf{0.08}/s.\\[0.1cm]
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=3.0cm,keepaspectratio]{credit.pdf}\\[0.05cm]
      \raggedright\footnotesize Free + outstanding credits $= W$. Little's law: residence $\approx W/\mu$.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Board: Dr Ian Mitchell, CC BY-SA 2.5, Wikimedia Commons. Multi-Agent Coordination, \S18.7.4 (hypothetical rates). Derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Fan-out also creates rate mismatches between fast producers and slow model consumers. Consider a producer emitting 5 diffs per second while a reviewer writes 500-token critiques at 40 tokens per second, a service rate $\mu = 0.08$ diffs/s. Over ten unbounded minutes, 2,952 diffs pile up, waiting 10.2 hours while the repository changes underneath them, recreating the stale observations that Lecture 4's context invalidation guards against. Per-agent token caps do nothing because each diff is cheap. A credit window, mirroring factory kanban limits and Lecture 15's collection credits, holds free plus outstanding credits $= W$ and applies backpressure by pausing the producer when credits run out. By Lecture 5's Little's law, a saturated window of $W = 8$ bounds mean residence to $W/\mu = 8/0.08 = 100\text{ s}$, and staying under one minute takes $W = 4$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Cancellation, status, and settlement messages must bypass data credits so a full window never deadlocks control signals.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 32 shows how generation counters cancel an entire subtree at admission while in-flight effects still settle.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cancellation closes admission; admitted work must still settle}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{cancel_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Multi-Agent Coordination, \S18.7.1, \S18.8.2, and Tables 7 and 8.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    When a supervisor aborts a task or hits a deadline, subtree cancellation separates stopping new admissions from settling admitted effects. Initially, every grant binds to every ancestor's generation counter (migration task generation 7), while the interface worker's test job is already admitted at the gateway (the enforcement point mediating external calls). Canceling the task advances generation to 8. Because the gateway checks every ancestor's generation at admission, the caller's next call rejects immediately even before the worker hears of cancellation. The already-admitted test stays owned with its operation ID and reservation until settled, applying Lecture 10's settlement rule across trees and Lecture 12's cleanup graph under separate admin credentials. That refutes freeing reservations at cancel request, as Slide 36 shows when failed and canceled work costs \$6 of \$18.

    \notecue{Watch for}{A partitioned remote gateway must fail closed (deny admissions) or declare a bounded stale-policy window, the maximum interval a cached policy may still admit a revoked grant.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 33 enforces hop-by-hop authority attenuation and partitioned budgets across the delegation tree.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Each hop narrows authority; allowances are reserved}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.65cm,keepaspectratio]{atten_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  \small Child grant $\subseteq$ parent grant. Permissions can be shared; money and tokens are partitioned.
  \mlsyscredit{Multi-Agent Coordination, \S18.8.1 and Exercise 7 (allowances); Saltzer and Schroeder 1975; Birgisson et al. 2014.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Generation counters revoke a tree, while attenuated delegation bounds what active descendants may do at each hop. Extending Lecture 8's capability grants and least privilege (Saltzer and Schroeder, 1975), runtimes can enforce narrowing with Lecture 8's macaroons (Birgisson et al., 2014, chained-caveat tokens that let a holder append restrictions via a keyed hash chain it cannot unwind). Down the tree, permissions narrow from root (read, edit, publish) to caller (edit callers) to inspection child (read one file), while budgets partition because every dollar spends once; Slide 34 computes how the root allowance splits, and the inspection child draws from its caller's share. The runtime also blocks a Lecture 7 confused deputy, where a privileged runtime is steered to misuse authority for a lower-privilege requester. If the inspection child claims the root as parent to grab publication rights, the runtime resolves authority strictly from runtime-assigned lineage. Any worker needing publication escalates to the task owner bound to the exact candidate hash under Lecture 9 action-bound approval, rechecked against lineage at dispatch.
