\begin{frame}{The serial part caps speedup; overhead grows per worker}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.26\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=4.0cm,keepaspectratio]{amdahl.jpg}\\[0.04cm]
      {\footnotesize Gene Amdahl, 2008}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.71\textwidth}
      \centering
      {\large $\text{Speedup}(N)=\dfrac{1}{(1-p)+\dfrac{p}{N}+c(N)}$}\\[0.35cm]
      \small\raggedright
      $p$: the fraction of the work that divides among $N$ workers\\[0.08cm]
      $c(N)$: \textbf{coordination tax}, the time spent on handoffs, as a fraction of the serial time\\[0.08cm]
      With $c=0$: \textbf{Amdahl's law}, capped at $1/(1-p)$\\[0.12cm]
      \uncover<2->{\textcolor{mlsysaccent}{\textbf{Any gain needs $c(N) < p\,(1-1/N)$.}}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Photo: Perry Kivolowitz, CC BY 3.0, Wikimedia Commons. Multi-Agent Coordination, equation 1 and Definition 18.1; Amdahl 1967.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Gene Amdahl argued in 1967 that serial housekeeping limits parallel speedup; for agents, planning, integration, and final checks remain serial fraction $(1-p)$, capping ideal speedup at $1/(1-p)$ (5$\times$ at $p = 0.8$). Equation 1 (Definition 18.1) adds coordination tax $c(N)$: preparing envelopes, delivering context, scheduling children, collecting receipts, and reconciling results, normalized to serial time. Step 2 derives the break-even inequality: splitting beats the serial reference only when $c(N) < p(1 - 1/N)$.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Equation 1 compares against a purely sequential partition; because a capable single controller already overlaps tools, production choices must benchmark against that real controller.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 8 plots Napkin Math 18.1 to find the worker count that minimizes elapsed time under linear overhead.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Six workers minimize elapsed time here, at 2.3 times}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.1cm,keepaspectratio]{screen.pdf}\\[0.05cm]
  \small Overhead $c(N) = 0.02\,(N-1)$: eight workers take 0.440 of the serial time, six take 0.433.
  \mlsyscredit{Multi-Agent Coordination, Napkin Math 18.1 (\S18.2.2; hypothetical workload). Derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Napkin Math 18.1 plots elapsed time for $p = 0.80$ and per-worker overhead $c(N) = 0.02(N-1)$. Four workers finish in 0.460 of serial time; elapsed time reaches its minimum at 6 workers (near $\sqrt{p/c} = 6.3$) in 0.433 of serial time, a 2.3$\times$ speedup; eight workers slow back up to 0.440 as overhead outweighs shrinking parallel gains. Halving overhead to 0.01 moves the optimum to 9 workers and lifts speedup only to 2.7$\times$; only shrinking the serial fraction moves the 5$\times$ ceiling.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Do not misread a flat valley around the optimum as harmless; past the peak, every extra worker spends tokens while making completion slower.}

\begin{frame}{Past its peak, each added worker lowers capacity}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.62\textwidth}

      $\dfrac{N}{1+\sigma(N-1)+\kappa N(N-1)}$\\[0.15cm]
      $\sigma$: work that serializes\\
      $\kappa$: pairwise reconciliation\\[0.15cm]
      Repair: $\sigma = 0.20$, $\kappa = 0.02$\\
      Research: $\sigma = 0.10$, $\kappa = 0.002$}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Optional}%

\begin{frame}{A 3.75 times token multiple pays only on hard tasks}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.53\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{breakeven.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \small Cost per accepted task, delegated over single:\\[0.05cm]
      {\normalsize $m \cdot \dfrac{p_{\text{single}}}{p_{\text{multi}}}$}, \quad $m = 15/4 = 3.75$\\[0.2cm]
      \begin{logblock}[7]
        For economic viability, multi-agent systems require tasks where the value of the task is high enough to pay for the increased performance.
      \end{logblock}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Multi-Agent Coordination, Napkin Math 18.6 (\S18.9.2). Excerpt: Hadfield et al., Anthropic Engineering, 2025. Derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Napkin Math 18.6 answers the opening hook directly. Under token-proportional spend, delegated-to-single cost per accepted task equals $m \cdot p_{\text{single}}/p_{\text{multi}}$, with $m = 15/4 = 3.75$. Breaking even requires $p_{\text{multi}} = 3.75\,p_{\text{single}}$, hitting $p_{\text{multi}} = 100\%$ at $p_{\text{single}} = 26.7\%$. To the right of 26.7 percent (for example, a single agent already accepting 60 percent of migrations would demand 225 percent delegated acceptance), delegation cannot win on token cost and can be justified only by deadline or coverage value, matching Hadfield et al.'s quote. Lecture 19 generalizes this into cohort economics.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The 4$\times$ and 15$\times$ multiples describe one reported research system; measure your own workload's token ratio.}

\begin{frame}{Cancellation closes admission; admitted work must still settle}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.94\textwidth,height=5.7cm,keepaspectratio]{cancel_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Multi-Agent Coordination, \S18.7.1, \S18.8.2, and Tables 7 and 8.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Subtree cancellation separates stopping new admissions from settling admitted effects. First, Step 1 binds every grant to every ancestor's generation counter (migration task generation 7), while the interface worker's test job is already admitted at the gateway. Next, Step 2 cancels the task by advancing generation to 8: because the gateway checks every ancestor's generation at admission, the caller's next call rejects immediately even before the worker learns of cancellation. Finally, Step 3 keeps the already-admitted test owned with its operation ID and reservation until settled (Lecture 10 across trees; Lecture 12 cleanup graph under separate admin credentials), refuting the fallacy of freeing reservations at cancel request (failed and canceled work was \$6 of \$18 in Slide 36's cohort).
