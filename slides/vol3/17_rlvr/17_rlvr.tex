\begin{frame}{RLVR scores sampled attempts with a program, then updates}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{loop_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{After Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards, \S17.2.1 and definition 17.1. Rewards illustrative.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Before inspecting how checks break, fix the basic training loop. Definition 17.1 defines RLVR as policy optimization whose reward comes from an automated check, such as tests, a compiler, or a proof checker, rather than human preference ratings or a learned reward model (a neural network trained to score outputs). For one task, the loop first samples a group of attempts, each inside its own isolated sandbox. Once each run ends, a terminal binary verifier assigns 1 for pass and 0 for fail. The weight update then raises token probabilities in rewarded attempts and lowers them in unrewarded ones, relying on outcome contrast across attempts rather than a recorded expert.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Raising measured reward improves Lecture 1's cost per accepted task only when the reward truly tracks real task acceptance.}

\begin{frame}{A task the policy never solves gives zero signal}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.5cm,keepaspectratio]{groups.pdf}\\[0.15cm]
  {\small A group of $G$ at pass rate $p$ holds a pass with probability $1 - (1-p)^G$; at $p = 0$, none.}
  \mlsyscredit{Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards, \S17.2.3 and equation 2. Coefficients computed.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Even with a working checker in code, RLVR learns only when sampled attempts produce different outcomes. To turn binary scores into signed updates, the optimizer subtracts a baseline (a reference score, here the group's mean reward) so green coefficients push token probabilities up and red ones push them down across a group of 8 attempts. When the policy never solves the task ($p = 0$), every reward and coefficient is 0, moving zero weights regardless of rollout spend; even a fixed positive baseline pushes all failures down alike without preferring any attempt. When 2 of 8 pass, winners rise and failures fall. When 8 of 8 pass, rewards tie again with zero gradient. Teams lift hard tasks off zero by starting from a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) checkpoint, stepping up from easier tasks (a curriculum), or rewarding verified milestones.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Identical zero rewards collapse eight completely different failure modes into one undifferentiated signal.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 11 tests a real pytest exploit where one added file turns a failing connection-pool test into reward 1.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Shaping terms must keep every pass above every failure}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{shaping_step}{2}\\[0.15cm]
  {\normalsize $R = c + \alpha f - \lambda\,\text{len}$, with $c \in \{0,1\}$ and $f, \text{len} \in [0,1]$: \ order holds if $\alpha + \lambda < 1$.}
  \mlsyscredit{Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards, \S17.3.3; Exercise 2 weights. Derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
    Shaped rewards add auxiliary format ($f \in [0,1]$) and length ($\text{len} \in [0,1]$) terms to binary outcome $c \in \{0,1\}$ via $R = c + \alpha f - \lambda\,\text{len}$. Passes earn at least $1-\lambda$ and failures at most $\alpha$, so passes outrank failures only if $\alpha + \lambda < 1$ under an enforced cap ($\text{len} \le 1$). Weights $\alpha = 0.2, \lambda = 0.3$ keep them apart, whereas Exercise 2 ($\alpha = 0.4, \lambda = 0.7$, sum 1.1) drops the lowest pass (0.30) below the best failure (0.40), even when a 7,200-token pass (0.570) beats a 400-token fail (0.365). Segment 1 shows that rewards measure only covered checks, false accepts grow when passes are rare, and optimization exploits every gap.

\begin{frame}{GRPO replaces the critic with a group of rollouts}
  \begin{columns}[c]

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Avoiding fragile critics and costly forks leads to two dominant policy optimizers. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), introduced by Schulman et al. (2017) with a clipped objective replacing costly trust-region constraints, trains a critic for per-token baselines and can run one rollout per task. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO, Shao et al. 2024, DeepSeekMath) drops the critic, sampling $G$ rollouts per task and broadcasting one normalized coefficient $\hat{A}_i = (r_i - \text{mean}(r))/(\text{std}(r) + \epsilon)$ to every sampled token of rollout $i$. On AIME 2024, DeepSeek-R1-Zero lifted pass@1 from 15.6 to Lecture 3's 71.0 percent (86.7 percent with majority-vote cons@64). Both keep a frozen starting reference policy and clip new-to-old token probability ratios to curb drift.

\begin{frame}{Symmetric clipping collapses entropy; a higher ceiling holds it}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.64\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}
        \node[inner sep=0] (de) {\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{dapo_entropy.png}};

          \node[fill=white, font=\small\bfseries, text=teal, inner sep=1pt] at (0.52,0.36) {symmetric clip};
        \end{scope}
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.34\textwidth}
      \small At $\pm$0.2, a rare token at 0.01 may rise only \textbf{0.002}.\\[0.3cm]
      Lost diversity $\rightarrow$ groups tie $\rightarrow$ no gradient.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Yu et al. 2025, DAPO, arXiv 2503.14476, Figure 2(b) (Qwen2.5-32B, AIME training). Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards, \S17.8.3. Derived.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Even with an isolated verifier and clean records, training stalls when the policy stops exploring on unsolved tasks. To diagnose that entropy collapse, the DAPO study (Yu et al. 2025, Figure 2b, Qwen2.5-32B on AIME) tracked token entropy (uncertainty across next-token choices) over training. Under symmetric $\pm 0.2$ clipping in teal, entropy drops from about 0.7 to near zero within roughly 1,000 steps, making group samples nearly identical so rewards tie and gradients vanish. The cause is ratio clipping. Capping the probability ratio at 1.2 lets a dominant 0.9 token reach 1.08, leaving it uncapped, while a rare 0.01 token can rise by only 0.002 to 0.012. DAPO's Clip-Higher in purple decouples the bounds, raising only the upper ceiling and sustaining entropy near 0.4.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Low entropy is healthy on fixed syntax or closing brackets. Track tool-call diversity on unsolved tasks, and use Lecture 12's semantic watchdog to stop collapsed loops.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Barriers, staleness, and verifiers bound the rest of the loop}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.98\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{freshness_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{RLVR, eqs. 7 to 9 and \S17.9.3 to \S17.9.5 (illustrative, derived; Explore: Asynchronous Freshness Pipeline on mlsysbook.ai).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Even with fast resets, three downstream limits in equations 7 to 9 still bound the loop. First, synchronous group barriers wait for the slowest member, stranding 65 percent of slot time when a group of 4 finishes between 0.2 and 16.8 s. Second, verifier pool sizing follows Lecture 5's Little's law. Multiplying 32 trajectories/s $\times$ 3 trials $\times$ 8 s yields 768 busy workers, requiring 960 slots at 80 percent target utilization to leave headroom for bursts. Third, asynchronous collection frees slots early and tracks policy-version lag, or staleness. At maximum staleness 2, short rollout A (lag 1) and medium rollout B (lag 2) are admitted, while long hard rollout C, started at v1 and finished at v4 (lag 3), is rejected. That cutoff creates survivorship bias against hard tasks, so teams shorten rollout age rather than blindly loosening staleness.

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    When the loop emits a checkpoint, did RL add capability or only sharpen reliability? In a competitive reasoning study, Yue and colleagues (2025, Table 4) trained Qwen2.5-7B with GRPO for 450 steps on the reasoning dataset Omni-MATH-Train, tracking pass@1 against Lecture 3's pass@256 (at least one pass in 256 samples). Over those steps, pass@1 rose from 9.9 to 42.5 percent, while pass@256 edged down from 67.2 to 64.3 percent. While not universal, this fits RL sharpening probability onto solutions the base model could already sample. Internal fleet promotion therefore stays separate from production release, which requires Lecture 13's matched-budget evaluation and repeated-run consistency ($\text{pass}^k$) on held-out suites training never ran, because training tests become training data. Segment 5 shows that the slowest stage caps loop speed while diversity and held-out outcomes govern health, since reward and length can rise while health falls.

\begin{frame}{Open questions}
  \normalsize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3cm}
    \item A monitor that penalizes detected hacks may teach hacks the monitor cannot see. How should a team use a monitor during training?
    \item Most agent tasks lack an executable check. When can a judge model be the reward, and how often must it be recalibrated as the policy shifts?
    \item If RL mostly sharpens what the base model can already sample, what must change for it to teach new capabilities?
  \end{itemize}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1 min}{Optional}%
    Even when all five takeaways hold, three open questions remain active areas of research. The first asks how to use hack monitors during training without teaching obfuscation that evades the monitor, a reason METR recommends patching scorers rather than penalizing detected hacks (Baker et al. 2025). The second asks when learned judges can stand in where executable checks do not exist and how often they must be recalibrated as the policy shifts. The third asks what allows RL to create new capabilities beyond sharpening base-model reach (Yue et al. 2025).
