\begin{frame}{Triage probes send only a capability gap to training}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{ladder_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Curation, \S15.2.2, figure 1 (redrawn).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    The intervention ladder orders remedies by rising cost and falling reversibility: context, tool and schema, runtime, supervised fine-tuning (imitating chosen demonstrations), and reinforcement learning (maximizing verified reward). First, Step 1 runs a context probe supplying the missing fact; if failure vanishes, fix retrieval or prompt assembly. Next, Step 2 runs schema probes (resuming saved prefixes with corrected tool descriptions) and runtime probes (clean sandboxes with raised limits), applying the end-to-end argument to keep adapter fixes universal across models. Finally, Step 3 sends only failures surviving all three probes to training, where the trained checkpoint must still pass evaluation.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The ladder sets the default investigation order rather than forbidding shortcuts; recorded evidence of a capability gap still justifies moving directly to training.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 9 asks students to calculate the accelerator cost of fine-tuning on all 3,000 hook failures.}

\begin{frame}{Predict: how many tasks leak across the split?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{2 min}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.82\textwidth,height=4.25cm,keepaspectratio]{straddle_step}{2}\\[0.04cm]
  {\small 8 attempts per task. Assign each \emph{run} to training with probability 0.8.}\\[0.08cm]
  {\normalsize\choice{A}{about 20\%}\qquad\choice{B}{about 50\%}\qquad\answerat{2}{C}{about 83\%}}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Curation, \S15.4 and its figure (formula plotted by images/make\_figs.py).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Suppose a collector runs 8 attempts per task and splits individual runs randomly 80/20 into training and test. A task stays clean only when all 8 attempts land on the same side. Step 2 reveals C: all 8 land together with probability $0.8^{8} + 0.2^{8} = 0.168$, so 83.2 percent of tasks straddle both splits (even at 2 attempts, 32 percent straddle). Varying prompts or seeds makes runs look distinct while keeping the underlying task identical, causing split leakage where test scores measure sibling recall rather than generalization (extending Lecture 13's contamination checks into internal curation).

    \notecue{Check}{Vote among A, B, and C before Step 2. C holds because every extra attempt gives another chance to cross the split; A mistakes the 20 percent test fraction for the leakage rate.}

\begin{frame}{A task group keeps every relative on one side}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.62\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=5.5cm,keepaspectratio]{groups_step}{2}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.36\textwidth}
      \small $g(f)$: the group of fixture $f$\\ $s(g)$: the group's partition\\[0.25cm]
      \uncover<2->{\footnotesize $g(f_i)=g(f_j) \Rightarrow s(g(f_i))=s(g(f_j))$\\[0.25cm]
      \small Assign before collection. A test task inspected while debugging moves to development.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Curation, \S15.4.1 and the partition-integrity relation.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Task-group partitioning protects evaluation validity before collection starts. First, Step 1 groups every attempt at one problem, paraphrased prompt, fork copying the same bug, and derived view (such as a redacted trace) into one task group $g(f)$, recording membership reasons in a split manifest. Then, Step 2 assigns whole groups to training, development, or final test ($g(f_i) = g(f_j) \Rightarrow s(g(f_i)) = s(g(f_j))$). If a teammate inspects a final-test failure to craft a training demo, that task group moves to development and a fresh unseen group replaces it. Likewise, held-out tests, reference patches, and verdicts stay off-limits to upstream generators, retrievers, and rankers.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Grouping only by repository name misses vendored copies and forks, while grouping by shared dependencies merges unrelated bugs; Slide 16 screens for unrecorded relatives.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 16 demonstrates MinHash locality-sensitive hashing for spotting near-duplicate patches and hidden task relatives.}

First, Step 1 introduces a defective fixture driving tests to their 120 s timeout: verifier capacity collapses to 4.3/s, unassessed records grow by 18.5/s (147.9 MB/s at an 8 MB ceiling), and an empty 32 GB staging store fills in 216.3 s (3.6 minutes). Then, Step 2 shows stopping dispatch late still overflows because in-flight rollouts hold up to 8.2 GB and 30 s controller lag adds 4.4 GB, requiring halt at or below 60 percent. Borrowing ATM credit flow control (Kung et al. 1994; Lecture 18 extends credits across agents), each rollout reserves one ceiling-sized credit until durable disposition.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Size staging for worst-case timeout collapse rather than average utilization, and charge the record ceiling to bound bursts.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 31 checks the exact moment a collection credit may safely return to the pool.}
  }
\end{frame}
