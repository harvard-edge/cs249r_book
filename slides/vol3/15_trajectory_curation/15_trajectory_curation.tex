\section{Triage}

\begin{frame}{Triage probes send only a capability gap to training}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=5.6cm,keepaspectratio]{ladder_step}{3}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Curation, \S15.2.2, figure 1 (redrawn).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    To separate a model capability gap from an external defect systematically, the intervention ladder orders remedies by rising cost and falling reversibility, moving from context, tool and schema, and runtime to supervised fine-tuning (imitating chosen demonstrations) and reinforcement learning (maximizing verified reward). The first rung runs a context probe that supplies the missing fact, so if the failure vanishes, the fix belongs in retrieval or prompt assembly. Next, schema probes resume saved prefixes with corrected tool descriptions, and runtime probes rerun in clean sandboxes with raised limits. Fixing those lower layers applies the end-to-end argument, placing each fix where it can be implemented completely and correctly so every model benefits. Only failures that survive all three probes move to training, where the resulting checkpoint must still pass evaluation.

    \notecue{Watch for}{The ladder sets a default investigation order rather than forbidding shortcuts. Recorded evidence of a capability gap still justifies moving directly to training.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 9 estimates the accelerator cost of fine-tuning on all 3,000 failures from the opening prediction.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict: how many tasks leak across the split?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{2 min}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.82\textwidth,height=4.25cm,keepaspectratio]{straddle_step}{2}\\[0.04cm]
  {\small 8 attempts per task. Assign each \emph{run} to training with probability 0.8.}\\[0.08cm]
  {\normalsize\choice{A}{about 20\%}\qquad\choice{B}{about 50\%}\qquad\answerat{2}{C}{about 83\%}}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Curation, \S15.4 and its figure (formula plotted by images/make\_figs.py).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Under a random split of runs, split leakage is the default outcome rather than a rare edge case. Suppose a collector runs 8 attempts per task and assigns each run independently to training with probability 0.8 and to test with probability 0.2, an 80/20 split. A task stays clean only when all 8 attempts land on the same side. Before reading on, choose A, B, or C for the share of tasks that straddle both splits.

    \notecue{Check}{The answer is C, about 83 percent. All 8 attempts land together with probability $0.8^{8} + 0.2^{8} = 0.168$, so 83.2 percent of tasks straddle both splits. Even at 2 attempts, 32 percent straddle. Every extra attempt creates another chance to cross the boundary. Varying prompts or random seeds makes runs look distinct while leaving the underlying task identical, causing split leakage where test scores measure sibling recall rather than generalization, extending Lecture 13's pretraining-contamination checks into internal curation. A tempting answer is A, which mistakes the 20 percent test fraction for the leakage rate.}

    \notecue{Teach}{Take a vote among A, B, and C before revealing the curve and answer C.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 14 measures this exact leakage on the real 80,036-run SWE-agent corpus.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Measured: a random run split leaks 89.9\% of issues}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.0cm,keepaspectratio]{corpus_split.pdf}\\[0.15cm]
  {\small Formula, per issue's own run count: 90.3\%. Measured on one seeded split: 89.9\%.}\\[0.08cm]
  \uncover<2->{\small Same repository is fine for ``new issues here''; it breaks a claim about new repositories.}
  \mlsyscredit{nebius/SWE-agent-trajectories, CC BY 4.0; 3,591 issues, 1,276 repositories; split and counts by images/data/corpus\_stats.py (seed 15).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Does that formula hold on real agent data? Testing two split rules across issue and repository boundaries on the 80,036-run nebius/SWE-agent-trajectories corpus with seed 15 in corpus\_stats.py confirms it. First, assigning each run to training with probability 0.8 leaks 3,229 of 3,591 issues, or 89.9 percent. Slide 13's per-issue formula predicts 90.3 percent, exceeding 83 percent because the median issue has 16 runs. Second, assigning whole issues to partitions reduces issue straddling to zero by construction. Yet under that same issue split, 77.4 percent of the 743 test issues still share a repository with training issues. Whether that overlap counts as leakage depends on the evaluation claim. Shared build tools fit ``new issues in existing repositories,'' whereas claims about unseen repositories require holding out whole repository families, as the corpus authors excluded mirrors and forks of benchmark repositories.

  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Curation, \S15.7.2; Shumailov et al., Nature 2024 (model collapse); Sculley et al., NeurIPS 2015 (hidden feedback loops).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Slide 27 showed one filter stripping recovery runs in one round. Suppose a policy trains repeatedly on its own filtered successes while training acceptance climbs. Predict A, B, C, or D.

    \notecue{Check}{The answer is B. Held-out transfer stalls while diversity drops. In their 2015 NeurIPS paper on hidden technical debt in ML systems, Sculley et al. warned of hidden feedback loops, where deployed outputs reshape future training data, and Shumailov et al. Option A is tempting while training acceptance climbs; C reverses reality because filtering drops recovery, and D ignores feedback. Countermeasures pair per-task caps, deduplication, an external anchor, halting rounds early when diversity and transfer flatten, and a separate final test. Segment 4 leaves two rules, keeping mistakes as context and bounding narrowing.}

    \notecue{Watch for}{Lower diversity alone can reflect pruning redundant retries, so read it beside held-out transfer. Schema, fixture, or grader changes can also make records stale immediately.}

\begin{frame}{A verifier stall fills 32 GB of staging in 216 s}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.66\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=4.7cm,keepaspectratio]{staging_step}{2}\\[0.02cm]
      {\footnotesize 1,024 rollouts of 45 s; 512 verifier slots; 8 MB ceiling per record}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.32\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=2.0cm,keepaspectratio]{atm-switch-forerunner.jpg}\\[0.03cm]
      {\footnotesize An ATM network switch. Credit flow control: send only while holding a free buffer slot.}\\[0.1cm]
      \uncover<2->{\footnotesize\textbf{Collection credit:} one per run, at the record ceiling, returned at durable disposition.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Curation, Napkin Math 15.4 (hypothetical collector), figure 4; Kung et al., SIGCOMM 1994. Photo: Barcex, CC BY-SA 3.0, Wikimedia Commons (FORE Systems ForeRunnerLE 25).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    A collector comfortable at average load still overflows when tests stall. Napkin Math 15.4 models 1,024 concurrent 45 s rollouts generating 22.8 records/s by Lecture 5's Little's law, feeding 512 verifier slots of 30.3/s capacity at 0.75 utilization. When a defective fixture drives tests to their 120 s timeout, capacity collapses to 4.3/s. Unassessed records accumulate at 18.5/s, or 147.9 MB/s at an 8 MB ceiling, filling 32 GB of empty staging in 216.3 s (3.6 minutes). Halting late still overflows, because in-flight rollouts hold up to 8.2 GB and 30 s controller lag adds 4.4 GB, forcing a halt at or below 60 percent. In 1994, Kung et al. prevented overflow in asynchronous transfer mode (ATM) network switches by forwarding only while holding a free downstream buffer slot. Borrowing that rule (Lecture 18 extends it across agents), each rollout acquires one ceiling-sized credit at dispatch and keeps it until durable disposition (commit or discard).

    \notecue{Watch for}{Size staging for worst-case timeout collapse rather than average utilization, charging the record ceiling to bound bursts.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 31 tests which lifecycle point reclaims staging safely and why returning credits earlier fails under stalls.}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict: when may a collection credit return?}
  \mlsysactivity{Predict}{1 min}
  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.96\textwidth,height=4.7cm,keepaspectratio]{credit_step}{3}\\[0.08cm]
  {\normalsize\choice{A}{when rollout finishes}\quad\choice{B}{when written to staging}\quad\answerat{3}{C}{at durable commit or discard}}
  \mlsyscredit{Trajectory Curation, \S15.8.2 and Napkin Math 15.4 (Explore: Collection Pipeline on mlsysbook.ai).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2 min}{Core}%
    Holding a credit until durable disposition sounds straightforward, yet choosing the wrong lifecycle hook breaks backpressure during a stall. Each rollout acquires an 8 MB collection credit before starting, then passes through rollout, the 32 GB staging store, and the verifier pool. Before reading on, identify which point (A, B, or C) actually reclaims staging and why returning the credit earlier overflows.

    \notecue{Check}{The answer is C, at durable commit or discard. Only durable disposition frees staging bytes, so holding the credit until then drains the pool automatically during a verifier stall and halts new dispatch before staging fills. A tempting answer is B, written to staging, which confuses writing bytes to staging with downstream reclamation. Returning a credit at A (rollout finish) or B frees credits while unassessed bytes still occupy the 32 GB store, letting a stall keep dispatching until overflow.}

    \notecue{Teach}{Give 45 seconds to vote among A, B, and C before revealing the stall comparison and answer C.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 32 specifies the lineage envelope every derived or redacted record must carry.}
  }
\end{frame}

    Credits bound staging during collection, yet auditability months later also demands knowing which run, grader version, license, and redaction produced every training record. \begin{frame}{Takeaways}

  \thumbline{thumb_typology_step3.pdf}{A recorded mistake stays as context; self-selected data narrows toward what it already solves.}\\[0.12cm]
