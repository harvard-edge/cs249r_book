\begin{frame}{Four verdicts: open trials stay in the denominator}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.36\textwidth}
      \small
      \textbf{\textcolor{figgreen}{pass}}: evidence meets the criterion\\[0.08cm]
      \textbf{\textcolor{figred}{fail}}: evidence shows a violation\\[0.08cm]
      \textbf{pending}: check still running\\[0.08cm]
      \textbf{unknown}: no evidence at the cutoff\\[0.25cm]
      Accept only if every mandatory criterion passes. Any fail ends it.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.62\textwidth}
      \centering\mlsyssteps[width=\textwidth,height=3.6cm,keepaspectratio]{verdicts_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
      {\footnotesize 100 trials: 60 accepted, 25 failed, 15 unresolved (illustrative)}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Agent Evaluation, \S13.2.3, the four-verdict rule. Counts illustrative; rates from images/calcs.py.}
  \note{[2.5 min] [CORE] Each criterion resolves to one of four verdicts. Pending and unknown are evaluation verdicts, not trajectory states. The aggregation short-circuits: one established failure of a mandatory criterion fails the trial; acceptance needs every mandatory criterion to pass; otherwise the trial stays pending or unknown. Step 1: 100 trials, of which 15 timed out or crashed. The chapter's default counts them as not accepted, 60 percent, and reports the bounds from resolving all of them either way, 60 to 75 percent. Step 2: the common mistake. Drop the 15 and the rate becomes 70.6 percent. ENGAGE: which tasks are most likely to time out? (Expected: the hard ones, so dropping them leaves the easy ones; that is survivorship bias.) WARN: dropping open trials also lowers the reported cost per accepted task without making the system cheaper. LINK: Lecture 10's bounded-liability admission keeps outstanding reservations separate from settled charges; a cutoff does not cancel a running cloud job. FLEX: [CORE].}
\end{frame}
