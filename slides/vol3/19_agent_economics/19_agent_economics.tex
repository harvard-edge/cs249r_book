\begin{frame}{Each accepted task carries $(1-\alpha)/\alpha$ rejected ones}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering\safeimg[width=\textwidth,height=5.3cm,keepaspectratio]{multiplier.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \small
      $\Ceff = \dfrac{\sum_i C_{\text{task},i}}{N_{\text{acc}}} = \bar C_{\text{acc}} + \dfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\bar C_{\text{rej}}$\\[0.3cm]
      {\footnotesize $\alpha$: share of initiated tasks accepted under one contract by a cutoff. A retry on one ticket adds no accepted task.}\\[0.3cm]
      \begin{mlsyscard}{mlsysaccent}\small A token cut shrinks one term. Acceptance divides \textbf{every} term.\end{mlsyscard}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \mlsyscredit{Agent Economics, \S19.2.2 (cohort cost per accepted task) and its rejected-per-accepted figure, redrawn at slide size.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Why does higher resolution shrink that gap so sharply? Cohort cost per accepted task (\S19.2.2) turns Lecture 1's planning ratio into an exact accounting identity with no distributional assumptions. Dividing total initiated spend, including failed, timed-out, and canceled work, by distinct tasks accepted under one contract by the cutoff gives $C_{\text{eff}} = \bar{C}_{\text{acc}} + \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bar{C}_{\text{rej}}$. The curve plots that failure multiplier across acceptance rates. At 25 percent acceptance, each accepted task carries 3 rejected attempts, while at 75 percent it carries only 0.33. On Slide 8, Run A's 168 resolved runs carried $332/168 \approx 2$ unresolved runs each. Below 30 percent acceptance, the multiplier climbs steeply. Discounting tokens shrinks one numerator term, whereas raising acceptance divides every term.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Three successful retries on the same ticket still count as one accepted task in the denominator.}

    \notecue{Next}{Slide 10 tests three accounting edge cases on canceled children, pending verdicts, and zero acceptances.}
  }
\end{frame}

  \centering
  \mlsyssteps[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.2cm,keepaspectratio]{resend_step}{2}\\[0.05cm]
  {\small Cached re-reads are still 50.8\% of the cached bill. If 10\% of the prefix misses, the bill rises 45.7\%.}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Does pricing cached reads at a tenth of fresh input eliminate Lecture 2's quadratic append-only resend bill? Napkin Math 19.2 tests a 30-turn trajectory starting from an 8,000-token initial prompt with +1,500 tokens appended per turn and 200 output tokens per call. The gray curve resends every token uncached, sending 892,500 input tokens for \$2.768. \begin{frame}{Compaction pays only if enough calls remain to repay it}
  \centering
  \safeimg[width=0.92\textwidth,height=4.15cm,keepaspectratio]{payback.pdf}\\[0.02cm]
  {\small $n^\star = 1 + \left\lceil \dfrac{p_{\text{out}}K_{\text{sum}} + p_{\text{in}}S_c}{p_{\text{cache}}(S - S_c)} \right\rceil$}\enspace{\footnotesize ($p$: \$15/\$3/\$0.30/M;\ $K_{\text{sum}}{=}1.5\text{k}$;\ $S{=}30.5\text{k}{\to}S_c{=}12\text{k}$)}\\[0.02cm]
  {\footnotesize Upfront $\$0.0225+\$0.0360=\$0.0585$; saves $\$0.00555$/call at 10\% cache $\Rightarrow n^\star{=}12$ (23 at 5\%).}
  \mlsyscredit{Agent Economics, \S19.3.2 and Napkin Math 19.3 (hypothetical; tariff of Napkin Math 19.2). Curves and payback (derived).}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{3 min}{Core}%
    Because cached re-reads still grow quadratically, a team may turn to Lecture 4's compaction, which summarizes earlier history to stay within context limits, hoping to trim bills too. Rewriting the prefix, however, forces a cache miss on the next call (Napkin Math 19.3). Summarizing $S = 30.5\text{k}$ tokens down to $S_c = 12\text{k}$ costs 1,500 output tokens at \$15/M (\$0.0225) plus resending 12k fresh tokens at \$3/M (\$0.0360), for \$0.0585 upfront. At a 10 percent cache rate, each later call saves 18.5k cached tokens, or \$0.00555/call. The blue net-cost curve therefore crosses zero at $n^\star = 12$ remaining calls, saving 1.92 cents when 15 calls remain. Under Slide 12's newer 5 percent cache rate, per-call savings halve while upfront cost stays fixed, pushing the orange break-even to $n^\star = 23$ calls and losing 1.96 cents with 15 calls left.

    \notecue{Watch for}{A summary that drops a key constraint and reduces acceptance erases any token saving.}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Optional}%
    Once latency headroom is set, a team decides between renting API tokens and owning dedicated servers. Extending Armbrust et al.'s (2010) cloud rent-versus-own trade-off, where fixed hardware bills even when idle, equation 6 (Napkin Math 19.9) solves for break-even load. With fixed capacity $F = \$28/\text{h}$, reference rate $R = 1{,}200\text{ out/s}$, marginal cost $v = \$2/\text{M}$, and a 5:1 input/output ratio at \$3/\$0.30/\$15, 80 percent caching gives an effective API price of \$19.2/M output and $u^\star = 37.7\%$. At 52 percent load, 2,246,400 out/h costs \$43.13 via API versus \$32.49 owned, saving \$10.64/h. Without caching (\$30/M), the crossing drops to 23.1 percent because provider cache discounts weaken ownership. Across Segment 4, capacity scales with goodput over acceptance, and high utilization is paid in waiting.

    \notecue{Watch for}{Compare matched capability, privacy, and tail latency, and remember idle off-peak hours still pay fixed capacity costs.}

  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{2.5 min}{Core}%
    Why do simple spending caps fail in practice? In SWE-agent's real cost check (models.py, commit 3ea751c, default per-task cap \$3), the harness increments instance\_cost first and checks per\_instance\_cost\_limit afterward, so the check can stop only the next call. In Run A under a \$4 cap, reported in Kapoor et al. (2024), Lecture 13's cost-accuracy evaluation study, 90 of 500 trajectories finished above \$4, peaking at \$4.20, and stayed close only because individual calls were small. Recall from Lecture 10's bounded-liability admission rule that the runtime must reserve an enforceable upper bound $\bar{c}$ before dispatch so settled plus reserved spend never exceeds budget, releasing unused funds only at settlement. Extending that rule to agent trees requires $\bar{c}$ to cover maximum completion, hidden reasoning tokens, and undiscounted input when caching is not guaranteed.

\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
  \thumbline{thumb_swe_step2.pdf}{Each accepted task carries $(1-\alpha)/\alpha$ failures; a real run cost \$5.10 per resolved task against a \$1.16 survivor average.}\\[0.12cm]
  \thumbline{thumb_payback.pdf}{Caching cuts the 30-turn bill 5.6$\times$ but keeps the quadratic term; compaction pays after 12 calls at a 10\% cache rate, 23 at 5\%.}\\[0.12cm]
  \thumbline{thumb_waterfall_step4.pdf}{At 9.4\% model time, no model speedup passes 1.10$\times$. A cascade saves 6.3$\times$ only through early exits under one check.}\\[0.12cm]
  \thumbline{thumb_kingman_step2.pdf}{Admit goodput over $\alpha$. From 70\% to 90\% utilization, 28.6\% more throughput costs 3.9$\times$ the wait.}\\[0.12cm]
  \thumbline{thumb_budget_step5.pdf}{Reserve before dispatch, release only at settlement, and price designs per resolved ticket with fallback.}
  \note{%
    \mlsysnotemeta{1.5 min}{Core}%
    Across the five segments, every economic rule reduces to a quantitative check. First, each accepted task carries $(1-\alpha)/\alpha$ failures, producing a \$5.10 cohort cost against a \$1.16 survivor average in a real repair run. Second, prefix caching cuts the 30-turn bill 5.6$\times$ while keeping quadratic growth, and compaction repays at 12 calls under a 10 percent cache rate versus 23 at 5 percent. Third, a 9.4 percent model share caps episode speedup at 1.10$\times$, while early verified exits buy a 6.3$\times$ cut in a cascade. Fourth, fleet admission scales as $\mathcal{G}/\alpha$, and Kingman's approximation prices a 3.9$\times$ wait increase from 70 to 90 percent utilization. Fifth, reserve budget before dispatch, release only at settlement, and include human fallback when pricing architectures.
