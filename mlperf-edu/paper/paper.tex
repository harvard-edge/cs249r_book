\begin{abstract}
Benchmarking supplies the empirical foundation for evaluating hardware-software trade-offs and diagnosing systemic bottlenecks in machine-learning systems. Industry-standard suites such as MLPerf establish this discipline by holding task, dataset, metric, and quality targets constant, but their compute requirements place them out of reach of single-node hardware. MLPerf EDU carries that discipline down to single-laptop execution through zero-discretion target inheritance and strict admission control. Every workload inherits its quality target from an upstream authority, and any missed threshold or procedural violation invalidates the run rather than being renegotiated locally. The suite packages \RegistryRows{} workloads across \RegistrySuites{} registry suites, all of which execute end to end on a consumer laptop in \ScoreReferenceTotalMinutes{}~minutes of reference compute. We report results under the same admission rules we ask students to apply. All \ScoreBearingPassing{} score-bearing cases meet their inherited targets, while \FunctionalSetupWorkloads{} workloads currently fall short of their published reference points and are reported as measured-versus-published gaps rather than as passes. The suite exposes quantization, sample-budget, and hardware-backend trade-offs across the Data-Algorithm-Machine axes without relaxing the evaluation contract. The path to production status runs through \mbox{cross-platform replication}, public evidence hosting, dataset-policy decisions, and external review.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Historical precedent demonstrates how standard benchmarks transform fields. SPEC CPU~\citep{henning2006} ended the era of uncalibrated instruction-count claims in computer architecture by enforcing standardized execution suites. Similarly, TPC~\citep{poess2000} established audited governance in database systems, demonstrating that rigorous disclosure rules matter as much as workload code. These suites transformed systems evaluation into an inspectable science.

MLPerf~\citep{mattson2020,reddi2020mlperf} extended this discipline to machine learning. Traditional workloads have binary output correctness that is cheap to verify. ML systems do not. Throughput and latency are coupled to statistical task quality (accuracy, BLEU, FID), so a speedup means nothing until the quality floor is stated. Fixing non-negotiable quality targets alongside latency measurements is the methodological contribution that made ML systems results comparable across submitters.

A gap remains in ML systems education and research. Learners and researchers need an accessible suite that instills production-grade empirical discipline, but production suites require multi-node GPU clusters, and local educational setups drop the quality targets and auditability that make results comparable. Five foundational educational and systems principles govern the design of any suite that would close that gap.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1.5pt]
\item \textbf{P1 (Quality-Performance Coupling).} Performance cannot be evaluated in isolation from task quality. Speedups achieved by outputting degraded results or relaxing accuracy targets are invalid, requiring benchmarks to enforce strict quality floors.
\item \textbf{P2 (Reproducibility and Auditability).} Evaluation claims require complete asset lineage and auditability so that results can be independently verified without relying on unvalidated self-reporting.
\item \textbf{P3 (Microarchitectural Fidelity).} Benchmarks must expose genuine systems bottlenecks, including memory bandwidth limits and execution stalls, on accessible single-node hardware rather than relying on synthetic toy operators.
\item \textbf{P4 (Statistical Variance and Determinism).} Evaluators must account for measurement stability, recognizing when executions are deterministic and when training variability requires multi-seed statistical aggregation.
\item \textbf{P5 (Multi-Dimensional Trade-Offs).} Systems evaluation extends beyond chasing a single performance number, training evaluators to navigate trade-offs across data scale, algorithmic choices, and machine hardware boundaries.
\end{itemize}

Guided directly by these five governing principles, we developed \textbf{MLPerf EDU}, an accessible, production-derived ML systems benchmark suite engineered for single-laptop execution. To achieve coverage without compromising benchmark authority, MLPerf EDU packages \RegistryRows{} canonical workloads across \RegistrySuites{} registry suites: Causal Language Modeling (nanoGPT), Text Classification (DistilBERT), Information Retrieval (SBERT), Code Generation (Qwen2.5-Coder), Function Calling (BFCL Qwen3 AST), Graph Node Classification (GCN on \texttt{ogbn-arxiv}), Time-Series Forecasting (PatchTST), Image Classification (ResNet8), Keyword Spotting (DS-CNN), Visual Wake Words (MobileNetV2), Anomaly Detection (Autoencoder), Image Generation (EDM Diffusion), Recommendation (NCF on \texttt{MovieLens-20M}), and Reinforcement Learning (MCTS MiniGo). Every workload binds its input pipeline, runner contract, and upstream target threshold into an inspectable dossier with strict admission control.

Using this artifact, researchers and students execute controlled empirical studies that illuminate non-obvious relationships across the Data (D), Algorithm (A), and Machine (M) taxonomy. For example, while ML practitioners expect GPU hardware acceleration to scale uniformly across models, sparse Graph Neural Networks (GCN) and agentic tool-calling AST parsing show zero GPU speedup ($0.98\times\text{--}1.01\times$) over a standard CPU due to irregular memory gather operations and warp divergence. Conversely, INT8 dynamic quantization halves parameter byte traffic, effectively doubling operational intensity and shifting memory-bound workloads rightward into the compute-bound regime.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Benchmarking is a mature discipline with well-governed suites, and a new suite must justify its existence against established alternatives. Existing evaluation surfaces generally force a trade-off. Production suites enforce strict quality-controlled evaluation, but require cluster compute or custom physical hardware. Local educational setups fit local budgets, but drop quality targets, ruining comparability. MLPerf EDU is positioned against production suites, client and edge benchmarks, microbenchmarks, and educational lab environments in turn.

\textbf{Production and client/edge suites.} The official MLPerf Training and Inference suites established quality-controlled, scenario-aware performance evaluation at production scale~\citep{mattson2020,reddi2020mlperf}. MLPerf Mobile~\citep{ignatov2021mlperf} and MLPerf Tiny~\citep{banbury2021mlperf} apply this methodology to smartphones and microcontrollers, while MLPerf HPC~\citep{farrell2021mlperf} addresses supercomputing clusters. Complementing physical system benchmarks, DataPerf~\citep{mazumder2023dataperf} measures data-centric AI algorithms and dataset quality, while AlgoPerf~\citep{dahl2023algoperf} evaluates algorithmic optimizer efficiency. 

A natural question is why existing MLCommons client or edge suites, namely MLPerf Client~\citep{mlcommons2024client}, MLPerf Mobile~\citep{ignatov2021mlperf}, and MLPerf Tiny~\citep{banbury2021mlperf}, cannot simply be repurposed for systems education and research. While MLPerf Client evaluates desktop PC user-experience scenarios and MLPerf Mobile/Tiny target smartphones and microcontrollers, these suites are specialized for specific hardware deployment classes. MLPerf Client focuses on desktop application inference pipelines (e.g., video conferencing, image super-resolution) without covering multi-domain model training, agentic tool-calling, graph neural networks, time-series forecasting, or structured Data-Algorithm-Machine (DAM) trade-off ablations. MLPerf Mobile and Tiny require physical smartphone device farms or custom microcontroller evaluation boards, while their model constraints (sub-megabyte to low-MB) omit modern transformer scaling, autoregressive KV-cache decode, and cross-encoder reranking. MLPerf Training and Inference, at the other extreme, require multi-GPU cloud clusters and multi-gigabyte dataset downloads. MLPerf EDU inherits MLPerf's rigorous discipline while targeting locally executable, single-node evaluation. \Cref{tab:comparison} summarizes this positioning feature matrix.

\textbf{Educational lab setups.} Local educational setups and framework-building courses, such as MiniTorch~\citep{rush2021}, TinyGrad, or educational implementations, fit local budgets and make tensors, operators, and automatic differentiation inspectable. However, they typically drop quality targets, sacrificing comparability. A classroom result that executes quickly but silently fails its accuracy target is not a valid baseline. MLPerf EDU provides this missing discipline. By using PyTorch~\citep{paszke2019pytorch} for its standard execution path, the suite decouples its specification from any custom educational framework.

\textbf{Microbenchmarks.} DAWNBench introduced time-to-accuracy, connecting system performance to a fixed task-quality threshold~\citep{coleman2019}. TorchBench provides broad PyTorch framework regression coverage~\citep{ansel2024torchbench}. MLPerf EDU shares the quality-first rule of DAWNBench and the executable PyTorch surface of TorchBench, but targets fixed classroom exercises with inherited thresholds rather than leaderboards or framework regressions.

\subsection{Benchmark Requirements}

To bridge the gap between production cluster benchmarks and unvalidated educational scripts, a single-node ML systems benchmark suite must translate the five governing principles (P1--P5) into five actionable benchmark requirements:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1.5pt]
\item \textbf{R1 (Zero-Discretion Target Inheritance).} Directly implementing P1 (Quality-Performance Coupling), quality targets must be inherited from upstream standards without local downward calibration or discretionary relaxation.
\item \textbf{R2 (Single-Laptop Envelope Fit).} Fulfilling single-node accessibility, workloads must execute to completion within standard consumer hardware constraints (x86 CPU, CUDA, MPS GPU, $\le 16\text{ GB}$ UMA RAM, $\le 190\text{ MB}$ dataset downloads) without cluster dependencies.
\item \textbf{R3 (Microarchitectural Fidelity).} Directly implementing P3 (Microarchitectural Fidelity), workloads must preserve real hardware execution bottlenecks (Compute-Bound vs. DRAM Memory-Bandwidth Bound) rather than relying on uncalibrated toy operators.
\item \textbf{R4 (Strict Admission Control).} Implementing P2 (Reproducibility and Auditability) and P4 (Determinism and Variance), performance claims are admitted only if task quality, scenario protocols, asset provenance, and host stability are 100\% satisfied; shortfalls trigger hard rejection.
\item \textbf{R5 (Multi-Lens Trade-Offs).} Directly implementing P5 (Multi-Dimensional Trade-Offs), the suite must support structured evaluation across Data (D), Algorithm (A), and Machine (M) taxonomy dimensions linked to curriculum learning goals.
\end{enumerate}

No existing benchmark surface satisfies all five requirements simultaneously (\Cref{tab:comparison}): production suites (MLPerf Train/Inf) enforce R1, R3, and R4 but violate R2; client/mobile/tiny suites violate R2 (specialized device dependencies) and R5; educational lab scripts satisfy R2 but omit R1, R3, R4, and R5; microbenchmarks (TorchBench) omit R1 and R4. MLPerf EDU is engineered to fulfill all five requirements for single-node ML systems evaluation.

The largest dataset any contract pulls is \mbox{190~MB} (\Cref{tab:datasets}) and the largest model checkpoint is roughly two billion parameters. Automated dataset fetching includes SHA-256 checksum verification, URL mirroring, and fallback URL recipes to guarantee download resilience against upstream link rot.

\subsection{Language, Code, and Agentic Workloads}
\label{sec:suite_language}

Language and retrieval workloads represent the dominant computational footprint in modern ML systems, spanning autoregressive text generation, bidirectional encoder representations, cross-encoder document reranking, and agentic tool invocation. Rather than viewing language models merely as statistical text generators, MLPerf EDU structures these workloads to expose the fundamental shift from compute-bound prompt prefill to memory-bandwidth-bound autoregressive decoding, alongside the control-flow challenges introduced by structured agent execution.

\textbf{Causal language modeling.} Character-level text generation using nanoGPT~\citep{karpathy2023nanogpt} on \texttt{tinyshakespeare}~\citep{karpathy2022nanogpt} serves as the foundational probe for transformer inference dynamics. During prompt prefill, batching matrix multiplications saturates parallel execution units. Once the model transitions to single-token autoregressive generation, memory traffic dominates. Every token generated requires loading the complete model weight matrix and reading past key-value activations from DRAM, reducing operational intensity to less than $1.0\text{ FLOP/byte}$. Students observe firsthand how key-value caching prevents redundant sequence recomputation while converting execution into a memory-bandwidth-bound workload evaluated against an inherited cross-entropy loss threshold $\le 1.4700$.

\textbf{Text classification.} Sentiment analysis using DistilBERT~\citep{sanh2019distilbert} on GLUE SST-2~\citep{socher2013sst2} captures fixed-window bidirectional encoder inference. Unlike autoregressive models, bidirectional attention processes the entire sequence in a single contiguous pass without dynamic state retention. The workload illustrates how model distillation reduces transformer parameter footprints by $40\%$ while preserving $97\%$ of BERT's language understanding performance. Evaluated against an inherited accuracy threshold $\ge 91.06\%$, it provides a clean baseline for dense tensor dispatch on single-node hardware.

\textbf{Information retrieval.} Document reranking using MiniLM-L6-v2~\citep{reimers2019sbert} on NanoBEIR~\citep{thakur2021beir} models multi-query candidate scoring loops in production search pipelines. In information retrieval systems, bi-encoders rapidly retrieve candidate documents, after which a cross-encoder performs joint self-attention across query-document pairs to compute fine-grained relevance scores. This workload exercises nested batching loops and pairwise matrix multiplications, evaluating retrieval precision against an inherited mean nDCG@10 threshold $\ge 60.72\%$.

\textbf{Code generation.} Automated Python function synthesis using Qwen2.5-Coder~\citep{yang2024qwen25} on HumanEval~\citep{chen2021humaneval} introduces execution safety and sandboxed code evaluation. Benchmarking code generation extends beyond string matching; valid code must be executed inside isolated Docker or Subprocess containers to evaluate unit-test correctness under pass@1 scoring. This workload teaches containerized execution management, resource isolation, and sandboxed test harness design against an inherited functional correctness threshold $\ge 57.30\%$.

\textbf{Function calling and agentic tool use.} Structured tool invocation using Qwen3 on the Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard (BFCL) AST evaluation split~\citep{yan2024bfcl} models the mechanics of LLM agents. The workload requires the model to generate syntactically valid Abstract Syntax Trees (ASTs) that match formal JSON function signatures. Unlike free-form text generation, agentic function calling involves dynamic syntax validation and branch evaluation. On parallel hardware backends, non-deterministic token branching triggers warp divergence, where execution threads within a SIMD warp take different control-flow paths. Evaluated against an inherited AST accuracy threshold $\ge 82.92\%$, this workload illustrates how agentic control flow impacts hardware efficiency.

\subsection{Graph Neural Networks and Temporal Dynamics}
\label{sec:suite_graph}

Graph neural networks and time-series forecasting represent non-Euclidean spatial topologies and multivariate temporal correlations, challenging traditional dense GEMM hardware accelerators with irregular memory access and long-context patch correlations.

\textbf{Graph node classification.} Node property prediction using a 3-layer Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)~\citep{kipf2017gcn} on \texttt{ogbn-arxiv}~\citep{hu2020ogb} demonstrates the severe systems bottlenecks created by irregular graph structure. Standard neural networks operate on contiguous 2D or 4D tensor buffers that map efficiently to SIMD vector registers and GPU warps. In contrast, graph neural networks rely on sparse gather and scatter operations to aggregate feature messages across graph edges. On the 169,343-node citation network of \texttt{ogbn-arxiv}, these irregular index lookups fragment cache lines, thrash L2 caches, and bottleneck on DRAM latency. As a result, GPU acceleration yields near $1.0\times$ speedup over CPU execution, exposing students to non-Euclidean memory thrashing evaluated against an inherited test accuracy threshold $\ge 71.74\%$.

\textbf{Time-series forecasting.} Multivariate temporal forecasting using PatchTST~\citep{nie2023patchtst} on the ETTm1 electricity transformer dataset addresses long-context sequence modeling in physical systems. Traditional time-series models process individual timestamps, leading to high computational complexity and noise sensitivity. PatchTST introduces channel-independent patch extraction, grouping adjacent timestamps into token patches before applying self-attention across time. This design reduces attention matrix complexity from $O(L^2)$ to $O((L/P)^2)$ while creating contiguous memory buffers that restore high operational intensity. Evaluated against an inherited test Mean Squared Error (MSE) threshold $\le 0.2900$, this workload demonstrates how domain-specific tensor representations restore hardware efficiency on sequence data.

\subsection{Vision, Audio, and Generative Edge Systems}
\label{sec:suite_vision}

Computer vision, audio processing, and embedded edge models capture the spectrum from ultra-constrained microcontroller execution to heavy iterative generative samplers, illustrating convolution dispatch, depthwise separable operators, audio spectrogram processing, and sub-megabyte memory boundaries.

\textbf{Image classification.} Object recognition using ResNet8~\citep{he2016resnet} on CIFAR-10~\citep{krizhevsky2009cifar} serves as the primary benchmark for spatial 2D convolutions and residual skip connections. Residual networks solve the vanishing gradient problem by adding identity shortcut connections across convolutional layers. Compute-bound 3x3 GEMM convolutions dominate execution, allowing parallel hardware units to saturate arithmetic pipelines. Evaluated against an inherited top-1 accuracy threshold $\ge 85.00\%$, ResNet8 provides an inspectable baseline for spatial convolution dispatch and memory layout optimizations on consumer processors.

\textbf{Keyword spotting.} Spoken word recognition using a Depthwise Separable CNN (DS-CNN)~\citep{zhang2017dscnn} on SpeechCommands~\citep{warden2018speech} models real-time audio keyword detection. Before neural execution, raw audio waveforms are transformed into 2D Mel-frequency cepstral coefficient (MFCC) spectrograms. DS-CNN processes these spectrograms by factorizing standard 2D convolutions into spatial depthwise convolutions followed by 1x1 point-wise convolutions, reducing parameter counts and FLOP requirements by over $80\%$. Evaluated against an inherited top-1 accuracy threshold $\ge 90.00\%$, this workload teaches front-end audio signal processing and depthwise operator efficiency.

\textbf{Visual wake words.} Person detection using MobileNetV2~\citep{howard2017mobilenet} on Visual Wake Words (VWW)~\citep{chowdhery2019vww} captures duty-cycled vision execution on edge devices. MobileNetV2 uses inverted residual blocks and linear bottlenecks to constrain memory footprint within sub-megabyte SRAM boundaries. In edge deployments, models must execute within strict power budgets, running periodically (duty-cycling) rather than continuously. Evaluated against an inherited top-1 accuracy threshold $\ge 80.00\%$, this workload exposes students to sub-megabyte memory bounds and low-latency edge inference constraints.

\textbf{Anomaly detection.} Industrial machine condition monitoring using a feature autoencoder~\citep{sakurada2014autoencoder} on ToyADMOS~\citep{koizumi2019toyadmos} models unsupervised acoustic quality control. Industrial sensors monitor factory machinery by detecting deviations from normal operational sounds. The autoencoder compresses input acoustic features through a narrow bottleneck layer before reconstructing the original signal. Abnormal machine behavior increases reconstruction error, serving as the anomaly score. Evaluated against an inherited ROC AUC threshold $\ge 0.8500$, this workload teaches feature reconstruction error metrics and bottleneck representation learning.

\textbf{Image generation.} Unconditional image synthesis using EDM diffusion~\citep{karras2022edm} on CIFAR-10 represents the heavy computational demands of generative AI. Unlike single-pass inference models, diffusion models generate images by iteratively sampling from a continuous stochastic differential equation (SDE) or ordinary differential equation (ODE). Each of the 18 solver steps executes a full UNet forward pass containing multi-head cross-attention and spatial convolutions, costing tens of GFLOPs per generated image. The inherited Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) threshold of $1.7900$ is the published minimum across three independent 50,000-image trials, so the contract reproduces the same three-trial seed stream (150,000 images total) and compares minima like for like. This workload exposes students to iterative sampler stepping and high-throughput GEMM compute saturation.

\subsection{Recommendation and Search-Coupled Systems}
\label{sec:suite_recsys}

Recommendation systems and reinforcement learning capture high-cardinality sparse embedding table lookups and tree-search-guided dual policy-value network inference, representing critical production workloads in web services and decision-making AI.

\textbf{Recommendation systems.} Collaborative filtering rating prediction using Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF)~\citep{he2017ncf} on MovieLens-20M~\citep{harper2015movielens} captures the dual memory and compute bottlenecks of personalization pipelines. In production recommendation systems, user and item identifiers are mapped through massive embedding tables containing millions of high-dimensional vectors. Looking up sparse user and item embeddings creates uncoalesced memory reads across DRAM. Once retrieved, the embedding vectors are concatenated and passed through dense multi-layer perceptron (MLP) layers to predict user preference scores. Evaluated against an inherited \texttt{hit\_rate@10} threshold $\ge 0.6350$, this workload demonstrates negative sampling strategies and high-cardinality sparse embedding table access.

\textbf{Reinforcement learning and search.} Strategic action selection using Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) guided dual policy-value networks (MiniGo)~\citep{minigo2018,silver2016alphago} models search-coupled decision making. MiniGo combines deep neural network evaluation with tree search. During game tree traversal, the system executes hundreds of MCTS simulations: selecting tree nodes, expanding leaf states, evaluating policy and value predictions using a dual-head convolutional network, and backpropagating rewards up the search tree. This hybrid architecture creates complex pointer-based memory access and dynamic search tree loops that challenge static graph compilers. Evaluated against an inherited move prediction accuracy threshold $\ge 0.4000$, this workload illustrates search-coupled neural execution, dynamic replay buffers, and dual policy-value heads.

\section{Benchmark Harness}
\label{sec:harness}

MLPerf EDU uses a single authoritative specification to define assets, execution pipelines, and validation rules for every workload. A unified command-line interface drives fetch, run, grade, report, and packaging operations.

\subsection{Workload Registry}

The declarative registry serves as the authoritative source of truth for all workloads and datasets in the suite. It stores workload identities, dataset splits, licensing terms, and inherited quality rules in machine-readable YAML dossiers. Centralizing these parameters eliminates undocumented execution deviations and guarantees that every local run executes against authoritative assets and target bounds.

\subsection{Load Generator}

The execution harness provides a uniform runtime environment across workloads, decoupling scenario scheduling from System Under Test (SUT) model execution. A Python-native LoadGen issues queries and logs latency samples, percentiles, throughput, and functional outcomes for SingleStream and Offline scenarios. SHA-256 digests record hardware fingerprints, asset dossiers, and checkpoint lineage in a self-contained \texttt{.provd.json} manifest. The manifest is tamper-evident but unsigned, so it supports local run validation and independent reproduction rather than authenticated attestation.

Workloads operate under one of two evaluation roles (\Cref{tab:reference-evidence}):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1.5pt]
\item \textbf{Score-bearing workloads (\texttt{Score}).} Evaluated against strict accuracy or quality target thresholds inherited from MLCommons standards (e.g., top-1 accuracy $\ge 85.0\%$, loss $\le 1.470$, mean nDCG@10 $\ge 60.72\%$). Task quality is non-negotiable under admission rules.

\subsection{Reporting Shortfalls}
\label{sec:shortfalls}
The admission rule that rejects a student's run applies equally to ours, so the shortfalls are reported here rather than aggregated away. All \ScoreBearingPassing{} score-bearing cases meet their inherited targets. Separately, \FunctionalSetupWorkloads{} workloads reproduce below their published reference point and are recorded as measured-versus-published gaps, not as passes: code generation at \CodeGenerationMeasured{} against \CodeGenerationPublished{}, function calling at \FunctionCallingMeasured{} against \FunctionCallingPublished{}, image generation at FID \ImageGenerationMeasured{} against \ImageGenerationPublished{}, recommendation at \RecommendationMeasured{} against \RecommendationPublished{}, and reinforcement learning at \ReinforcementLearningMeasured{} against \ReinforcementLearningPublished{}.

Three of these gaps are narrow enough to sit inside the reproduction procedure's own noise, and image generation is the clearest case: the committed trials span a wider FID range than the amount by which the best trial misses, so the shortfall is not separable from sampler variance. Reinforcement learning is different in kind. MiniGo was executed under a reduced generation budget to fit the single-laptop envelope, so its gap measures the budget, not the method, and it is reported as a miss rather than rescued by relaxing the target. Keeping these five visible is the point. A suite whose contribution is zero-discretion target inheritance cannot quietly re-baseline the cases where inheritance is inconvenient.

This mapping classifies the 14 single-node workloads into two distinct microarchitectural regimes under reference hardware ceilings ($150~\text{GB/s}$ DRAM bandwidth, $2.5~\text{TFLOP/s}$ compute peak), as illustrated in \Cref{fig:roofline}. The runtime distribution across workloads is shown in \Cref{fig:runtime}.

The Roofline placement is consistent with the design thesis behind MLPerf EDU. Consumer laptop hardware exhibits the same two microarchitectural regimes, compute-bound and DRAM memory-bandwidth bound, that dominate datacenter analysis, even though the absolute ceilings differ by orders of magnitude. The knee point at $I_{\text{knee}} = \frac{2500\text{ GFLOP/s}}{150\text{ GB/s}} = 16.7\text{ FLOPs/byte}$ separates the two regimes on this host.

\textbf{Compute-bound workloads ($I \ge 16.7\text{ FLOPs/byte}$).} Workloads right of the knee saturate execution units before exhausting memory bandwidth. LLM Prefill ($I=45.2$), ResNet8 image classification ($I=38.6$), and EDM diffusion image generation ($I=52.1$) exhibit high operational intensity. Their dense matrix multiplications (GEMMs) map efficiently to parallel arithmetic logic units, limited by peak compute capacity ($2.5\text{ TFLOP/s}$) rather than memory bus transfer rates.

\textbf{DRAM memory-bandwidth bound workloads ($I < 16.7\text{ FLOPs/byte}$).} Workloads left of the knee stall waiting for memory data along the $150\text{ GB/s}$ slanted DRAM ceiling ($P = 150 \times I\text{ GFLOP/s}$). LLM Decode ($I=0.5\text{ FLOPs/byte}$) is constrained by single-token autoregressive KV-cache reads across DRAM. GCN node classification ($I=1.2\text{ FLOPs/byte}$) relies on sparse gather/scatter index lookups on \texttt{ogbn-arxiv}, fragmenting memory access and thrashing L2 caches. NCF recommendation ($I=2.1\text{ FLOPs/byte}$) depends on high-cardinality sparse embedding lookups on \texttt{MovieLens-20M}, bottlenecking on shared memory bus bandwidth.

\subsection{Determinism and Variance}
\label{sec:determinism}
Evaluating systems performance requires isolating true microarchitectural execution behavior from stochastic statistical noise. A single execution attempt may confirm functional correctness, but establishing authoritative timing and quality claims requires evaluating determinism and variance across repeated runs~\citep{bouthillier2019,pham2020}.

The determinism sweep covers \DeterminismWorkloads{} inference workloads at \DeterminismSeeds{} seeds each across both the CPU and MPS backends, for \DeterminismExecutions{} executions in total. Across that sweep the maximum observed spread between seeds is \DeterminismMaxSeedSpread{} and the maximum delta between backends is \DeterminismMaxBackendDelta{}, so scored inference is reproducible to the recorded precision on this host. Timing is not equally stable even when quality is exact. Repeated-timing cases show a coefficient of variation between \FiveRunMinCV{} and \FiveRunMaxCV{}, which is why the suite reports timing separately and never lets it gate a quality decision. We claim reproducibility only for the workloads, backends, and precisions in this sweep. It is not a general claim about MPS determinism, and the ledger records one exploratory float32 configuration whose score moved when the attention implementation was left unpinned.

Training is a different regime. The training seed sweep currently covers \TrainingSeedWorkloads{} workloads at \TrainingSeedCount{} seeds each, and in \TrainingSeedFlipping{} of them the seed alone decides the admission verdict: one seed clears the inherited target and another falls below it on an otherwise identical configuration. As the per-epoch convergence trajectories in \Cref{fig:curves} show, a shortfall under an unlucky initialization persists across extended epochs rather than converging away. Two workloads at three seeds is too thin a base for a distributional claim, and we do not make one. It is, however, sufficient to establish the operational point that matters for a benchmark contract. A single training run cannot support an admission verdict, so multi-seed aggregation is a submission requirement for training workloads rather than a recommendation. Extending the sweep to every training workload at five seeds is the first item of measurement work queued for the next release.

\section{DAM Taxonomy Evaluation: Multi-Dimensional Intersections}
\label{sec:dam_eval}

The suite structures systems optimization around the Data (D), Algorithm (A), and Machine (M) taxonomy. Conventional ML systems benchmarking frequently treats these dimensions as separable, assuming that optimization choices along one axis compose additively with choices along another. While datacenter-scale clusters with massive memory bandwidth and over-provisioned compute can mask cross-layer inefficiencies, resource-constrained single-node environments sharply expose architectural coupling. 

We formulate the \textbf{Non-Decomposability Principle}: the optimal system configuration on constrained hardware is not the linear composition of independent single-axis choices,
\begin{equation}
(D, A, M)^* \ne (D^*, A^*, M^*).
\end{equation}
Optimizing each dimension in isolation yields configurations that either degrade into severe microarchitectural regressions (such as $11\times$ slowdowns from missing GPU kernels) or catastrophic quality failures ($9.9\%$ drops below admission floors). Evaluating single-node systems requires a multi-tier methodology: Tier~1 establishes baseline single-node characterization (\Cref{sec:characterization}); Tier~2 establishes controlled single-lens baselines (\Cref{sec:single_lenses}); and Tier~3 investigates the non-linear pairwise and three-way intersections (\Cref{sec:intersections_pairwise}, \Cref{sec:intersections_factorial}), synthesized as the simplicial DAM Triangle in \Cref{fig:dam_intersections}.

\subsection{Single-Lens Foundations: Controls and Surprises}
\label{sec:single_lenses}

Before evaluating multi-layer interactions, the suite measures single-factor controls along each axis. To provide a rigorous baseline, each single lens demonstrates that intuitive scalar assumptions fail when confronted with empirical execution.

\textbf{Machine ($M$) Lens: The $100\times$ UMA Acceleration Spread.}
The Machine lens evaluates hardware backends (CPU reference baseline vs Apple MPS GPU) across representative workloads (\Cref{fig:cpu_vs_mps}, \Cref{tab:machine_exhaustive}). On datacenter accelerators, GPU speedups are often reported as a uniform multiplier. On a unified-memory SoC, hardware acceleration spans two orders of magnitude ($2.00\times$ to $207.35\times$). At one extreme, visual wake words (MobileNetV2) achieves a $207.35\times$ speedup ($215.19\text{ s} \to 1.04\text{ s}$) because depthwise separable convolutions execute with high arithmetic density and vectorize across the GPU execution units, whereas the CPU incurs severe instruction dispatch overhead across 48,608 image windows. At the other extreme, anomaly detection ($2.00\times$) and graph node classification ($2.38\times$) exhibit near-parity. GCN training bottlenecks on irregular pointer-chasing and sparse gather/scatter lookups over $1.16\text{M}$ edges, saturating the $150\text{ GB/s}$ DRAM memory bus rather than floating-point execution units.

\textbf{Algorithm ($A$) Lens: Precision Bifurcation Under Strict Admission.}
Drawing from algorithmic benchmarks such as AlgoPerf~\citep{dahl2023algoperf}, the Algorithm lens evaluates reduced-precision representations (FP16, BF16, INT8) against full FP32 baselines (\Cref{fig:algo_lens}, \Cref{tab:quant_summary}). On DistilBERT sequence classification, moving from FP32 to 16-bit floating-point cuts weight memory by $2.0\times$ ($260\text{ MB} \to 130\text{ MB}$) and accelerates inference by $3.3\times$--$3.5\times$ ($9.92\text{ s} \to 2.96\text{ s}$ on FP16, $2.83\text{ s}$ on BF16). However, under MLPerf EDU's strict zero-discretion admission rules, the two 16-bit formats bifurcate. FP16 preserves full task fidelity ($0.9106 \ge 0.9106$, \badgePass), whereas BF16's reduced 7-bit mantissa incurs accumulated rounding errors on classification logits, dropping validation accuracy to $0.9083$ ($< 0.9106$, \badgeMiss). Two formats considered interchangeable on a hardware specification sheet produce opposite admission verdicts under rigorous evaluation.

\textbf{Data ($D$) Lens: Sample Budget Floors.}
Grounded in data-centric systems evaluation~\citep{ng2021datacentric,mazumder2023dataperf}, the Data lens scales training sample budgets (100\%, 50\%, 25\%, 10\%) while holding model architectures fixed (\Cref{fig:data_lens}, \Cref{tab:budget}). Halving the training budget reduces wall-clock execution time almost exactly linearly across PatchTST ($854 \to 425\text{ s}$), GCN ($719.8 \to 360\text{ s}$), and NCF ($1420 \to 710\text{ s}$). In all three workloads, halving the sample budget drops task accuracy below inherited target floors. This demonstrates an immutable data floor: no free efficiency is available on the data axis alone at these operational points, and the admission rule refuses the trade instead of absorbing accuracy loss into a footnote.

\subsection{Intersectional Dynamics: Pairwise Interactions}
\label{sec:intersections_pairwise}

Crossing pairs of taxonomy dimensions reveals trade-offs inaccessible from any single lens.

\textbf{$A \cap M$: Quantization Inversion and Kernel Support.}
Algorithmic dynamic INT8 quantization is widely taught as an unmitigated latency optimization that cuts memory footprint by up to $4.0\times$. On CPU, quantizing DistilBERT's 38 Linear modules accelerates execution by $10\%$ ($76.73\text{ s} \to 68.76\text{ s}$ under settled execution). However, on Apple Silicon MPS, PyTorch lacks native INT8 quantized tensor execution kernels. Attempting to execute INT8 dynamic quantization forces execution fallback to the CPU host runtime, resulting in an execution time of $82.18\text{ s}$ (or $68.76\text{ s}$ settled) compared to $9.92\text{ s}$ (or $6.25\text{ s}$) for FP32 on the MPS GPU. The algorithmic optimization makes the system \textbf{$8.3\times$ to $11.0\times$ slower}. A representation choice is fundamentally meaningless without hardware kernel alignment.

Furthermore, the layer count reported by profiling tools does not predict the sign of an INT8 optimization. Both DistilBERT sequence classification and MiniLM-L6 information retrieval quantize exactly 38 Linear modules. Yet on the CPU baseline, dynamic INT8 accelerates DistilBERT by $10\%$ ($76.7\text{ s} \to 68.8\text{ s}$) while slowing MiniLM-L6 retrieval by \textbf{$48\%$} ($105.6\text{ s} \to 155.8\text{ s}$). Because dynamic quantization quantizes activation tensors at runtime on every forward pass, its computational overhead scales with the number of forward invocations. DistilBERT evaluates 872 sentences in large batches of 32, amortizing activation quantization across sequence tokens. In contrast, cross-encoder retrieval performs 100 iterative forward passes per query to re-rank candidate documents across NanoBEIR datasets, causing dynamic activation quantization overhead to overwhelm integer execution savings. 

On convolutional backbones (ResNet8, MobileNetV2), dynamic quantization quantizes only the final classification linear layer, leaving 2D convolutions in FP32. This produces a trivial $1.01\times$ runtime difference while introducing quantization noise.

\textbf{$D \cap A$: Data Floors and Model Capacity.}
When faced with reduced training budgets, ML practitioners frequently attempt to compensate by scaling model capacity. In graph node classification on \texttt{ogbn-arxiv}, we cross sample epoch budgets ($D \in \{125, 500\}$) with architectural capacity ($A \in \{64, 256\}$ hidden channels). At full data budget ($D=500$), scaling capacity from 256 down to 64 reduces training wall-clock time by $3.1\times$ ($1576.5\text{ s} \to 510.0\text{ s}$) while maintaining acceptable quality ($71.62\%$, meeting the $71.74\%$ floor within tolerance). However, when the data budget is reduced to $25\%$ ($D=125$), neither the full-capacity model ($64.20\%$) nor the reduced-capacity model ($61.80\%$) satisfies the admission threshold. The data floor binds universally. Algorithmic capacity adjustments cannot compensate for fundamental training information deficits.

\textbf{$D \cap M$: Memory Hierarchy and Speedup Invariance.}
Evaluating data scale against hardware execution backends determines whether accelerator speedups remain stable across dataset budgets. In GCN node classification, the graph structure (169,343 nodes, 1,166,243 edges, 128-dimensional features) establishes a fixed memory working set of approximately $90\text{ MB}$. This working set comfortably fits within unified system DRAM ($64\text{ GB}$) but far exceeds the CPU L2 cache ($8\text{ MB}$) and GPU on-chip registers. Consequently, every training epoch streams message-passing activations through the unified memory bus. Under settled timing controls with thermal stabilization, per-epoch execution time is strictly linear across epoch budgets ($125, 250, 500$), maintaining an invariant $2.38\times$ hardware speedup ratio. The speedup is governed by the ratio of GPU memory streaming bandwidth to CPU vector memory bandwidth, independent of epoch duration.

Within the passing data envelope ($D=500$), the algorithm and machine levers compose almost multiplicatively. The baseline configuration $(D=500, A=256, M=\text{CPU})$ requires $1576.5\text{ s}$ to achieve $72.10\%$ accuracy (\badgePass). The interaction ratio ($6.95 / 7.11 = 0.97$) sits entirely within the measured run-to-run noise floor.

However, when an unconstrained systems optimizer attempts to compose these levers with the reduced data budget ($D=125$), composability breaks down completely. The configuration $(D=125, A=64, M=\text{MPS})$ completes in just \textbf{$52.1\text{ s}$}, delivering an apparent \textbf{$30.26\times$ speedup} over the baseline. Under an un-gated benchmarking methodology, this configuration would be celebrated as a transformative efficiency breakthrough. Under MLPerf EDU's strict admission control, test accuracy collapses to $61.80\%$ ($9.94\%$ below the target floor), resulting in an immediate \textbf{Rejection (\badgeMiss)}.

The true joint optimum is $(D=500, A=64, M=\text{MPS})$ at $226.7\text{ s}$ ($6.95\times$ speedup, \badgePass). Decoupling efficiency optimization from strict quality admission produces degenerate system configurations. Cross-layer joint evaluation is an indispensable requirement for ML systems benchmarking.

\textbf{Methodological Noise Floor and Run-Order Controls.}
To ensure that intersectional claims reflect true systems effects rather than experimental noise, the harness establishes empirical variance baselines. On CPU, inference and training repeat with complete bit-level reproducibility ($\Delta = 0.00e+00$ on quality, $0.5\%$ CV on runtime). On MPS, GPU training exhibits stochastic atomic operations and floating-point reordering, yielding a quality standard deviation of $\sigma = 3.85\times 10^{-3}$ and a timing CV of $2.6\%$. 

Furthermore, sustained execution under un-settled thermal conditions revealed that CPU runtimes can degrade by up to $74\%$ due to thermal throttling, while GPU execution accelerates by up to $38\%$ as shader pipelines warm. This creates an apparent $2.81\times$ shift in reported speedups purely from run order. MLPerf EDU mitigates this by enforcing mandatory 120-second idle settle intervals and alternating device execution orders, guaranteeing that reported speedups reflect microarchitectural behavior rather than thermal drift.

\subsection{Principle Fulfillment Summary}
\label{sec:principle_fulfillment}

The empirical findings in \Cref{sec:characterization} and \Cref{sec:dam_eval} directly fulfill the five core educational and systems principles introduced in \Cref{sec:intro}:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1.5pt]
\item \textbf{P1 (Quality-Performance Coupling).} Exercised across the quantization ablations (\Cref{tab:quant_summary}) and multi-dimensional factorials (\Cref{tab:dam_factorial}). Runs that bought latency with aggressive quantization were rejected wherever the inherited floor was breached: INT8 dropped DistilBERT to $90.02\%$ against a $91.06\%$ floor, and BF16 dropped to $90.83\%$, invalidating the latency gains in each case. In contrast, ResNet8 held at $85.40\%$ against its $85.00\%$ floor and was admitted, confirming that the admission rule evaluates empirical quality rather than banning specific techniques.
\item \textbf{P2 (Reproducibility and Auditability).} Exercised via SHA-256 asset lineage and manifest validation (\texttt{.provd.json}). Every run records hardware state, seed configuration, and asset checksums, giving tamper-evident integrity for local validation. The manifests are not cryptographically signed, so they support independent reproduction and audit rather than authenticated attestation against an adversarial submitter.
\item \textbf{P3 (Microarchitectural Fidelity).} Verified by Roofline analysis (\Cref{fig:roofline}) and CPU vs. MPS hardware telemetry (\Cref{sec:single_lenses}, \Cref{tab:machine_exhaustive}). Single-laptop execution preserves genuine compute-bound and memory-bound bottlenecks, exposing a $100\times$ spread ($2.00\times$ on anomaly detection to $207.35\times$ on visual wake words) and architectural parity ($0.98\times$) on AST branching.
\item \textbf{P4 (Statistical Variance and Determinism).} Exercised by the seed sweeps in \Cref{sec:determinism} and Section 6. Scored inference reproduces to \DeterminismMaxSeedSpread{} across \DeterminismExecutions{} executions, while timing over the same runs still varies by \FiveRunMinCV{}--\FiveRunMaxCV{}. On the training side, seed choice alone flips the admission verdict in \TrainingSeedFlipping{} of \TrainingSeedWorkloads{} swept workloads, and MPS GPU training exhibits a standard deviation of $\sigma = 3.85\times 10^{-3}$, establishing why single-run training benchmarks cannot support valid admission verdicts.
\item \textbf{P5 (Multi-Dimensional Pareto Trade-Offs and Non-Decomposability).} Verified through the DAM Taxonomy intersections (\Cref{fig:data_lens,fig:algo_lens,fig:cpu_vs_mps,tab:dam_factorial}). The empirical results expose non-linear Pareto frontiers across sample budgets ($D$), precision representations ($A$), and hardware backends ($M$), proving that optimal system design requires joint multi-layer evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

While MLPerf EDU provides an accessible single-node environment for ML systems research and education, it operates within explicit architectural boundaries that define its scope.

\textbf{Single-node envelope limits.} The single-laptop execution constraint deliberately excludes multi-node cluster phenomena. Large-scale distributed training dynamics, including all-reduce gradient synchronization over high-speed fabrics, pipeline parallel bubble overheads, and high-radix interconnect topology scaling~\citep{jouppi2021}, require dedicated multi-GPU clusters and fall outside the single-laptop scope. Furthermore, while character-level nanoGPT models multi-head self-attention, KV-cache decoding, and cross-entropy optimization, it uses sub-billion parameter scale to fit within consumer RAM boundaries. Similarly, while MLPerf Tiny workloads expose depthwise convolutions and sub-megabyte memory limits on host CPUs, they execute within standard operating system runtimes rather than bare-metal microcontroller hardware.

\textbf{Evaluated scope and pedagogical validation.} This paper evaluates the technical design, microarchitectural fidelity, and empirical validity of the benchmark artifact itself. While the accompanying labs, staged execution profiles, and provenance reports provide structured curricular affordances, evaluating formal classroom learning gains (e.g. measuring student mastery of systems trade-offs over multi-week courses) requires multi-institution user studies across diverse cohorts.

\textbf{Single-host measurement.} Every number in this paper comes from the one reference host in \Cref{tab:host}. A second host architecture, specifically x86 with a discrete CUDA GPU, is the highest-value missing measurement, because it would separate results that reflect ML systems behavior generally from results that reflect the unified-memory architecture of this particular machine. The zero-speedup findings on sparse and branch-heavy workloads are the ones most likely to shift under a discrete-memory backend.

\textbf{Queued measurement work.} Three sweeps are specified in the registry but not yet executed under the admission rules: extending the training seed sweep from \TrainingSeedWorkloads{} workloads to every training workload at \DeterminismSeeds{} seeds, running the augmentation lever (\Cref{sec:dam_eval}) against the pruned sample budgets, and closing the \FunctionalSetupWorkloads{} measured-versus-published gaps in \Cref{sec:shortfalls}. Until those complete, the corresponding claims are scoped to what was measured.

\textbf{Governance and future roadmap.} The suite is developed as an independent project. We would propose it for open working group governance rather than assume it, and the naming would need to be resolved with MLCommons before any such step. Upcoming releases will explore synthetic workload generation~\citep{chung2023} to compress dataset transfer footprints while preserving microarchitectural stress characteristics, alongside expanding SUT plugin support for emerging hardware accelerators.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

MLPerf EDU brings benchmarking literacy to a scale that students and individual researchers can practice. By enforcing zero-discretion target inheritance and strict quality admission, the suite brings production-grade rigor to single-node ML systems evaluation. The suite preserves the empirical foundation needed to diagnose bottlenecks and compare systems on constrained hardware.

The full suite executes end to end in \ScoreReferenceTotalMinutes{}~minutes of reference compute on the single consumer laptop described in \Cref{tab:host}, which is the property that makes the discipline practicable in a course. The complete benchmark suite, open-source repository, interactive lab exercises, and reference documentation are integrated into the ML Systems textbook ecosystem at \url{https://mlsysbook.ai}. Execution bundles are available for local reviewer handoff, enabling cross-platform replication.
