\begin{abstract}
Professional machine-learning benchmarks teach a discipline that is often absent
from coursework. A valid result must bind a workload, data, model, quality gate,
system description, measurement protocol, and evidence package. Official MLPerf
suites establish this discipline at production scale~\citep{mattson2020,reddi2020mlperf,banbury2021mlperf},
but many representative paths are too costly for an individual student to
reproduce. We present \textbf{MLPerf EDU}, an independent, executable framework
that adapts benchmark methodology to laptop-class teaching and research. The
registry contains \RegistryRows{} workload rows across \RegistrySuites{} suites.
Five rows are score-bearing, three are performance-bearing, and the remaining
\SystemsOnlyRows{} are explicitly systems-only. The framework couples real-data
quality gates, checkpoint-backed inference, repeatable timing, dataset and model
dossiers, artifact manifests, and portable provenance packages. We report eight
committed five-run project references produced from one clean source revision.
All five training references meet their declared quality targets, and all three
inference references meet their functional and repeatability gates. These are
project reference measurements, not MLCommons-verified results. Cross-platform
replication, public package hosting, dataset-policy decisions, and external review
remain necessary before a public release.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Machine-learning systems now span training, serving, retrieval, recommendation,
vision, embedded inference, and agentic pipelines. Comparing those systems
requires more than timing a script. A benchmark must hold task semantics and
quality constant while it records the model, data, hardware, scenario, and
measurement procedure. MLPerf established this discipline for production
systems~\citep{mattson2020,reddi2020mlperf,banbury2021mlperf}. The same discipline
is valuable in education, yet production-scale workloads and submission
infrastructure often exceed the time, hardware, and operational budget of a
course or an individual artifact review.

The usual educational substitute is a collection of small models. Small models
solve the resource problem, but they do not automatically teach benchmarking.
A student can report a faster run after changing the data, silently weakening
quality, measuring warmup, or using a synthetic shortcut. None of those results
supports a fair systems comparison. The missing learning objective is
\emph{benchmarking literacy}, the ability to construct, inspect, reproduce, and
defend an evidence-backed performance claim.

MLPerf EDU addresses that gap with an executable framework rather than a static
scoreboard. It retains the original paper's central idea, an evaluation tuple
that makes the controlled variables explicit.

\begin{definition}[Evaluation Tuple]
A benchmark configuration contains the model, data, hardware, scenario,
constraints, and protocol. It produces a measurement vector containing latency,
throughput, quality, and energy. We write this relationship compactly as
$\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}$. A result is comparable only when its
evidence binds every component of $\mathcal{S}$.
\end{definition}

This formulation separates two questions that are often conflated. The first asks
whether a workload is useful for teaching a systems concept. The second asks
whether its evidence is strong enough to support a public comparison. MLPerf EDU
therefore assigns every registry row one of three result roles. Score-bearing rows
have real-data task-quality gates and five-seed reference protocols.
Performance-bearing rows have functional or parity gates, repeatable timing
protocols, and model lineage. Systems-only rows remain useful for instruction and
research but cannot support a public score.

The paper makes five contributions.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A pedagogical benchmark contract.} The framework turns the evaluation
tuple into executable registry, runner, grading, and reporting rules.
\item \textbf{A laptop-class workload suite.} Thirty rows cover ten domains while
preserving explicit distinctions among quick checks, comparable runs, and
research variants.
\item \textbf{Quality before performance.} Public candidates must pass declared
quality or functional gates before their timing can be interpreted.
\item \textbf{Auditable evidence.} Runs emit JSON, HTML, CSV, and provenance
manifests with source, asset, hardware, metric, and checkpoint metadata.
\item \textbf{Verified project references.} Eight current candidates have
five-run evidence generated from one clean source revision and checked through
the public command path.
\end{enumerate}

The goal is not to replace official MLPerf suites or imply MLCommons approval.
It is to make professional evaluation practice inspectable at a scale students
and researchers can run locally. \Cref{fig:architecture} summarizes the system.

\section{Design Principles}
\label{sec:design}
% ============================================================================

Three principles guide MLPerf EDU's design, each addressing a specific failure mode of existing pedagogical tools.

\subsection{Pedagogical Transparency Over Performance}

Production benchmarks optimize for throughput with custom CUDA kernels,
mixed-precision arithmetic, and operator fusion. Those optimizations can make
the computation invisible to students. MLPerf EDU inverts this priority for its
reference workloads. Local models use inspectable Python and PyTorch with
explicit operations and named dimensions, while the SLM suite uses permissive
off-the-shelf Hugging Face models to expose modern serving behavior. A student
reading the DS-CNN implementation sees depthwise and pointwise convolutions as
separate \texttt{nn.Conv2d} layers with \texttt{groups=in\_channels}, not a fused
kernel.

\subsection{Real Data With Provenance}

A benchmark without real data teaches stress testing, not systems evaluation. Students must observe how data distributions affect convergence. \Cref{tab:datasets} documents representative datasets, while the executable registry carries structured asset dossiers for each dataset and model. Data is either bundled for zero-network runs, fetched from public upstream sources, or marked with an explicit public-release warning when the release policy still needs owner or MLCommons review.

\subsection{Complete Train--Checkpoint--Inference Loop}

Many tools provide only training or only inference. MLPerf EDU provides the
complete pipeline. Training code produces checkpoints, checkpoints load into
inference harnesses, and harnesses measure latency under realistic scenarios.
The current registry records dataset, model, checkpoint, profile, quality, and
public-result metadata in one place, ensuring deterministic, reproducible
experiments.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\textbf{Registry-driven workload metadata.} Workload rows declare the user-facing selector, suite, profile coverage, assets, and quality policy.},label={lst:factory},float=t]

\section{Workload Suite}
\label{sec:suite}

The executable registry contains \RegistryRows{} rows across \RegistrySuites{}
suites. A row binds a canonical workload or variant to its assets, runner,
scenario, profile behavior, quality policy, and public role. This registry is the
source of truth. The paper imports its counts and evidence table from a generated
snapshot, which prevents prose from drifting away from executable metadata.

The three profiles describe execution intent. \texttt{min} is a quick
installation and plumbing check. It can use reduced or synthetic inputs and
never supports a public score. \texttt{max} is the standard comparable path with
the declared model, real data, gate, and protocol. \texttt{pro} is the research
envelope for ablations, backend studies, repetitions, and user-controlled
variants. A \texttt{pro} result is comparable only when its recorded
configuration defines the comparison.

\textbf{Selection principles.} A workload should satisfy resource accessibility,
behavioral fidelity, and pedagogical decomposability. Accessibility means that

implementation preserves the systems behavior it claims to teach, even though it
does not reproduce production scale. Decomposability means a student can vary one
factor and explain the resulting change. The framework does not currently claim a
measured roofline classification for every row. Unmeasured taxonomy fields remain
unmeasured rather than being filled with architectural guesses.

\subsection{Language, Recommendation, Graph, Time-Series, and RL Suites}

The language suite includes NanoGPT training, checkpoint-backed prefill and
decode, precision variants, speculative decoding, BERT training, LoRA
fine-tuning, and mixture-of-experts training. NanoGPT provides the clearest
end-to-end teaching chain. Training produces a quality-approved checkpoint.
Prefill and decode consume the same checkpoint and record its digest, making
model lineage part of the inference result rather than an informal assumption.

Micro-DLRM uses MovieLens-100K to expose sparse embeddings and dense feature
interaction~\citep{naumov2019deep}. Its score is ROC AUC, not raw accuracy, and
the current target is 0.76. The dataset remains fetch-only and restricted pending
a publication-policy decision. The implementation and local evidence are
complete, but technical success does not settle redistribution permission.

Graph, time-series, reinforcement-learning, and additional language rows broaden
the instructional surface. They are systems-only in the current public contract.
This label prevents their reduced tasks or exploratory targets from being
mistaken for validated public baselines.

\subsection{Vision and Tiny Suites}

ResNet-18 and MobileNetV2 train on Fashion-MNIST with top-1 targets of 85\% and
78\%, respectively~\citep{he2016,sandler2018}. Their paired architectures support
comparisons of residual blocks, depthwise separable convolution, parameter count,
and training throughput while keeping task quality explicit.

The anomaly-detection autoencoder trains on MNIST and uses anomaly AUROC rather
than reconstruction loss as its public task metric~\citep{lecun1998,koizumi2019}.
This distinction matters. A low reconstruction loss on normal examples does not
establish useful anomaly ranking. DS-CNN and visual wake-word rows remain
systems-only until their full public task and evidence contracts are strong
enough for promotion.

\subsection{Agent Suite}

The four agent rows isolate retrieval, code generation with verification,
reason-and-act control flow, and structured tool calling. They measure systems
behavior such as request latency, iterations, and tool-call validity. They are
currently systems-only. The small bundled fixtures are appropriate for
deterministic labs, but they do not justify public capability claims. This role
separation lets the suite teach agentic pipeline costs without overstating the
statistical meaning of a tiny task set.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

The current evidence campaign evaluates eight public candidates on an Apple M5
Max host. Each candidate uses five fresh-process runs from source commit
\texttt{\EvidenceSourceCommit}. Training rows use seeds 0 through 4 and report
the median task metric. Inference rows repeat the complete functional and timing
protocol five times. Every retained run passed execution, grading, provenance,
seed, and quality or functional checks.

The campaign uses a create-once policy. A failed attempt is retained and cannot
be repaired by replacing selected seeds. Five attempts were rejected because
their timing coefficient of variation exceeded the declared limit during host
contention. Their quality and functional gates passed, but they remain excluded
from the public-candidate aggregate. This separation is important because
accuracy does not excuse unstable timing.

\subsection{Inference Latency}

Inference evidence is eligible only after correctness and model identity are
established. NanoGPT prefill and decode load the same approved training
checkpoint and record its SHA-256 digest. Prefill uses \PrefillWarmups{} warmup
passes and \PrefillMeasurements{} measured passes over a fixed context. Decode
uses \DecodeWarmups{} warmup requests and \DecodeMeasurements{} measured
requests, each with \DecodeSteps{} decode steps. Reports include median, p90, and
p99 latency together with token throughput.

The SLM path pins the model revision and evaluates a bundled continuation suite.
A performance-bearing result must generate at least \SLMTokenFloor{} continuation
tokens and keep continuation perplexity at or below \SLMPerplexityLimit. Its
timing protocol uses \SLMWarmups{} warmups and \SLMMeasurements{} measurements.
These modest gates detect broken or materially changed inference paths. They are
not broad capability evaluations.

On the recorded CPU backends, NanoGPT prefill reaches a median
\NanoGPTPrefillMedianK{} thousand tokens/s, NanoGPT decode reaches
\NanoGPTDecodeMedian{} tokens/s, and SLM decode reaches \SLMDecodeMedian{}
tokens/s. The SLM range is \SLMDecodeMin--\SLMDecodeMax{} tokens/s. All three
references satisfy the declared five-percent cross-run coefficient-of-variation
gate. The value of these observations lies in their checkpoint, protocol,
quality, sample, and hardware context rather than in a claim of portable speed.

\section{Educational Integration}
\label{sec:education}

The benchmark contract is itself the learning object. Students do not merely
optimize a model. They learn to decide whether two results are comparable and to
defend that decision with artifacts. The intended progression has four stages.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Reproduce.} Run a fixed profile and verify its report and provenance
manifest.
\item \textbf{Diagnose.} Attribute time to data loading, model execution, or
request scheduling.
\item \textbf{Optimize.} Change one systems variable while preserving the declared
quality or parity gate.
\item \textbf{Defend.} Explain the comparison and identify the evidence supporting
each claim.
\end{enumerate}

Three executable labs turn that progression into concrete work. The first
examines optimization under a fixed training task. The second introduces an
inference system-under-test interface and compares a baseline path with a
KV-cache path while checking output parity. The third compares dense and sparse
architectures, including active parameter fraction and throughput. A separate
tutorial executes a first benchmark, locates its reports, and verifies its
provenance manifest.

\subsection{Example 1: Systems Optimization Challenge}

Students begin with a deliberately inefficient but correct configuration. They
profile the run, form a hypothesis, and change one variable such as batching,
data loading, precision, or caching. The submission is not the fastest observed
number alone. It includes the original and modified configurations, task-quality
outcomes, timing distributions, and a statement of comparability.

\subsection{Example 2: Cross-Architecture Comparison}

A dense model and a sparse mixture-of-experts model can have similar headline
parameter counts while activating different fractions of their parameters per
token. Students report both total and active parameters, throughput, and task
quality. The exercise makes clear why parameter count by itself is not a systems
cost model.

\subsection{Example 3: Anomaly Detection Threshold Tuning}

Students train the autoencoder on normal examples and evaluate ranking over normal
and anomalous examples. They tune a threshold for an operating point but grade
the public candidate with AUROC, which is threshold-independent. This exercise
connects model behavior, metric choice, and deployment policy.

Each lab has a deterministic smoke mode that executes real forward, backward,
inference, or parity checks without requiring a network connection. The smoke
paths establish software regression coverage, not classroom efficacy. This draft
does not claim measured learning outcomes. Course pilots, accessibility review,
and instructor studies remain future work.

\section{Architecture and Implementation}
\label{sec:architecture}

The implementation follows the original white-box design. A declarative registry
selects assets, runners, scenarios, profiles, gates, and public roles. A unified
command-line interface drives fetch, audit, run, grade, verify, report, package,
and validation operations. The flat \texttt{workloads.yaml} file is generated
from the native suite registry and serves as a compatibility mirror rather than a
second source of truth.

\subsection{Unified Dataset Factory}

Dataset recipes distinguish bundled, fetched, cached, synthetic, and restricted
assets. Reports record the resolved mode and relevant digests. A \texttt{min}
runner may use a reduced or synthetic input, but the public audit rejects that
mode for score-bearing evidence. Dataset dossiers also record citation, license,
release status, and expected cache behavior.

\subsection{Model Registry}

Model records bind a user-facing identifier to its implementation or pinned
upstream revision. Checkpoint-backed inference adds a lineage record containing
the source training workload, source task quality, and checkpoint digest. This
prevents a serving result from silently changing weights between repetitions.

\subsection{Load Generator and Inference Harness}

The Python-native load generator implements inspectable scheduling for
single-stream, offline, server, and multi-stream experiments~\citep{reddi2020mlperf}.
It records warmup and measurement counts, latency samples and percentiles,
throughput, and functional outcomes. It is designed for teaching and local
experimentation. It is not a drop-in replacement for official MLPerf LoadGen.

\subsection{Power Measurement}

The optional power path records an explicitly labeled aggregate estimate when
calibrated counters are unavailable. That estimate can support controlled
classroom comparisons, but it cannot support an absolute energy claim. Future
platform-counter or external-meter integrations should preserve the report
schema while identifying their measurement source and calibration.

\subsection{Submission Artifacts}

Each run produces a JSON report and human-readable HTML and CSV views. A
\texttt{.provd.json} manifest binds the report and declared sidecars by size and
SHA-256 digest. Hardware and software fingerprints, asset dossiers, quality
metadata, and checkpoint lineage travel with the result. Packages rewrite
machine-specific absolute paths so that verification works after extraction in a
clean directory.

Hashing establishes integrity, not authenticity. A self-consistent manifest can
still be fabricated by its creator. Independent reruns, signed attestations, or
controlled execution are needed for stronger trust. The framework deliberately
states this boundary instead of claiming that checksums prevent fraud.
The manifest therefore provides \mbox{unauthenticated integrity}, not proof of
origin or execution.

\subsection{CLI as Evaluation Tuple Instantiation}

The CLI makes the evaluation tuple concrete. Suite, workload, variant, and
profile select the model, data, scenario, constraints, and protocol. The commands
below cover the normal review path.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={CLI path from setup to verified evidence.},label={lst:cli}]
$ mlperf init --profile min
$ mlperf list --profile max
$ mlperf fetch --workload nanogpt-train \
    --profile max
$ mlperf run --workload nanogpt-train \
    --profile max
$ mlperf grade submissions/
$ mlperf verify submissions/run.provd.json
$ mlperf package submissions/run.provd.json
$ mlperf audit --policy public
\end{lstlisting}

The same metadata drives individual runs and release presets. This reduces the
chance that a paper-only configuration diverges from the executable path.

\section{Validation Methodology}
\label{sec:feedback}

Validation is layered because a single green status can hide different kinds of
failure. Unit and integration tests exercise runners, schemas, registry
invariants, grading, provenance, packaging, and generators. The
\texttt{smoke} preset runs twelve representative \texttt{min} workloads.
\texttt{coverage} runs all thirty \texttt{min} rows. \texttt{max} runs all thirty
comparable paths, and \texttt{release} runs both complete \texttt{min} and
\texttt{max} collections. Each phase verifies provenance and applies its declared
quality or functional gate.

The final local review revision passed 382 tests. The four named validation
presets passed 12/12, 30/30, 30/30, and 60/60 workloads, respectively, with no
run, grade, provenance, or review-contract failures. A clean Python 3.12 wheel
installed outside the checkout, passed its packaged audit, executed a workload,
and verified the resulting provenance. Thirty-five policy-permitted reference
packages reverified. Five MovieLens-derived packages were correctly blocked from
redistribution.

The strict public audit has no internal implementation blockers and retains nine
external-publication warnings. Eight require public URLs for the raw evidence
packages. One additionally records the unresolved MovieLens policy decision.
Hosted Linux CI and independent reproduction remain separate gates. Local success
does not imply that either has passed.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================================

\textbf{MLPerf.} The official MLPerf Training, Inference, and Tiny suites~\citep{mattson2020, reddi2020mlperf, banbury2021mlperf} set the industry standard. MLPerf EDU inherits their architectural diversity and measurement discipline while reducing the hardware requirement from enterprise clusters to a single laptop. \Cref{tab:comparison} summarizes the positioning.

\textbf{DAWNBench.} Introduced the time-to-accuracy and cost-to-accuracy metrics~\citep{coleman2019} that influenced MLPerf's quality-target design. DAWNBench focused on cloud cost optimization; MLPerf EDU focuses on pedagogical accessibility.

\textbf{TorchBench.} Covers 80+ PyTorch models for regression testing within the framework development pipeline~\citep{ansel2024torchbench}. Unlike MLPerf EDU, it is not designed for education and does not include datasets, quality targets, or a load generator.

\textbf{AIBench and BenchCouncil.} The AIBench suite~\citep{gao2021aibench} provides comprehensive AI workloads across domains including datacenter, edge, and IoT. While AIBench targets industrial deployment characterization, MLPerf EDU targets pedagogical understanding with white-box implementations.

\textbf{Educational ML frameworks.} TinyTorch teaches framework internals through bottom-up construction. Fast.ai and d2l.ai focus on algorithmic understanding. MLPerf EDU is complementary. It provides workloads that such frameworks benchmark, and its focus is on systems performance measurement rather than ML algorithms.

\textbf{Agent benchmarks.} SWE-bench~\citep{jimenez2024swebench} and AgentBench measure agent \emph{capability} (task completion rate). MLPerf EDU's agent workloads are orthogonal. They measure the \emph{systems efficiency} (latency, memory, power) of agentic pipelines, a distinction critical for production deployment.

\textbf{Non-ML benchmarks.} The SPEC CPU~\citep{henning2006} and TPC~\citep{poess2000} benchmark suites provide relevant methodological precedent for educational benchmarking. SPEC's emphasis on reproducible measurement conditions and TPC's multi-dimensional metrics (throughput, price-performance, energy) informed MLPerf EDU's evaluation tuple design. Our work transfers these established benchmarking principles to the ML systems domain.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

The framework reduces scale until a workload fits local hardware. That choice
preserves inspectability and access, but it does not guarantee that every
production bottleneck survives. Micro-DLRM preserves sparse lookup and dense
interaction structure without reproducing terabyte-scale embedding pressure.
Character-level NanoGPT preserves attention and autoregressive decode while
omitting production tokenization and data scale. Systems-only rows make similar
tradeoffs and should not be interpreted as scaled competitive benchmarks.

Only eight rows currently have evidence strong enough for the proposed public
roles. The other twenty-two remain systems-only even when their code executes and
their local metrics look plausible. This is a deliberate limitation. Promotion
requires a real task, justified gate, multi-run protocol, asset policy, and
portable evidence rather than confidence based on one successful run.

The current references come from one Apple M5 Max system. Training quality should
transfer within normal numerical variation, but runtime is platform-specific.
Cross-platform work should include Linux CPU and CUDA systems, clean-wheel
installation, repeated timing, and explicit treatment of numerical drift. DLRM's
narrow quality margin deserves particular attention.

MovieLens-100K remains fetch-only and approval-dependent for public benchmark
use. The project also needs an authoritative license for the complete published
artifact, public hosting for evidence packages, and external decisions about the
name and any relationship to MLCommons.

Power reporting is not yet suitable for absolute energy comparison. The default
estimate teaches report structure but requires calibrated counters or external
meters for defensible joule claims. Agent workloads likewise need larger,
independently governed task sets before they can move beyond systems-only use.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

\textbf{Internal validity.} Five fresh-process runs reduce sensitivity to one
seed or one timing sample, but they do not eliminate shared implementation error.
The provenance digest detects byte changes and path drift; it does not prove that
the computation occurred.

\textbf{External validity.} One laptop establishes local feasibility, not
performance portability. The evidence records hardware and software fingerprints
so later systems can test both quality transfer and timing variation.

\textbf{Construct validity.} The framework assumes that practicing controlled
comparisons on reduced workloads improves production benchmarking judgment. That
claim is plausible but unmeasured. Formal course studies and external artifact
reviews are needed to evaluate it.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

MLPerf EDU restores professional benchmark discipline to a scale that students
and individual researchers can inspect. Its central contribution is not a list of
small models. It is an executable contract that binds model, data, hardware,
scenario, quality, protocol, and evidence before a performance claim is accepted.

The present implementation contains \RegistryRows{} rows across
\RegistrySuites{} suites. Five are score-bearing, three are performance-bearing,
and \SystemsOnlyRows{} remain systems-only. Eight five-run project references
pass their current quality, functional, provenance, and repeatability gates.
Those results make the benchmark ready for external technical review, not for a
claim of official status. Public evidence hosting, cross-platform reproduction,
dataset policy, licensing, and MLCommons feedback remain visible next steps.

The educational value lies in the workflow students practice. They reproduce a
baseline, diagnose a bottleneck, change one variable, preserve quality, and defend
comparability with artifacts. That workflow can outlast any particular model or
hardware generation.

\subsection*{Reproducibility}

The repository contains the executable registry, reference runners, evidence
summaries, review packets, website, labs, tutorial, and paper generator. The
public entry point is \texttt{mlperf}. Runs produce JSON, HTML, CSV, and
\texttt{.provd.json} artifacts. The paper imports generated registry counts and
reference values so its quantitative claims remain synchronized with the
committed evidence. The larger raw packages are retained for
\mbox{local reviewer handoff} and do not yet have public URLs.
