# Sustainable AI: ML System Context Audit Report

## 1. Overall Assessment
**ML System Context Strength: HIGH**

The chapter generally excels at framing sustainability through the lens of Machine Learning Systems. It effectively grounds abstract sustainability concepts using concrete ML workloads (e.g., the energy equivalence of training GPT-3, H100 cooling constraints, Archetype A energy walls, and the stark energy gap between the compute-bound *prefill* phase and memory-bound *decode* phase). The mathematical grounding via "LEGO" cells and `MLSysIM` quantities ensures the arguments are highly rigorous.

However, a technical audit reveals a few sections where the text falls back on "generic data center" or "general semiconductor" descriptions. While factually accurate, these sections miss opportunities to explicitly explain *why* ML architectures uniquely exacerbate or interact with these physical constraints. Strengthening these areas will elevate the text from "sustainability in computing" to true "sustainability in ML Systems."

## 2. Identified Gaps & Specific Recommendations

### Area 1: Power Delivery and 48V DC Distribution
**Location:** `## Power delivery` (@sec-sustainable-ai-power-delivery)
**The Gap:** The chapter discusses power delivery inefficiencies and mentions that "Some ML facilities use 48 V DC distribution, which eliminates one conversion stage and improves efficiency by 2–3 percent." This frames the shift to 48V as a minor efficiency optimization (like it is in traditional cloud servers) rather than an electrical necessity for dense ML hardware.
**ML Tie-in Recommendation:**
*   **The Physics of $I^2R$ Losses:** Explicitly tie 48V distribution to the massive current demands of AI accelerators. An ML node with 8 GPUs drawing 700W each requires over 5,600W just for the accelerators. At traditional 12V delivery, this requires pushing nearly **470 Amps** across the baseboard. At that amperage, the $I^2R$ copper losses (heat generated by the busbars and cables themselves) become physically unmanageable.
*   **The Constraint:** Moving to 48V reduces the current by 4$\times$, which slashes $I^2R$ distribution losses by **16$\times$**. Frame 48V DC not as a "nice-to-have" 2% efficiency gain, but as a hard physical requirement for integrating tightly coupled ML baseboards (like NVIDIA's HGX or OCP's OAM) without melting the power connectors.

### Area 2: Waste Heat Recovery
**Location:** `### Cooling` (@sec-sustainable-ai-cooling)
**The Gap:** The text discusses waste heat reuse, noting that data centers can supply hot water to district heating systems or greenhouses. This is a standard cloud data center talking point.
**ML Tie-in Recommendation:**
*   **High-Grade vs. Low-Grade Heat:** Traditional air-cooled CPU data centers produce "low-grade" heat (warm air at ~35°C), which is difficult to reuse economically without energy-intensive heat pumps.
*   **The ML Advantage:** ML clusters are uniquely suited for waste heat recovery. Because GPUs can operate safely at higher junction temperatures and are cooled via direct-to-chip liquid cold plates, the return liquid loop from an ML Coolant Distribution Unit (CDU) provides "high-grade" heat (often hot water at 50–65°C). Make the point that the extreme thermal density of ML racks actually makes them thermodynamically superior candidates for municipal district heating compared to traditional IT infrastructure.

### Area 3: Semiconductor Manufacturing (Scope 3)
**Location:** `### Water, chemicals, and critical materials` (@sec-sustainable-ai-water-usage-caae)
**The Gap:** The chapter effectively highlights the massive water consumption of TSMC's Arizona fab (34M liters/day) and the energy cost of EUV lithography. However, it applies these metrics to semiconductors generally rather than isolating why AI chips carry a higher embodied footprint.
**ML Tie-in Recommendation:**
*   **Reticle-Limit Dies:** Explain that AI accelerators are typically "reticle-limit" dies (the maximum physical size the lithography machine can print). Larger dies mean fewer chips per wafer and higher vulnerability to defects, meaning more silicon, water, and chemical processing is effectively wasted per viable chip.
*   **Advanced Packaging (CoWoS):** High-performance ML requires immense memory bandwidth, necessitating 2.5D/3D packaging (like TSMC's CoWoS) to integrate High Bandwidth Memory (HBM). This introduces entirely new manufacturing stages, additional chemical washing, and baking steps that standard monolithic CPUs do not require, heavily amplifying the embodied carbon and water cost of ML silicon.

### Area 4: Data Center E-Waste & Obsolescence
**Location:** `### Disposal, e-waste, and embedded AI` (@sec-sustainable-ai-disposal-phase-b4f1)
**The Gap:** The section mentions the relentless upgrade cycle (3-5 years) for training servers but then quickly pivots to discussing the e-waste of IoT and embedded AI devices. It leaves the specific mechanics of data center ML obsolescence unexplored.
**ML Tie-in Recommendation:**
*   **Interconnect-Driven Obsolescence:** ML accelerators rarely reach the end of their physical silicon lifespan. They become obsolete due to memory bandwidth and interconnect bottlenecks (e.g., the jump from PCIe Gen4 to Gen5, or NVLink bandwidth limits).
*   **Proprietary Baseboard Waste:** Unlike traditional CPUs that socket into standardized motherboards, modern GPUs are often tightly coupled onto proprietary baseboards (e.g., HGX). Upgrading the fleet to support a new LLM architecture often means discarding the entire baseboard, the custom networking HCAs, and sometimes the host server, rather than simply swapping a PCIe card. This architectural tight-coupling multiplies the e-waste generated per upgrade cycle.

### Area 5: The "Duck Curve" and ML Load Shifting
**Location:** `### Use phase` (@sec-sustainable-ai-use-phase-a0a1)
**The Gap:** The text states that training a 175B model represents an "inflexible power draw that runs 24/7, making it difficult to shift workloads to times of higher renewable energy availability."
**ML Tie-in Recommendation:**
*   **Checkpointing as a Scheduling Lever:** While ML training *typically* runs 24/7, it is actually highly flexible compared to synchronous web-serving. Because distributed training already requires robust checkpointing (to survive hardware failures), a training job is essentially a massive batch process that can be paused and resumed. Schedulers can exploit this: a cluster can checkpoint its state and pause during the 4-hour evening grid peak (when fossil peaker plants spin up), and resume training when wind generation spikes at night. Tying "carbon-aware scheduling" explicitly to the ML mechanism of *checkpointing* grounds the policy firmly in systems architecture.
